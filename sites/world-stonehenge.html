Stonehenge and Its Landscape

On Salisbury Plain, Stonehenge stands among burial mounds and the remains of paths, enclosures and timber circles. Farming communities made and revisited these places over many generations. Archaeological surveys reveal their relationships in the landscape, while geological studies trace some of the stones to distant sources.

The Neolithic and Early Bronze Age around Stonehenge in Wiltshire, including earlier earthworks, several phases at the stone circle and evidence for three distinct groups of stone. Avebury is the separate northern component of the World Heritage property.

An earthwork before the stone circle

The Greater Cursus to the north was already ancient when the major stone settings went up. Its bank and ditch enclose a long strip of chalk downland. Early Neolithic communities also built long barrows in the area. These works leave clues to gathering and burial across Salisbury Plain well before the most familiar stages of Stonehenge. [4] [1]

Around 3000 BCE people dug a circular ditch and formed banks. A ring of pits known as the Aubrey Holes belongs to the early history of the site, and cremated human remains have been found in and around the earthwork. Around 2500 BCE builders raised the large sarsens, constructing a circle with lintels and a horseshoe of taller paired uprights. Subsequent work rearranged smaller stones within the enclosure. Houses, timber rings and a route to the river

At Durrington Walls, floors and postholes mark houses, while pottery and animal bones record food preparation and large gatherings. Some houses were occupied around the time of the major work at Stonehenge. English Heritage presents a connection with the builders as an archaeological interpretation. Nearby Woodhenge shows another form of monument, recognizable through the arrangement of postholes left by its timbers. [6] [8] [9]

The Avenue runs from the circle toward the River Avon. Near Stonehenge its alignment corresponds to midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset along the monument’s principal axis. The link to the river and the solar alignment are visible relationships. The ceremonies that may have used the route and the meanings participants attached to them remain matters of interpretation. [3] [13] [5]

What the stones say about their sources

Chemical analyses show that 50 of the 52 sampled surviving sarsens belong to a closely similar group. A core from one of the uprights matches stone from West Woods in the Marlborough Downs, making that area a likely source for most of the large sarsens. Geological comparisons trace several of the smaller bluestones, including types of dolerite and rhyolite, to the Preseli area of west Wales. [14] [15]

The flat sandstone known as the Altar Stone has a different geological history. In 2024, researchers compared the ages and chemistry of mineral grains and matched it to rocks of the Orcadian Basin in northeastern Scotland. A 2026 follow-up found the closest age matches in Caithness sandstone and assessed possible glacial dispersal, alongside the need for substantial human transport. The precise source outcrop and itinerary remain unresolved. [16] [17]

A landscape revisited across generations

Round barrows at New King Barrows and elsewhere on the downs brought new burials into view of older monuments. The surrounding area spans thousands of years of construction, burial and alteration. The local terrain map places individual sites in relation to the Avon and the chalk ridges. The linked historic landscape maps show the scale of the earthworks and why Avebury is treated as a separate component of the same World Heritage property. [1] [11] [5] [18] [19]
