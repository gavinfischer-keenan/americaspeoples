Conflict & Resistance in Australia: From Pre-Colonial Evidence to the Frontier Wars

Archaeology and historical forensics reveal both deeply rooted pre-colonial conflict regulation (and lethal weapon trauma like Kaakutja on the Baaka–Darling River) and a century-long guerrilla defense across colonial pastoral and policing frontiers.

Material traces of pre-colonial conflict & asymmetrical frontier war

Despite older generalizations about the 'archaeological invisibility' of Australian warfare, close osteology (e.g., Kaakutja on the Baaka–Darling and Roonka on the Murray) alongside shield and spear depictions confirm regulated pre-colonial violence. [1] [8] [17]

Between 1788 and 1900, British settlers, militias, and Native Mounted Police detachments faced agile Indigenous guerrilla resistance—from Hawkesbury sandstone ridges and Tasmania's Midlands to Kooroongoora Warrior Lodges and Kimberley limestone caves—reconstructed via ballistic artifact surveys and conflict rock art. [18] [19] [15] [12]

Regional resistance from Hawkesbury & Pinjarra to Windjana Gorge

Organized Indigenous tactics evolved rapidly across terrains—ranging from Darug corn-raid guerrilla strategy along the Hawkesbury (1794–1816) and Kalkadoon defense in NW Queensland crags (1884) to Bunuba leader Jandamarra's Kimberley gorge campaign (1894–1897). [4] [11] [14]

Archival silence, Native Mounted Police & landscape forensics

Systematic archaeological surveys of Queensland Native Mounted Police detachments, cartridge spreads, and oral histories across Overland Corner and Dead Man's Flat recover conflict events systematically omitted from colonial ledgers. [13] [16] [9]
