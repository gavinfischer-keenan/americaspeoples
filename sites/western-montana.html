Old sites of the mountain valleys

The Bitterroot and Clark Fork valleys made corridors through the mountains. Early camps, such as the MacHaffie site in the foothills near the eastern margin of this study area, preserve stone toolmaking and other activity from long before the routes recorded in colonial accounts. The material is fragmentary. Distinct archaeological dates show visits or deposits at specific places, while the extent or duration of local use remains an open question. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Trade and federal pressure

Salish, Pend d’Oreille and Kootenai leaders met United States officials near Missoula in 1855. The treaty shaped which communities were pressured to relocate and what land they held. In the Bitterroot Valley, Fort Owen grew from a Jesuit mission property into a trading place. In the Big Hole valley, the Army attacked a Nimiipuu camp in 1877 during the flight from forced relocation. It connects the Montana chapter to Idaho’s council and fighting sites across the Bitterroot Mountains. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Painted rock in the Big Belts

The Big Belt sites are outside the Bitterroot Valley but within this western Montana study’s broad geographic frame. Researchers measured pigment from several rock paintings and reported a calibrated age envelope. The dated images give one intermediate chapter between much earlier archaeological evidence and the nineteenth-century records. Their regional pin represents the landscape around the named sites with coarse precision. Source 1 ↗

Connections across the Bitterroot

Fort Connah adds a different part of the nineteenth-century landscape. The Hudson’s Bay Company traded near Post Creek in the same decade that the new international boundary altered the political position of its southern posts. Ten years later tribal leaders encountered a different negotiating partner at the Hellgate council. The two places record distinct changes in trade and government. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
