Calanais and the stone settings of Loch Roag

The long avenue on the west coast of Lewis leads to a stone circle, a taller central monolith and a small burial cairn. Other rings and stone settings occupy neighboring ridges and the shores of Loch Roag. Peat and changes in sea level have transformed the land between them, altering what people can see of the prehistoric landscape.

The guide covers the architecture and use of Calanais I, nearby settings, and the effects of farming, peat accumulation and coastal change on the archaeological record around Loch Roag.

A standing circle acquired a burial chamber

At Calanais I, the avenue approaches a ring on a low rise. A larger monolith commands its interior, with short rows branching east, west and south. Excavations showed that the small chambered cairn was introduced after the circle had been erected. The surviving stone plan therefore contains work from separate moments. Archaeologists have also examined the adjacent rock outcrop as part of the setting, though its role remains an interpretation. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Different kinds of setting around Loch Roag

Calanais II and III are close to the main ring but have different plans. The second site has a cairn and evidence of a timber setting. At the third, an inner setting lies within a broader ring. Calanais IV encloses another small cairn. On Great Bernera, the stones at Cleitir form an arc on a slope facing water. Several more sites survive as fallen stones or partly buried features. The distribution shows repeated choices to build in this landscape, with much less certainty about how closely in time their builders worked. [7] [8] [9] [10] [5]

People worked the ground before and after the stones

Cultivation at the main site places the monument in land already used by farming communities. The relationship is more secure than the location of their homes. Historic Environment Scotland notes that no contemporary settlement has been recognized beside the main ring. Later shieling huts at Beinn Bheag and the remains of a house and drying kiln near Calanais I demonstrate further uses of the ground. These later buildings cannot fill the gap in Neolithic settlement evidence. [1] [11] [4]

A coast that changed and a landscape hidden by peat

Researchers have combined field survey, coring and geophysical work on land and in the sea loch. Offshore sediments and buried surfaces point to changes in shoreline and vegetation. At Site XI, anomalies and surveyed remains strengthen the case that a single upright was one element of a larger stone setting. A network of sites, outlooks and potentially navigable approaches helps explain why the loch matters to the monuments. Reconstructed shorelines and routes remain estimates. [12] [13] [14]

From alignment to interpretation

The bearings of the rows and the position of the local horizon can be measured. Researchers have compared them to where the Moon or Sun would have appeared at particular times, as well as to nearby hills and outcrops. Such studies help describe the experiences a setting could have offered. Establishing which views mattered to the builders, and which were intended when stones were first placed, remains more difficult. [3] [4]
