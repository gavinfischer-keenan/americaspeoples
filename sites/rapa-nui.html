Coastal places and stone work

Rapa Nui’s monuments occupy a landscape of beaches, gardens, water sources and stone working. At Anakena, careful redating distinguishes signs of early activity in the dunes from separate construction phases of Ahu Nau Nau. At Rano Raraku, the soft volcanic material that gave form to statues also produced unusually fertile soils when worked. Plant remains suggest that people cultivated the inner slopes alongside quarrying. The dates from one patch of quarry soil describe its use, with other parts of the island following their own histories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Ceremony and political change

On the rim of Rano Kau, ‘Orongo’s stone buildings and rock carvings mark a later concentration of ceremonial activity. The nineteenth century brought foreign slave raiding and new demands over land and sovereignty. A bilingual document records the agreement negotiated by Rapanui leaders and Chilean representatives in 1888. That document’s versions require careful reading alongside later acts of confinement, including the islanders’ experience in Haŋa Roa already represented on the atlas. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Cultivation at different scales

Date-bearing charcoal and rat bones can bracket stages of platform use at Anakena. They cannot establish the date of every stone or of first landfall. At Rano Raraku the evidence concerns both deposited soil and the plants found in it. Comparing the two sites makes it possible to ask how the use of beach settlements, ceremonial platforms and inland slopes changed over generations. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

What survived in the archives

The bilingual proclamation at the end of this sequence survives in an archive assembled under later governments. Its precise date marks the record of an agreement. The parties’ understandings, and the consequences of later administration for islanders confined around Haŋa Roa, require separate evidence. The local archaeology supplies an independent history of social and economic change before those external records. Source 1 ↗

Rapa Nui — sustained habitation into the historical period

The 2024 ancient-genome study places initial settlement around 1250 CE and infers sustained Rapanui demographic presence after settlement into the nineteenth century. Its analyses do not support a severe seventeenth-century population collapse.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1250 CE to 1860 CE
Dating method: Primary genomic study's reconstructed demographic history, explicitly interpreted as island-level continued presence.

This is an island-level inference from archaeology, historical context and demographic modeling, not proof of uninterrupted occupation at each site. The 1860 boundary limits this particular modeled interval before the slave-raiding crisis; it is not extinction, abandonment or an end to Rapanui presence. No census size is inferred from genetic effective population size.

Haŋa Roa — confinement and Angata’s resistance

The 1914 record places Rapanui residents under confinement around Haŋa Roa within a sheep-company regime. María Angata Veri Tahi led resistance that year; naval intervention followed. People continued to act within and against colonial restrictions.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1914
Dating method: Documented event or explicitly identified source window.

1914 is a documented episode and observation, not the start or end of all confinement. The town anchor does not outline the wall, locate every confrontation or establish a casualty count.
