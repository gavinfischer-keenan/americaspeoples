Costa Rica and Panama · PLACE / COMPLEX

Indigenous communities of Costa Rica and Panama

At Guayabo, stone channels carry water through a settlement built into the slopes of Volcán Turrialba. On a beach ridge in Bocas del Toro, fish bones, grinding stones and pottery tell another history of households drawing on a shoreline and trading with people inland. These places belonged to a region of distinct Indigenous communities connected by rivers, trails and coastal travel. Their histories are visible at different scales, from the traces of a meal to the design of a public plaza. The time slider puts this evidence in sequence. A dated grave shows a particular use of a place. Pottery styles can identify an earlier horizon of activity, and several dated deposits together can outline a period of building or settlement. People left all three kinds of evidence in Costa Rica and Panama. Sometimes they buried the dead in places where people had once lived.

Early activity on a shifting coast and inland foothills

Cerro Mangote is now well inland from the active shore of Parita Bay. When people were gathering shellfish and grinding plants at the low hill beside the Santa María River, the shore lay much closer. Shell accumulated alongside stone cobbles worn along their edges. The site also served as a burial ground in later periods. One adolescent’s remains date to 2476–2293 BCE, near the beginning of the already mapped Monagrillo pottery tradition. The burial and the accumulation of shell are evidence of different episodes in a long local history. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

On the Caribbean side of Costa Rica, excavators at Las Mercedes-1 found early pottery deep beneath later deposits in the eastern part of the site. The sherds resemble pottery from the La Montaña complex, defined partly through research in the Turrialba valley. This comparison places the finds somewhere in a broad horizon ending about 300 BCE, with possible beginnings between 1500 and 800 BCE. The ceramics matter because they show activity long before the well-known stone buildings rose. Source 1 ↗

A river village, a highland house and kitchens

People at Sitio Sierra lived beside the Santa María River, upriver from the Pacific coast. A refuse deposit dated about 300–550 CE contained fish from the coast and estuary as well as freshwater fish. Nearby remains of deer and birds broaden the picture. Two cemeteries also preserve evidence from different generations. Comparing bone chemistry from the earlier and later groups suggests a changing balance of maize and other foods. The smaller samples behind these comparisons represent a few people and one part of the settlement’s many centuries. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

In the Chiriquí highlands at Barriles Bajo, the outlines of a possible oval timber house remained as stains in the soil. Charcoal from one posthole dates to 541–680 CE. At Lomas Entierros, on a hill above Costa Rica’s lower Tárcoles River, traces are smaller still. Heated starch grains and burned shell point to cooking in Structure 13. Maize starch and the pattern of activity in Structure 44 suggest possible food storage. Imported pottery there links local domestic spaces to connections with Greater Nicoya. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

How people honored the dead in Gran Coclé

At Sitio Conte and El Caño, within a few kilometres of each other on the Río Grande floodplain, some funerals brought together many people and large quantities of pottery and ornament. At Sitio Conte, the people who prepared Burial 11 surrounded twenty-three individuals with vessels. One person received much of the gold. At El Caño, the excavation of Tomb 7 documented successive acts of burial and offering. An infant’s ornaments prompted the excavators to consider whether some distinctions were passed down through families. The findings show unequal treatment of the dead. Assigning the mourners’ intentions and the causes of death requires additional evidence. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Along the La Villa River at Cerro Juan Díaz, a different set of burials provides evidence for the lives of people in the same broad archaeological region. Isotope measurements on bones and teeth suggest that diets and movements became more varied by about 700 CE. Much later, people buried children and adults across an earlier floor. In the excavated group, ornaments or other offerings were recorded with children alone. Similar attention to children in older graves gives the practice a local history across several centuries. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Streets, terraces and the movement of water

The stonework at Guayabo and Las Mercedes-1 shows how builders reshaped slopes and directed movement. At Guayabo, crews fitted stone retaining walls to changes in level, laid paved approaches and controlled water crossing the central space. Excavations place the most intense construction from about 850 to 1150 CE. Work continued in some parts of the settlement after that. The place was made through several stages of landscape alteration and building. Source 1 ↗

At Las Mercedes-1, charcoal beneath four foundations places major construction between 1000 and 1200 CE. Paths approached a plaza and raised platforms, and one wide ramp led toward a circular foundation. The site brings the scale of collective building into view. Later graves in some of the central spaces show that the community changed the uses of its built environment long after the main phase of construction. Source 1 ↗

Four sites in the Diquís Delta

The stone spheres make sense in relation to the places where people put them. At Finca 6, two spheres flanked the ramp to Mound 1, while groups elsewhere in the settlement formed lines in open spaces. A separate sample dates the ramp of Mound 2 to 1170–1280 CE. At Batambal, stone outlines and spheres occupied a hill visible across the delta. At Grijalba-2, builders faced artificial mounds with limestone and paved approaches with river cobbles. These differences help explain why excavators study the four settlements individually. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At El Silencio, the largest recorded Diquís sphere stands near a paved strip at the edge of a river terrace. Its granodiorite mass represents selection, shaping and placement on a striking scale. The dated sample from El Silencio belongs to earlier activity on the terrace, between 550 and 650 CE. Later pottery and the stonework give the place a second chapter whose exact construction dates remain open. Source 1 ↗

Connections across watersheds

Sitio Drago brings a Caribbean island into a history often told from Pacific river valleys. The residents of the village on Isla Colón drew fish from reefs, mangroves and waters farther offshore. Pottery recovered there includes material linked to central Panama, the Chiriquí–Diquís region and northern Costa Rica. Movement across water and between watersheds could involve several communities passing things along. Individual trips and exact paths remain hard to reconstruct. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The Spanish foundation at Panamá Viejo in 1519 introduces a new documentary record at one harbor. The archaeological sequence across the region had already recorded centuries of building, exchange and changes in the use of older places. Their histories continue at different places and scales into the era of Spanish colonization. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

Cerro Mangote — a burial in an older shell midden

On a low hill beside the Santa María River, people buried an adolescent in a place with a much older history of shell gathering and food preparation. The interment dates to 2476–2293 BCE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Burial CO-40 Ent. 6A: 2476–2293 cal BCE, published IntCal20 2σ
Dating method: Published calibrated BCE limits converted to astronomical years.

The calendar range dates one individual. The midden formed through multiple episodes, and shell deposits and later burials can occupy the same ground.

Las Mercedes-1 — early pottery beneath the later center

People left pottery on the Caribbean foothill plain centuries before the most prominent stone buildings rose at Las Mercedes-1. Excavators identified early sherds deep in the eastern part of the site and recognized affinities with the La Montaña ceramic complex.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Early ceramics attributed to approximately 1500/800–300 BCE
Dating method: Typological assignment to La Montaña-related early pottery. The earliest published possible boundary is used for the display envelope.

The early range rests on ceramic comparisons. The excavators allow considerable uncertainty in its beginning, and no excavated buildings are securely dated to this horizon.

Sitio Sierra — food remains from a river village

Discarded fish bones at Sitio Sierra bring everyday meals into view. The village lay by the Santa María River, where it drew on inland resources as well as fish from the Pacific estuary and coast.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Refuse deposit dated 300–550 CE in Cooke’s site summary
Dating method: Published archaeological context range for the sampled refuse deposit.

The share of marine fish comes from a single screened refuse sample. It represents the sampled deposit and its recovery method.

Barriles Bajo — a possible house in the Barú foothills

At Barriles Bajo, a pattern of postholes suggests that a timber building once stood where only traces in the soil remain. The date from one posthole places that part of the settlement’s history in 541–680 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Erl-5211, context C36: 1424 ± 45 radiocarbon BP, 541–680 cal CE at 95.4%
Dating method: Published calibration of charred wood in a posthole, with IntCal98 retained as reported.

The authors interpret the rows of posts as a possible oval house. One charcoal sample directly anchors one posthole, while its association with the layout is archaeological.

El Silencio — an early deposit on the Térraba terrace

An early deposit at El Silencio places human activity on a Térraba River terrace in 550–650 CE. The terrace eventually also held paving and a stone sphere about 2.57 metres across.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Beta-335658: 1450 ± 30 radiocarbon BP; 550–650 cal CE (2σ)
Dating method: Published calibrated sample from a stratigraphic profile.

The only radiocarbon determination in the nomination is from a profile linked to the earlier Aguas Buenas occupation. The sphere and adjacent paving lack an independently established construction date.

Lomas Entierros — cooking and storage above the Tárcoles

At Lomas Entierros, residues and microscopic scraps show how two households or work spaces used different rooms. Traces in Structure 13 point to indoor cooking, while Structure 44 may have held stores as well as some food preparation.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Cobblestone-structure phase: 646–1185 cal CE
Dating method: Published calibrated architectural envelope for the site’s principal construction phase.

The structure interpretations come from samples in two excavated areas. Low concentrations may reflect how a structure was used, how long it was used or how well residues survived.

Sitio Conte — Burial 11 on the Río Grande de Coclé

At Sitio Conte, the people who prepared Burial 11 placed vessels around twenty-three individuals. One person received an exceptional share of gold ornaments, giving archaeologists evidence of conspicuous differences in how the dead were honored.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Burial No. 11: approximately 700–900 CE in the Penn Museum excavation archive
Dating method: Museum archaeological attribution of Burial 11, retained as published. The 450–900 CE cemetery-wide estimate is separate.

The museum’s broad age for this grave reflects archaeological chronology. Burial furnishings may signal rank or ritual distinctions. The identities, causes of death and positions of the other people in life remain uncertain.

El Caño — multiple burial and offerings in Tomb 7

At El Caño, excavators found forty-three people in Tomb 7, together with offerings added as part of a sequence of funerary acts. The exceptional adornment given to one infant led the team to ask whether some social distinctions were inherited.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: MU062: 770–905 cal CE (1180–1045 cal BP) or 920–965 cal CE (1030–985 cal BP)
Dating method: Published calibrated ranges are displayed separately; the outer bounds organize the record without filling the intervening gap.

The dated charcoal came from an offering area and has two reported calibration windows. The identities and circumstances of death of individual people require separate evidence.

Batambal — stone structures on a visible hilltop

Batambal’s builders placed stone features on a hill overlooking the lower Térraba River. Excavations exposed low rectangular outlines, while four spheres were recorded around the site.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Rectangular structures assigned to the Chiriquí period, 800–1500 CE
Dating method: Architectural comparison explicitly used in the National Museum excavation dossier. Earlier site assays in different deposits provide separate context.

The structures are dated by comparison. Published assays of 610–670 and 660–780 CE come from earlier stratified deposits. Excavations left the functions of several stone outlines unresolved.

Grijalba-2 — limestone mounds above the Balsar

On a terrace of the Balsar River, builders at Grijalba-2 faced low artificial mounds with limestone and laid cobblestone approaches. A stone sphere stood beyond the main cluster of buildings.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximate late Diquís, Chiriquí-period horizon, 800–1500 CE
Dating method: National Museum comparative assignment to the late Diquís settlement tradition and the World Heritage component context.

The published nomination reports no radiocarbon determinations for Grijalba-2. Architectural comparison supplies the broad period, and political subordination is an interpretation of regional settlement pattern.

Guayabo de Turrialba — roads, platforms and waterworks

Guayabo’s builders combined stone-paved approaches, retaining walls and channels in a settlement on the flank of Volcán Turrialba. Building in the central complex reached its greatest documented intensity from about 850 to 1150 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Most intense phase of monumental construction, 850–1150 CE
Dating method: Published excavation synthesis based on multiple calibrated construction-associated samples and stratigraphic interfaces.

Radiocarbon dates come from particular organic deposits associated with construction. Separate features have earlier or later ranges, and the buildings preserve multiple stages.

Sitio Drago — homes and exchange on Isla Colón

On the northwestern shore of Isla Colón, Sitio Drago’s inhabitants built a village along a raised beach ridge. They used reef, mangrove and forest resources and acquired pottery whose styles link them with central Panama and several Costa Rican regions.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Occupation indicated by fifteen radiometric determinations, approximately 690–1410 CE
Dating method: Published occupation envelope from the 2012 excavation and survey synthesis.

The 690–1410 CE envelope is an approximation derived from several sampled contexts. Ceramic similarities can reflect movement of objects or ideas through intermediaries, with the individual journeys unresolved.

Sitio Sierra — a later cemetery beside the village

Several centuries after the early cemetery at Sitio Sierra, people interred their dead in a separate later burial area. Bone and teeth preserve a record of meals across generations at the Santa María River village.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Later burial group, approximately 950–1150 CE. Directly dated AG3 A-4: 978–1151 cal CE (IntCal20, 2σ)
Dating method: Published contextual cemetery range corroborated by a directly dated burial.

The isotope sample is small and the values have overlapping dietary interpretations. The age spans refer to the excavated cemetery groups.

Las Mercedes-1 — a plaza linked by paved roads

At Las Mercedes-1, paved roads approached a civic center of raised platforms, a plaza and waterworks. Evidence from four building foundations places a major phase of construction between 1000 and 1200 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: La Cabaña A infrastructure, 1000–1200 CE
Dating method: Author’s period synthesis using radiocarbon determinations of stratigraphically associated charcoal from four building foundations.

The published reconstruction gives the calendar range for the main building campaign. The exact timing of the long causeways and the roles of individual structures remain under study.

Finca 6 — entrances to elevated buildings

At Finca 6, broad ramps led to circular earthen platforms faced with stone. A sample from the ramp of Mound 2 places one part of this architecture in 1170–1280 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Mound 2 ramp, Beta-270394: 810 ± 40 radiocarbon BP; 1170–1280 cal CE (2σ)
Dating method: Published calibrated determination associated with the excavated Mound 2 ramp.

The date attaches to Mound 2’s ramp sample. The two flanking spheres were documented at Mound 1, and separate alignments elsewhere in the site lack a direct date.

Cerro Juan Díaz — burials over an earlier floor

At Cerro Juan Díaz, people placed graves across a clay floor and cut through it for at least one burial. Most of the people in this excavated area were laid on their backs, and every grave with a recorded offering belonged to a child.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Operation 5 tooth collagen, four IntCal20 2σ ranges: 1053–1285, 1178–1276, 1219–1280 and 1263–1395 cal CE
Dating method: Outer bounds of four published direct AMS tooth collagen determinations, Beta-160227, Beta-419309, Beta-419308 and Beta-160228.

The age envelope covers four directly dated people. Dietary marine carbon may shift some ages later, and the floor’s original function and the age of undated interments remain open.

Cerro Juan Díaz — increasing isotope diversity

Over the long history of Cerro Juan Díaz, chemical signatures in bones and teeth record changing foods and movement. Around 700 CE, the diets and mobility histories of the people sampled began to vary more widely.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Increase in dietary and mobility variation by about AD 700.
Dating method: Published date convention retained; no new radiocarbon calibration. BCE uses astronomical year numbering (1 BCE = 0); calendar BP is referenced to 1950.

The results are from 49 sampled individuals. Isotopes reflect combinations of foods and local geology, which limit assignments of individual origins and ranks.

Panamá Viejo

Panama City's Spanish foundation dates to 1519. The old urban center was destroyed in the 1671 attack and was not rebuilt as the colonial city.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1519 CE
Dating method: Sourced foundation or arrival chronology; negative historical years converted to astronomical year numbering.

Approximate editorial location anchor, not a surveyed historic footprint. An ended city-site marker does not mean regional human absence. The municipality continued at its relocated site.

Monagrillo / He5 — coastal settlement evidence

The Monagrillo type site occupied the active shore of Parita Bay. Shell evidence suggests much use occurred in the dry season.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 2800–1400 cal BCE
Dating method: Published calibrated BCE bounds converted to astronomical year (1 BCE = 0).

The site range is 2800–1400 calibrated BCE, later than the earliest regional ware. Seasonal use is not an uninterrupted village census.
