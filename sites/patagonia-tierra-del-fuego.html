Santa Cruz’s Atlantic coast

In Santa Cruz, the Atlantic coast offered changing shoreline habitats. Shell middens show repeated uses of headlands and bays long after the oldest known occupation of Patagonia. The archaeologists who dated Cabo Blanco and Sitio Moreno mapped deposits within coastal landscapes. Their results measure episodes of food preparation and other local work over centuries rather than one migration event. Source 1 ↗

Land and sea in the Beagle Channel

Across the Strait of Magellan, published calibrated ages from the Beagle Channel show a long span of activity. At Túnel I, different layers contain evidence from separately dated occupations. At Shamakush, another coastal context records a much later time. The differing proportions of animal remains in such sites raise questions about how canoe-using peoples combined shore, sea and land foods. A date on an animal bone or on charcoal records its deposit and is more precise than regional names for subsistence practices. Source 1 ↗

The Dawson Island mission

European shipping and pastoral expansion brought violence and displacement. Chile’s 1890 decree granted a lease for the Salesian mission on Dawson Island, a place where Selk’nam people were forcibly confined. That document has a day and named colonial authority. The early archaeology gives other kinds of dates and makes the history of the region’s inhabitants visible long before published outsiders’ accounts. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Reading radiocarbon across the region

Some coastal dates come from land animals or charcoal and others from marine material, which requires reservoir corrections. The entries here use published calibrated windows. Their limits are distinct from dates inferred from rock paintings, from the earliest arrival evidence inland or from recent government records. Even along one shore, rebuilding and erosion can change precisely which activities archaeologists can recover. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
