Atlantic Brazil: mounds, villages and exchange

Along Brazil’s Atlantic coast and eastern river valleys, people made strikingly different places to live, work and remember their dead. Shell mounds rose beside lagoons. Communities built houses on wooden piles in Maranhão’s wetlands, worked stone in Ceará’s coastal dunes and made large ceramic vessels in the inland valleys. Some pots cooked food before becoming burial urns. A green-stone pendant and graphite in pottery reveal connections beyond a single settlement. This history follows excavated places and dated episodes, from early coastal mounds to the villages of the centuries before European colonization.

Fishing grounds, forests and early mounds

Rio Caipora preserves an early shell mound in southern Santa Catarina. Charcoal from its base dates to the sixth millennium BCE. The modern coast lies well away from this mound, but geologists place it beside an earlier lagoon landscape. The species and sediments brought into the mound are clues to a shoreline that later shifted as lagoons changed and filled. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At Piaçaguera, near an ancient bay in São Paulo, burial groups used the same mound during overlapping periods. Chemical evidence from teeth and bones suggests that people relied heavily on marine fish while eating foods from other environments as well. Slight differences between burial groups are a reminder that neighboring families can share burial grounds and draw on different foods. Source 1 ↗

Farther south at Babitonga Bay, a directly dated object reveals another part of coastal life. A harpoon component recovered at Morro do Ouro was made from southern right whale bone. The find and a second dated piece from nearby Conquista support the case for people pursuing large marine animals. Later dated burials at Morro do Ouro provide a different perspective. Alongside fish, there is evidence of substantial plant consumption, with cultivation remaining an open question. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

A sand core, a dry platform and a growing cemetery

The builders at Encantada III made a low mound with a sandy core and a thin shell-bearing cover. Burned material in the cover may have been brought in from elsewhere. The studied deposits contain few artifacts and no known graves. Its builders could have used the place for occasional processing or as a landmark. A much younger charcoal date near the surface may reflect a later visit. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Around the Saquarema lagoon in Rio de Janeiro, archaeologist Lina Maria Kneip found hearths and burials at Beirada and reported possible traces of temporary dwellings in this group of sites. In the Guapimirim wetlands at Amourins, people laid thick shells on mud to create a dry surface. The individual mounds they built were subsequently covered and joined. Excavated blocks from two graves preserve steps in the preparation of the places where people laid their dead. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Dates from shell mounds require attention to how the material arrived. At Tarioba farther northeast, researchers found evidence that shell layers contain material mixed over time and revised the chronology. At Cabeçuda on the Laguna shore, shell determinations date part of a mound that later rose to about twenty metres. Published calibrations differ, so the map retains the source and sample behind each range. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

The water itself also matters to a radiocarbon date. A shell records carbon from the water where the mollusc lived, while charcoal records carbon from the atmosphere absorbed by a plant. Estuaries mix water from different sources, so two materials buried together can give different raw ages. At Cabeçuda, comparisons among shell, charcoal and bone help researchers judge how to convert those ages into a calendar range. Source 1 ↗

At Jabuticabeira II, southern Santa Catarina, older shell deposits alternate with darker layers where excavators uncovered hearths and clusters of graves. The positions of those features support an interpretation of a cemetery built in many episodes. Shell was a bulk building material. Deposits rich in fish bones, including croaker and sea catfish, sit among the traces of funeral gatherings. The excavated sequence shows how people made a monumental place through repeated acts of burial and remembrance. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Later ways of using the lagoons

Around the first centuries CE, later graves were added at Cabeçuda, while a dark deposit rich in fish bones accumulated over the older shell layers at Jabuticabeira II. Charcoal and partly burned material in the cap point to work that took place before the deposits were carried to the mound. The older burial layers already contain fish remains, so the contrast between shell and bone primarily shows a change in construction. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Several kinds of place were in use around the seventh century. People at Içara 01 buried their dead in the dunes, in some cases after cremation or other preparation. On the dune beside Lagoa dos Freitas, bones of estuary fish are abundant and excavators found no graves. Those bones record catching and gathering at a different sort of place. A separate upper layer dates to a much later period, with some ranges extending beyond 1500. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Galheta IV preserves a built funerary mound, animal-processing deposits and Itararé-Taquara pottery. Isotopes in sampled teeth are consistent with coastal lives, and the pottery closely resembles other coastal assemblages. These findings bring burial practice, fishing and ceramics into the same local story while leaving room for contact with people elsewhere. Source 1 ↗

By the later centuries of the guide, broken vessels from Riacho dos Franciscos II beside the Jaguaruna lagoon preserve compounds consistent with maize use. The pots belong to the Guarani ceramic tradition, and a dated context places use of the locality in the thirteenth or fourteenth century. Vessel residues show which foods people sometimes prepared in pots. Fish bones, when they survive, tell a complementary story of what people caught and ate. The histories of both practices can be read along the same shore. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At Galheta IV, some pottery preserved residues from aquatic foods. The contrast with the maize evidence at Riacho gives concrete examples of how people used vessels around the lagoons. Some substances leave more detectable traces than others, and many meals never go into a pot. The shape and chemistry of a vessel each answer different questions about what people made and did. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Farther north, builders at Sernambetiba raised small shell mounds on a natural shell bed near Guanabara Bay. Some of the mounds were later joined by broader layers. Excavators uncovered human burials and dark bands containing charcoal and fish bones. Charcoal from two depths dates separate deposits around the turn of the era and into the first centuries CE. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Fishing communities used the coast in different ways

On the coast southwest of Guanabara Bay, people fished and collected food around Guaratiba’s mangroves and tidal channels. At Zé Espinho, they also buried their dead among shell deposits. A dated context associated with one burial falls between 600 and 1222 CE. The shell mounds contain traces of repeated activity over a much longer period. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A cremation on Ilha do Cabo Frio was found near Una pottery. Its calibrated range reaches into the sixth century. Pierced shark teeth and cut marine shells came from other deposits on the island. The archaeologists considered the new burial practice a possible sign of contact with Una potters. Source 1 ↗

Potters lived on hills across the Zona da Mata

On hills near the Rio Novo and its tributaries, people made, finished and used clay vessels. The early pottery sample at Mata dos Bentes has a published age of 604–804 CE. Later dated pieces from Primavera and Poça show that similar skills were practiced at other hills. Flattened, textured, painted and polished surfaces preserve choices potters made for different vessels. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Charcoal and pottery were found together in a hearth deposit at Córrego do Maranhão near Carangola. Several measurements overlap from the late thirteenth century onward. At Teixeira Lopes and Emílio Barão, pottery samples place activity before 1500. These date ranges apply to particular deposits and pots, and later ages at the same places show how the record can accumulate through separate visits and settlements. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A cemetery and a village in the Paraíba Valley

People placed dozens of clay urns in the ground at Caçapava 1. Some were buried alone and others in groups. A dated urn context falls between 1040 and 1260 CE, while several later urns and a pit oven have overlapping ranges from 1280 to 1430 CE. The shapes link the vessels to the Aratu-Sapucaí pottery tradition. Source 1 ↗

Farther west at Santa Marina in Jacareí, archaeologists documented patches of dark earth with pieces of clay vessels and worked stone. Two charcoal samples date activity there to 1400–1475 CE. Some patches overlap, which suggests that work areas or houses changed as people used the site. Source 1 ↗

On a terrace above the Rio Preto

At Rio Preto Oeste near São Mateus, the field team found ceramic fragments, combustion features and an older vessel reused as an urn. Soot and finger marks survive on the pottery. The assemblage has features associated with the Aratu tradition, together with surface treatments that led the excavators to propose contact with Tupiguarani potters. Source 1 ↗

The report’s conclusion places the village tentatively in the fourteenth century. The authors flagged possible contamination in some charcoal samples, and a different part of their report prints conflicting ages. The mapped century follows their archaeological interpretation and remains provisional. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Burials along the Araruama lagoon

At Morro Grande, large clay pots and painted bowls were used in burial rites. Charcoal from an early urn reaches the sixth century, and charcoal from a hearth by a burial elsewhere on the site dates to a substantially later episode. The difference in ages and the position of the finds suggest that people returned to the locality after a long interval. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At Bananeiras, mourners placed a young woman in an urn with painted bowls and shell pendants. The latest published calibration of her bones gives 1500–1681 CE. An earlier calibration gave two possible ranges, 1420–1510 CE and 1600–1620 CE. Pottery residues from the site give independent evidence of sixteenth-century activity. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

Fires in the dunes of western Ceará

At Jericoacoara, a dark soil layer held traces of hearths, charcoal and tiny shells. People chipped locally available stone into tools, changed their edges as they wore, and left fragments of bowls. Buried charcoal brings some of those visits into view as early as the fifth to sixth centuries CE. Other dates mark separate episodes later in the first millennium. Wind later exposed and shifted parts of the site. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Villages above the Maranhão wetlands

Hardwood posts preserve the outlines of communities along rivers and lakes that flooded seasonally. At Boca do Rio and Cabeludo, over a thousand surviving posts at each site form large central clusters with smaller groups nearby. Cooking vessels occurred most often around the likely houses. Figurines and more finely finished bowls concentrated near the centers. At Caboclo and Armíndio, the plans show clusters of much the same size. Source 1 ↗

The villages were spread across several river basins. Samples from Formoso include a timber dated within 441–637 CE and a distinct run of later dates on posts and soot. At Encantado, two wooden piles date broadly to the eighth through tenth centuries. A tree could grow for generations before its wood became part of a structure, so one piece of wood supplies one piece of the chronology. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A small, twice-perforated greenstone pendant from Boca do Rio points beyond the wetlands. Tests identified its material as nephrite, with no known occurrence in Maranhão in the study. The stone or the finished object traveled to the river settlement. Its starting place and the people who carried it remain unknown. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Potters and mourners in the interior

In western Bahia, groups of lidded clay urns at Praça de Piragiba preserve how people buried their dead along the Ribeirão Santana. In Alagoas, excavations on the Serra da Barriga found the living surface of an Indigenous village where potters made everyday vessels and forms used in burial. A date on pottery from that village belongs to the centuries before the ridge became central to Quilombo dos Palmares. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

At Serra do Evaristo I in Ceará, excavation exposed eighteen large vessels, six with remains of people. In the drier country near Brejo Santo, mourners placed an adult in an internally painted bowl and covered it with a second vessel. Two teeth from that burial give separate possible ranges in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Archaeologists describe the ceramics at Evaristo as Aratu and the painted vessel at Brejo Santo as Tupiguarani. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Rock shelters in the Pernambuco Agreste

People returned to sandstone shelters in the Catimbau valley to bury their dead. At Alcobaça, woven plant fibres, potsherds and hearths are part of a long sequence of use. A directly dated burial from 992–1152 CE was distinct from earlier deposits of burned remains. At Pedra do Cachorro, excavators reconstructed how an adult's remains had been prepared for secondary burial. The cause of death is unknown. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Graphite in pots from southern Bahia

At Canaã, near the Rio Colônia, potters mixed graphite into some vessels and coated others with it. Bones of hunted animals, stone tools and pottery collected together suggest a place where people prepared food. One graphite-coated sherd dates to approximately 1306–1496 CE. Chemical tests on four other sherds found the strongest resemblance to a graphite sample from the Jequié area, roughly 130 km away. Other deposits remain possible, and the route the graphite traveled is unknown. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Conflict, injury and the treatment of the dead

At Pedra do Cachorro in Pernambuco, archaeologists found an adult man’s bones that had been cut, broken while fresh and gathered into a secondary burial. The excavators read these marks as deliberate treatment of the dead. They tell us what people did with the body, but do not establish how the man died. The map therefore shows this as a burial, with the bone evidence explained in its record. Source 1 ↗

At Zé Espinho, a ray spine near a woman’s ribs has been discussed as a possible injury. A later examination questioned its position, and the dated male burial shown here is a different individual. The case remains a research lead. Sixteenth-century accounts describe organized Tupinambá warfare, but those later accounts cannot locate an earlier battle at one of these villages. This pass has added the excavated settlements and burial evidence while leaving the dates and participants of unverified conflicts open. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Dating method: TL measures time since firing. The approximate CE range references the dated 2021 lab report and its stated uncertainty.

The dated coated sherd is distinct from the four pieces measured by XRF. Untested sources remain possible, and disturbed deposits complicate the associations among individual fragments.

Canaã — possible graphite supply from the Jequié area

The strongest match among the tested deposits points toward the Jequié area, roughly 130 km from Canaã. Potters used graphite as a coating and mixed it into clay.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Graphite-coated sherd C-06-1 (DAT-5426): 620 ± 95 years by thermoluminescence in the report signed February 2021, approximately 1306–1496 CE.
Dating method: TL measures time since firing. The approximate CE range references the dated 2021 lab report and its stated uncertainty.

The Jequié attribution is provisional. Other deposits remain untested, and the four fragments measured for chemistry lack individual dates. This map view uses the same archaeological evidence as the Canaã site record.

Jericoacoara 1 — fires and tools between coastal dunes

In the dunes of western Ceará, people lit hearths, chipped stone tools and used pottery. Some tools were retouched or reused for other tasks, and the excavated layers held shell as well as charcoal.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: C1 charcoal Beta-504658 1526–1367 cal BP (424–583 CE), C1 shell Beta-504659 1487–1285 cal BP (463–665 CE), C2 and C5 charcoal Beta-478976 and -478975 1267–1070 cal BP (683–880 CE), and C1 charcoal Beta-290953 1055–924 cal BP (895–1026 CE). Earlier C2 sample omitted.
Dating method: Published calibrated BP ranges expressed as calendar years relative to 1950. The article used SHCal20 for charcoal and Marine20 with a reservoir correction borrowed from Bahia for the shell.

The layers record repeated use at multiple concentrations. The shell's correction comes from Bahia de Todos os Santos, and the charcoal provides independent dating evidence. Later wind erosion displaced some surface finds.

Baixio dos Lopes — painted pottery in a secondary burial

Near Brejo Santo, mourners placed an adult’s bones in a large vessel painted on the inside and covered the vessel with a ceramic lid. The vessel belongs to the polychrome pottery archaeologists call Tupiguarani.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Beta-447236, carbonate from two teeth of one adult, 1280–1320 or 1350–1385 cal CE.
Dating method: Published AMS carbonate calibration for the human teeth. The analysis used enamel because preserved collagen was unavailable.

One adult supplies the direct date. Much of the surrounding site had been disturbed before the rescue dig, and its original extent remains uncertain.

Pedra do Cachorro — mortuary use of a Catimbau rock shelter

In the sandstone shelter of Pedra do Cachorro, people laid an adult man to rest after handling and rearranging his remains. Cuts and fractures on the bones helped archaeologists reconstruct this treatment.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Human long bone PCe050hbsS01, Sepultamento 01 / Beta-424624: 1265–1305 and 1365–1375 cal CE (2σ), Quadro 1.
Dating method: Published AMS calibration of human bone from burial 01.

The excavators interpret the marks as intentional treatment of the body in a mortuary context. They do not establish the cause of death. Other burials in the shelter date to much earlier times.

Alcobaça — a dated burial in a Catimbau shelter

At Alcobaça, excavators found human burials among traces of pottery-making, basketry and other activity. The directly dated burial dates to 992–1152 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: AL.55-1 / Beta-474452, adult rib, 958–798 cal BP (992–1152 CE).
Dating method: Published calibrated BP endpoints in Borges et al. Table 1 expressed as CE relative to 1950. Bastos et al. identifies the dated rib.

The radiocarbon assay dates one burial. The other burials, rock art, campfires and objects represent a longer history at the shelter.

Rio Caipora — early shell-mound building

Builders at Rio Caipora laid down a shell mound in the lagoon country of what is now southern Santa Catarina. A charcoal sample from the base dates to 7570–7320 cal BP.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 7570–7320 cal BP (basal charcoal, Beta 234199, conventional 6590 ± 60 ¹⁴C BP, Giannini et al. 2010 Table 1).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The age of the wood and its relationship to deposition affect how closely the sample dates the start of building.

Piaçaguera — diets at a coastal burial mound

People buried at Piaçaguera drew on several food sources near an ancient bay. Chemical evidence in their teeth and bones points to substantial marine fish consumption alongside plants and land or freshwater foods.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 7151–5668 cal BP, published dated burial assemblage estimate. Individual calibrations are reported at 95.4% in Table 2.
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

Dietary proportions depend on the isotope model and baseline foods. The dated burials can establish presence across this span, with gaps in activity still possible.

Beirada — burial and work on the Saquarema lagoon

The sandy ridge between the Saquarema lagoon and the ocean gave Beirada’s builders access to fishing and gathering grounds. The excavations found hearth deposits, stone tools and numerous burials across four layers.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 5437–3440 cal BP, calibrated site chronology summarized by Guida, Bastos and Rodrigues-Carvalho (2024). Earlier excavation reports record a basal layer determination of 4520 ± 190 radiocarbon BP.
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The published range brackets dated site deposits. The residential interpretation comes from the arrangement of features, and the building plans are less certain than the graves.

Encantada III — sand core and shell cover

People raised a sandy core and covered it with a thin, shell-rich layer at Encantada III. The deposited cover contains burned shells and charcoal, with relatively few fish bones.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4971–4651 cal BP (charcoal, Beta 189713, 4320 ± 40 ¹⁴C BP, Merencio and DeBlasis 2021).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The purpose of the small mound remains uncertain. A younger charcoal result from near the surface may record separate activity long after construction.

Morro do Ouro — a whale-bone harpoon from Babitonga Bay

People at Babitonga Bay made a harpoon component from southern right whale bone. A specimen recovered at Morro do Ouro has a direct date of 4900–4710 cal BP.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4900–4710 cal BP (68.3% interval; direct radiocarbon measurement of object Col. Tiburtius 4215).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The dated specimen establishes the age of the bone. The use of such tools to pursue whales is the researchers’ inference from several kinds of evidence.

Morro do Ouro — fishing and plant foods

The people buried at Morro do Ouro ate fish, plants and other foods. Dental and chemical evidence points to a substantial role for plant foods alongside the resources of Babitonga Bay.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4824–4527 and 4510–4101 cal BP (outer radiocarbon ranges on sampled human remains, Pezo-Lanfranco et al. 2018).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

Plant use is better documented than cultivation at this site. The burial dates are an envelope with no consistent order by depth and cannot supply an exact sequence of house construction.

Cabeçuda — shell deposits at a growing mound

At Cabeçuda, people built a substantial mound in the Laguna landscape. Two shell samples place deposits from the mound in the third millennium BCE under the published calibration.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4417–4081 cal BP (shell, Beta 280006 and Beta 280008, each 4180 ± 60 ¹⁴C BP; DeBlasis et al. 2021 Table A1 calibration).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

Marine shell ages depend on local reservoir effects and the chosen calibration. Sample ages cannot date every layer or burial at the mound.

Cabeçuda — a later burial at the lagoon mound

People continued returning to Cabeçuda to bury their dead after earlier deposits had accumulated. This later date comes from collagen rather than a sample of the mound’s shell fill.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1860–1703 cal BP (collagen, Beta 297831, 2030 ± 30 ¹⁴C BP; DeBlasis et al. 2021 Table A1 calibration).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The calibrated range applies to the sample. Human collagen calibration can be affected by aquatic foods eaten in life.

Amourins — a shell platform above the mangrove mud

Builders spread thick shells over muddy ground at Amourins to make a dry surface. They raised small mounds, laid people to rest and added deposits that eventually joined adjacent mounds.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4250–3970 and 3910–3570 cal BP at 2σ (charcoal, 3800 ± 40 and 3530 ± 60 ¹⁴C BP, Gaspar et al. 2013 Table 1).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The charcoal samples date parts of the build-up. The published shell result gives a separate range and the foundations are incompletely exposed.

Read the history & sources ↗

Jabuticabeira II — building a cemetery in shell

At Jabuticabeira II, people buried their dead in groups of graves, lit fires and covered earlier burial surfaces. Repeated layers of shell built up a much larger monument around these events.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Basal shell Beta 253672 at 3471–3219 cal BP (Giannini et al. 2010 Table 1); basal charcoal 2880 ± 75 ¹⁴C BP at 3200–2770 cal BP and change toward fish-rich construction at 1860–1573 cal BP (Villagrán et al. 2011).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The dated deposits span many building episodes. Thin dark layers contain postholes and hearths within groups of graves, where their arrangement supports a funerary reading.

Jabuticabeira II — a fish-rich cap above the older shell mound

Later builders covered much of the cemetery at Jabuticabeira II with dark material rich in fish bone, charcoal and sediment. The cap retained patterns of deposition seen in the older shell mound.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Proposed start of fish-rich building at 1860–1573 cal BP; near-top bone Beta 234201 at 1329–1178 cal BP. Published calibrated ranges from Villagrán et al. (2011) and Giannini et al. (2010).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The exact transition date varies with chronology. The amount of fish bone in a construction layer cannot directly measure changes in diet or identify the builders.

Içara 01 — a burial ground near the Araranguá

People returned to the dunes near the lower Araranguá to bury their dead. The excavators found both single and multiple graves, with differences in how the bodies had been prepared.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1533–1320 and 1416–1185 cal BP (charcoal Beta 72196, 1580 ± 50 ¹⁴C BP; Beta 72197, 1450 ± 60 ¹⁴C BP; Merencio and DeBlasis 2021).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The charcoal anchors activity at the site. The bones establish several burial practices, while seasonal use and travel from the interior remain interpretations.

Lagoa dos Freitas — fishing and gathering at a dune site

People visiting the dune by Lagoa dos Freitas left fish bones and shell in a low coastal deposit. Catfish and croaker are abundant among the remains.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1360–1290 and 1315–1275 cal BP at 2σ (lower layer II; Beta 403222 and Beta 426824; Pavei et al. 2019 Table 1).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The excavators found no graves or hearth structures. The debris establishes processing and use of coastal foods, with the duration of stays unresolved.

Lagoa dos Freitas — later use of the dune

A second dated layer at Lagoa dos Freitas records people again using the dune centuries after the earlier fish-rich deposit. They left evidence of estuarine fishing and use of land animals.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 495–320 and 485–305 cal BP at 2σ (upper layer I in Trench 1; Beta 403220 and Beta 403221; Pavei et al. 2019 Table 1).
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

These dates apply to charcoal in Trench 1. Surface pottery of Guarani style is a separate observation from the dated deposits.

Riacho dos Franciscos II — lagoon-side pots and maize

People using Guarani-style vessels at Riacho dos Franciscos II prepared plant foods by the Jaguaruna lagoon. Residues on some potsherds are consistent with maize processing.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 730–565 cal BP (LT22559, 770 ± 40 ¹⁴C BP; Admiraal et al. 2023 Table 1). A second determination, LT22560, is 534–327 cal BP.
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon ranges in years before 1950 CE. Map years use 1950 minus cal BP.

The associated date anchors activity at the site. The dated sample need not be the same age as each analyzed vessel. Residues reflect some uses of the pots and leave other foods less visible.

Sernambetiba — layered shell mounds and burials by Guanabara Bay

People at Sernambetiba raised small mounds of shell near Guanabara Bay and covered some with more layers that joined them together. Excavators found fish bones, charcoal and human burials at the site.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 500 cm conventional charcoal: 1920 ± 70 ¹⁴C BP, 1981–1613 cal BP at 2σ. 80 cm AMS charcoal: 1800 ± 40 ¹⁴C BP, 1809–1543 cal BP at 2σ. Gaspar et al. (2019), Table 2.
Dating method: Published SHCal04 calibrations for charcoal. Map year equals 1950 minus calibrated BP. The two sample intervals are −31 to 337 and 141 to 407 in astronomical year numbering.

Two independently dated charcoal samples come from different depths. Their calibrated ranges overlap and date sampled deposits rather than the full duration of construction or residence.

Tarioba — occupation chronology and time mixing

Tarioba — occupation chronology and time mixing

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 2023 revision: start 3974–3642 cal BP; end 3593–3199 cal BP. Earlier 2014 model: around 3800 cal BP, lasting up to five centuries.
Dating method: Published calendar dates retained; calibrated BP referenced to 1950. No recalibration. BCE converted to astronomical years (1 BCE = 0). Unspecified BP is not converted.

Shell reworking and reservoir corrections complicate simple layer-by-layer dating; older duration estimates remain source-specific.

Galheta IV preserves a constructed funerary mound and marine-resource processing areas within an approximately 1300–500 cal BP sequence.
