Aleutian Islands: maritime communities, colonial pressures and continuity

The Aleutian and Pribilof islands hold histories of skilled maritime life, enduring villages and profound colonial disruption. Tools, houses, gardens, churches and working harbours reveal how Unangan communities lived and changed across the centuries before 1934.

Stone-working places at the eastern end of the chain

On Anangula, near Umnak Island, early craftworkers struck long blades from prepared stone cores and shaped their edges into cutting, scraping and gouging tools. Flakes preserve the labour of making tools around 9,000 years ago, far more clearly than the wood and bone that rarely survived there. The raw stone came from elsewhere, making the workshop part of a landscape of movement and material knowledge. This early blade site is separate from the younger village remains also visible on Anangula; the two should not become one settlement lasting unchanged for millennia. Source 1 ↗

The Quarry Site on Amaknak adds two distinct episodes to the eastern islands’ chronology. Charcoal associated with charred stones and a possible hearth dates to 7730–7580 calibrated years before 1950. Charcoal near the upper cultural limit gives a later range of 6950–6740 calibrated years before 1950. Blades and microblades connect these observations to stone-working traditions. The samples demonstrate activity at different times, not continuous residence through the centuries between them. They also fill part of what once looked like a poorly documented stretch of regional history. Source 1 ↗

Houses, boats and coastal food

At Chaluka beside present-day Nikolski, successive houses, discarded tools and food remains accumulated into a substantial village mound. Its roughly four-millennia history belongs to a living community landscape. On Amaknak, excavations at the Bridge site exposed portions of 30–40 partly sunken, stone-lined structures. Some had several rooms. Fireplaces, vents and channels beneath floors show builders organizing warmth and work indoors, while repair and rebuilding altered the houses. The dated charcoal samples come from different contexts, so the broad site chronology cannot be assigned wholesale to every room or wall. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Maritime skills joined these homes to their resources. Unangan hunters used covered skin boats, often called bidarkas, while larger open boats carried people and cargo. Sea-lion skins supplied boat covers, and other parts of the same animals became food, tools and clothing. Fishing could require cooperation: two paddlers could stabilize their boats while hauling a large halibut. Salmon, birds and marine mammals offered different seasonal opportunities. Sharing catches distributed food and the work of processing it, helping households manage the uncertainty of any individual hunting trip. Source 1 ↗

At the western end of the chain, the Near Islands synthesis places early settlements on Agattu and Shemya by roughly 3,000 years ago. At Aga Cove and Krugloi Point, coastal resources sustained households far from the eastern islands. Fishhooks, cutting tools, harpoon points and woodworking implements changed through the archaeological sequence. These were communities with their own histories, not simply the last stops on one known voyage. Much later, Attu residents still moved between a winter village and summer fishing places, showing how permanent homes and seasonal travel could coexist. Source 1 ↗

The fur trade enters through violence and coercion

Russian fur hunters encountered armed resistance at Agattu in 1745 and moved on to Attu. The historical account describes captive-taking and an attack that killed fifteen Unangan men in an effort to seize women. This was an intrusion into inhabited islands with established communities and political relationships. Farther east, Ivan Solov’ev’s expedition reached Sanak in 1771; the island’s history records renewed violence before the Russians departed in 1772. Sea-otter commerce expanded through these encounters, imposing demands on local skills and labour as well as taking animals from coastal waters. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The Pribilofs became a different kind of fur-trade workplace. Russian-associated settlements appeared at Garden Cove on St. George in 1786 and near Marunich on St. Paul in 1787. Hunting companies forced Unangan men from Aleutian and Alaska Peninsula communities to travel north for commercial sealing. These voyages served merchants’ demands rather than a freely chosen search for a new home. Sod houses, camps and later settlement consolidation gave the extraction economy a material geography. The forced movements also separated hunters from the households and subsistence work of their home communities. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Gardens and company-directed settlement changes

Korovinski on Atka Island preserves two different village histories. An ancestral settlement ended following a major volcanic disruption, centuries before Russians and Unangan reoccupied the locality around 1820. The later settlement included houses, warehouses and a church serving the Russian-American Company’s western administration. Extensive garden plots survive behind sod walls, with furrows still marking cultivation. Potatoes brought a new food-producing practice into this maritime economy. The excavator suggests the walls helped protect crops from domestic animals; their exact purpose and the productivity of the gardens remain interpretations rather than measured annual totals. Source 1 ↗

Companies also reorganized where people lived. In 1823, the Russian-American Company moved Sanak’s population and hunting group to Belkofski as sea-otter grounds declined; hunters continued returning to Sanak. On St. Paul in 1825, the company shifted the Polovina settlement toward Village Cove, where loading ships was easier. At Attu, a company settlement established at Chichagof Harbor in 1805 developed into the island’s sole permanent winter village by 1830. Later, families leaving Korovinski established present-day Atka around 1870. A move in permanent homes could transform daily life without ending travel or knowledge of older places. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

Church craft, seasonal movement and industrial shores

At Unalaska, local builders working with Ioann Veniaminov erected the first Church of the Holy Ascension in 1826, after his arrival in 1824. A replacement followed in 1858. The church constructed in 1894–1896 incorporated timbers from both predecessors, connecting generations of labour in one building. Its nave, sanctuary, side chapels and bell tower form a cruciform plan; interior work, especially the Chapel of St. Innocent’s icon screen, preserves Unangan craftsmanship. Missionary teaching and translation belonged within an existing community whose people participated in making the institution’s material world. Source 1 ↗

Changing commerce continued after Russian rule. A trading post opened on Sanak in 1873, and Akutan’s 1878 post linked settlement growth with trade and cod processing. At Attu in 1909, Jochelson’s expedition accompanied residents from Chichagof Harbor to Sarana Bay for the summer, recording seasonal movement rather than abandonment. Akutan’s industrial whaling station opened in 1912, processing whales for a commercial industry quite different from household food gathering. It remained active at the atlas’s 1934 endpoint. Villages, coastal work and community knowledge continued alongside these new demands, rather than ending when imperial ownership changed. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

Village life at Sandy Dunes and Pauloff Harbor

Sanak’s Sandy Dunes preserves repeated settlement beside an old pond. Small round or oval houses had hearths along one side. Shell middens protected food remains, and tools made from distant stone connect household life to travel across the island chain. Far later, the shore at Pauloff Harbor held buildings for a commercial cod fishery. Bunkhouses, sheds and stores supported the work of catching, salting and shipping fish. Cobb’s May 1912 photograph records the schooner Sequoia loading salted cod. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Chaluka and Nikolski — a village landscape across millennia

At Chaluka, layers of houses, tools and food remains built a large village mound beside present-day Nikolski. Archaeology and community history describe roughly four millennia of residence and changing use at this enduring settlement.

Dates and evidence notes

Dating method: Rounded archaeological and community-history synthesis.

Amaknak — charcoal and stone tools at a possible hearth

Excavators found charred stones, charcoal, blades and microblades at Amaknak’s Quarry Site. A charcoal sample from the possible hearth dates this activity to 7730–7580 cal BP, placing people in a period once thought poorly represented in the eastern islands.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 7730–7580 cal BP; Beta-237109,6820±40 radiocarbon BP
Dating method: Published OxCal 3.1/IntCal 04 calibration, Table 1; calendar BP 1950 converted to 5781–5631 BCE with no year zero.

The possible hearth is dated by one charcoal sample. The older 8040–7920 cal BP determination used possible soil organic material and is not used as this record’s date.

Amaknak Bridge — stone houses and elaborate hearths

On a knoll above the channel, builders terraced stone houses, repaired walls and laid new floors and hearths across older ones. Most houses were round or oval, about 3–4 metres across. A few were larger and rectangular.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Published charcoal-date envelope 3922–2358 cal BP; approximately 1973–409 BCE
Dating method: Outer envelope of calibrated charcoal ranges reported by Rogers; not a new pooled radiocarbon model.

The envelope spans samples from different contexts. It does not date every structure or establish continuous occupation throughout the whole range.
