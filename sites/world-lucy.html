Lucy and her world

On November 24, 1974, Donald Johanson and Tom Gray found the first fragments of a remarkably complete skeleton at Hadar in Ethiopia’s Afar region. Known as Lucy and as Dinkinesh in Amharic, and catalogued as AL 288-1, she lived about 3.2 million years ago. Her bones connect the story of discovery to a much older story of walking on two legs.

Lucy’s find area in the Afar and selected East African evidence for Australopithecus afarensis and other hominins of its time.

A skeleton from Hadar

Johanson and Gray were returning from a survey when they spotted a fragment of arm bone. The search recovered parts of the skull, trunk, pelvis and limbs, together representing about 40 percent of an individual skeleton by bone count. Lucy was assigned to Australopithecus afarensis, a species named for the Afar. [1] [2] [3]

November 1974 is the date of discovery. Lucy’s geological age of just under 3.18 million years comes from the position of her bones within Hadar’s layers. Radiometric ages from volcanic deposits, alongside magnetic and fossil evidence, place the skeleton in the Kada Hadar Member. [1]

Lucy’s fused bones and erupted wisdom teeth indicate an adult. The National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa holds the original fossil. Researchers can study and exhibit casts made from molds of the bones. [1]

Walking on two feet

Lucy’s pelvis, thigh and knee preserve the alignment and joint structure of a biped. In combination, bones from the same skeleton bring more information about how she moved than a single fossil joint could provide. The species retained features, including curved finger bones, consistent with climbing. [1] [3]

At Laetoli, the Site G footprints record actual steps in volcanic sediment. They predate Lucy by almost half a million years, and their usual attribution to Australopithecus afarensis rests on the track anatomy and the fossils found at Laetoli. An unusual neighboring trail from Site A suggests another form of bipedal gait. [8] [9]

Neighbors in the Afar

At Dikika, the juvenile skeleton known as Selam preserves anatomy Lucy’s remains leave incomplete. The Dikika team reported a lower limb consistent with bipedality and upper-body traits relevant to climbing. The fossil belongs to the same species and is slightly older than Lucy. [4] [5]

At Woranso-Mille, the 2025 description of teeth and jaws supported assigning a foot from Burtele to Australopithecus deyiremeda. Its grasping big toe points to a different mix of climbing and upright walking in a probable contemporary of Lucy’s species. Several kinds of hominins shared the Afar landscape across this interval. [6] [7]

Lucy’s place in the family

Australopithecus afarensis is a candidate for an ancestral population of later hominins. Comparing multiple species and their ages is essential, because the older skeleton of an upright walker by itself leaves open which population gave rise to Homo. Lucy provides unusually detailed evidence of one individual on that branching history. [3] [6]
