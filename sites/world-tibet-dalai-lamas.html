Tibet and the Dalai Lama institution

The title Dalai Lama began in a sixteenth-century encounter between a Tibetan teacher and a Mongol ruler. Over time, recognition of a new incarnation became a way to transmit religious authority. The Fifth Dalai Lama’s government joined that lineage to a state centered on Lhasa, and the rupture of 1959 changed what the institution could do and where it could operate.

The history of the lineage, the Lhasa-based Ganden Phodrang government and its continuation as a religious institution in exile. Cultural Tibet includes regions such as Ü-Tsang, Kham and Amdo. Its extent differs from both the historical reach of Lhasa’s government and the present Tibet Autonomous Region. Dates after 1934 continue the history within this guide.

How a lineage is recognized

In Tibetan Buddhist practice, a tulku is a teacher recognized as the rebirth or emanation of a predecessor. Such lineages existed before the Dalai Lamas and serve several Buddhist traditions. Recognition draws on religious authority and community acceptance. Search parties may use a predecessor’s instructions, reported dreams or oracles, and a child’s recognition of people or objects. The procedures have varied by period, and competing identifications have sometimes divided religious and political authorities. [6] [19] [15]

In 1578 the Mongol ruler Altan Khan bestowed the title Dalai Lama on the Geluk teacher Sonam Gyatso at an encounter in the Qinghai Lake region. Sonam was counted as the third holder because two earlier teachers, Gendun Drup and Gendun Gyatso, were identified retrospectively as the first and second. The exchange brought the Mongol court a prominent Buddhist teacher and the lineage a powerful patron. The Fourth Dalai Lama, Yonten Gyatso, came from Altan Khan’s family. [5] [4] [1] [37]

The Fourteenth Dalai Lama’s office recounts that a search party traveled to Amdo and presented a young boy in Taktser with objects associated with his predecessor. In this account, his responses mattered to the searchers. Recognition was followed by transfer to Lhasa and enthronement in 1940, while a regent exercised political authority. [27] [28]

From monasteries to government

The Geluk school grew around Tsongkhapa’s teachings and monasteries such as Ganden, Drepung and Sera. After military victories by the Khoshut Mongol leader Güshi Khan, the Fifth Dalai Lama took political authority in 1642. The new government took the name Ganden Phodrang from the Dalai Lama’s residence at Drepung. Building at the Potala soon provided a palace for government, worship and ceremony. [1] [11] [10] [7] [13]

The government relied on officials, councils, district administrators and local connections. Monastic and lay officials both served it. Power shifted among the Dalai Lama, appointed regents, aristocratic families, monasteries and external patrons. During a holder’s minority or a vacancy after death, a regent could govern for years. After the Fifth Dalai Lama died in 1682, Regent Sangye Gyatso kept his death secret for an extended period while the next holder was raised. The Sixth Dalai Lama’s eventual enthronement and deposition exposed the political stakes of recognition. [9] [7] [8] [12]

Mongol patronage, Qing intervention and Lhasa self-rule

Qing troops drove Dzungar Mongol forces from Lhasa in 1720. In 1727 the court established permanent imperial representatives there, known as ambans. Its influence over Lhasa varied across reigns and crises. Following the wars with the Gurkhas, the Qing court introduced the Golden Urn in 1793 for selecting among candidates in major reincarnation lineages. Historians trace both the court’s efforts to regulate recognition and the ways Tibetan and Mongol leaders negotiated and used the procedure. Its practical role differed across successions. [19] [12] [20] [38]

Political geography varied within cultural Tibet. In Kham and Amdo, local rulers, monasteries and imperial officials exercised authority in different combinations. After the Qing collapsed in 1911–1912, the Thirteenth Dalai Lama returned to Lhasa, and his government exercised substantial de facto self-rule until 1950. International recognition of independence was limited, and successive Chinese governments maintained claims to Tibet. Earlier imperial relationships, functioning government on the ground and modern claims to sovereignty address different historical questions. [21] [20] [22] [23]

Land, work and religious life

Monasteries trained scholars, preserved and printed texts, and organized rituals and festivals. Some also managed agricultural estates. Ordinary families encountered religion through household shrines, donations, pilgrimage and ritual services as well as through relatives in monasteries. The Dalai Lamas belonged to the Geluk school, alongside longstanding Nyingma, Kagyu and Sakya traditions. Bön maintained its own ritual and monastic institutions within the same cultural region. [15] [3] [2] [1]

In many areas under Lhasa’s government, the state, aristocrats and monasteries held estates. Households owed landlords grain, labor or other dues, and hereditary obligations could limit where a family could move or work. Some arrangements allowed a person to live away from an estate in exchange for payment. Obligations and material conditions varied by household and locality. Oral histories preserve the experiences of villagers, monks and officials as well as the records left by governing institutions. [16] [17] [18]

Tenzing Phuntsok, interviewed decades later, recalled that he and his brother entered a monastery as part of their nomadic family’s tax obligation. Another interview records Kesang Tsering’s experience of entering a monastery at eight and eventually becoming a factory worker after the 1959 uprising. These recollections show how obligations and political changes reached into individual lives. [39] [40]

From Chamdo to the 1959 uprising

In October 1950 the People’s Liberation Army defeated Tibetan forces at Chamdo. The Seventeen-Point Agreement, signed in Beijing on 23 May 1951, placed Tibet under the People’s Republic of China while promising to preserve its existing political system and the Dalai Lama’s position and to respect religious practice. Beijing presents the agreement as the basis for what it calls peaceful liberation. The Dalai Lama’s account describes the delegation as acting under duress. Contemporary diplomatic correspondence records objections and debate over the circumstances of the signing. [24] [25] [28] [36]

Land redistribution, collectivization and interventions in religious life in some eastern Tibetan areas helped drive armed resistance in the 1950s. The crisis reached Lhasa in March 1959. An uprising and its suppression ended the earlier arrangement. The Dalai Lama left Lhasa on 17 March, crossed into India on 31 March and received asylum. After reaching India he publicly repudiated the agreement. Refugees followed, and new institutions took shape in exile as the PRC reorganized government and landholding within Tibet. [26] [23] [28] [29] [36]

A religious lineage in exile

After an initial period in Mussoorie, the Dalai Lama moved to Dharamsala in 1960. Tibetans built institutions of exile governance, education and worship in India, including elected representative bodies and relocated monastic communities. In 2011 the Fourteenth Dalai Lama transferred his formal political responsibilities to elected exile leadership. The religious institution continued separately from the political office he had held. [28] [31] [33] [32]

In 2025 his office affirmed that the Dalai Lama institution would continue and said the Gaden Phodrang Trust alone held authority over recognition. China’s government responded that any successor required central government approval and that procedures included the Golden Urn. These positions show how recognition still brings religious practice into contact with competing claims of authority. [34] [35] [19]
