A coastal port for an inland city

Rome stood inland on the Tiber. In the first century CE Claudius built a harbour near the river’s mouth, and in the early second century Trajan added a sheltered, six-sided basin to the complex. Quays, canals and storage buildings organized the transfer of cargo between seagoing ships, warehouses and smaller vessels bound up the river. Excavation at Portus also records the people who worked, lived and were buried around this industrial landscape. [3] [4]

Samples from Portus preserve a changing story of food supply. The excavation team suggests that early third-century workers consumed wheat and olive oil brought from North Africa. Pottery and other finds show more North African products in later deposits. Egypt was another grain supplier to Rome, and Alexandria had a long history as a Mediterranean harbor. These sources reveal different parts of the supply system, including what reached the port and what workers themselves ate. [4] [9] [11]

Roads and shipping linked ports to different inland markets. Travel time depended on the kind of transport, terrain and season. The Itiner-e atlas brings archaeological traces and written evidence together and records different degrees of confidence in road alignments. A surviving stretch of paving can support part of a route, while the stretch between excavated points may be reconstructed from other evidence. [16] [17]

Two kinds of city on commercial networks

Floods, eruptions and subsequent excavation shape which buildings can be studied. Pompeii offers an exceptional street-level record because of the Vesuvius eruption of 79 CE. Shops opposite the Stabian Baths opened onto a busy part of the city. Their relationship to streets and public buildings helps place trade within the daily life of a neighborhood. [5] [18]

Palmyra grew around an oasis in the Syrian Desert. Merchants and caravans linked it with settlements in Roman Syria and with territories toward and beyond the Euphrates. Funerary reliefs tell another part of its history. The portraits and inscriptions marked family tombs and communicated names and relationships in locally meaningful ways. They preserve the choices of individual families in a multilingual provincial city. [12] [19] [20]

Citizens, freedpeople and military households

A funerary portrait from early imperial Rome may represent a man who had been freed from slavery. Such objects give uneven access to the past: people who could commission a portrait are easier to see than many of the enslaved people whose labor supported Roman households and businesses. The sculpture’s setting and comparison with inscribed reliefs help historians interpret who it may portray. [21]

Military service was one route to citizenship for some noncitizens. Bronze diplomas record grants to qualifying individuals. In 212 CE Caracalla’s edict extended Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. A fragmentary Greek copy preserved on papyrus is an unusually direct witness to that imperial decision. Enslavement and distinctions of wealth and social rank persisted. [22] [23] [21]

At Vindolanda, an invitation from Claudia Severa asks Sulpicia Lepidina to attend a birthday celebration. The wooden tablet preserves the domestic and social lives of officers’ households near a northern garrison. It is an unusually intimate document, surviving alongside other letters and accounts from the site. [14] [15]

Artifacts and what they can tell us

A harbour, a letter and a legal document answer different scales of question. Portus shows how cargo could be handled, the Vindolanda tablet records a relationship between two people, and the citizenship edict captures a change intended to reach almost the whole free population. [4] [15] [23]

Aqueducts, Hydraulic Gold Mining & Cosmopolitan Urban Mobility

High-density Roman urbanization depended completely on gravity hydraulic engineering and industrial metallurgy. Eleven aqueducts delivered ~1 million cubic meters of spring water daily into Imperial Rome; the 49-meter three-tiered Pont du Gard supplied Nîmes; Segovia and Lugdunum used granite arcades and inverted lead siphons; and across northwestern Spain at Las Médulas (~400 km of high canals) and Rio Tinto waterwheel mines, hydraulic engineers sluiced mountainsides for coinage gold and silver. [24] [25] [26]

Ancient DNA sequencing from Imperial Rome (1st–3rd centuries CE) demonstrates that imperial road-and-port integration literally transformed city biology: residents traced deep genetic roots disproportionately across Greece, Anatolia, Syria, and Egypt alongside Mediterranean Europe—reflecting massive merchant, freedperson, and slave mobility flowing into Rome's Portus and Monte Testaccio commercial engine. [27]

Morus to my lord Epimachus, greeting. I write to inform you that we have winnowed the barley of the man from the Oasis on the 8th, and we never had so much trouble in winnowing it; for it rained and the wind was irresistible, and Panares knows how we worked to succeed in transferring all the rest with the help of the gods. After many inquiries I found some that was dry, and not to be paid for in ready money, but after four months. You will examine the question how you are to transport it, and, if you please, write to me about this, and say what proportion I am to dispose of beforehand from the large holding, and whether you want me to mix what belongs to the man from the Oasis with the rest. I have stored my share in the room belonging to your father. I supplicate on behalf of you and al your children and all your brothers and ... I pray for your health, my lord.
