China's Dynastic Capitals & Grand Canal: Hydraulic States from Erlitou to Ming–Qing

Unlike fragmented empires that rose once and vanished, successive Chinese dynasties stitched the millet-growing North China Plain to the rice-rich Yangtze basin with the 1,800-kilometer Grand Canal, stamped brick ramparts, and nationwide fiscal banking.

Yangtze & Yellow River hearths, Grand Canal pound locks, Song Kaifeng & Hangzhou, Ming Nanjing, Changsha/Dingzhou/Jingdezhen kilns, Quanzhou–Zhangzhou ports, and Pingyao banks.

Urban grids and Central Plain vs. multi-regional origins

Radiocarbon banks across Early China show walled third-millennium complexes emerging simultaneously in Zhejiang (Liangzhu), Shaanxi (Shimao), and Shanxi (Taosi) before Central Plain states at Erlitou and Anyang standardized bronze ritual hierarchies. [3] [2] [5]

The Grand Canal: hydraulic unification across shifting rivers

Linking five natural river basins over 1,800 kilometers, Sui, Song, Yuan, and Ming engineers invented pound-lock gates at Huai'an and summit feeders in Shandong to supply northern capitals with Jiangnan tribute rice. [1] [6]

Sui–Tang underground granaries in Luoyang and Northern Song Kaifeng's stacked brick street layers buried under Yellow River silt reflect massive canal-borne state logistics. [1] [2]

Kiln workshops, Shanxi banks and interior tea-salt industries

High-temperature coal kilns in Hebei (Dingzhou), copper-painted Tang dragon kilns in Changsha, and Jingdezhen export porcelain combined with Pingyao *piaohao* silver drafts and Zigong deep-bore salt wells. [7] [2] [8]

Maritime Silk ports from Canton and Quanzhou to Moon Harbor

From Guangzhou's Tang Nanhai Temple and Song Quanzhou's oyster-cemented bridges to late-Ming Yuegang (Moon Harbor), Chinese coastal ports brokered silk and blue-and-white ceramics directly for Persian, Southeast Asian, Japanese, and Manila bullion. [4] [10] [11] [12]
