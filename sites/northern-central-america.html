Two kinds of volcanic evidence

People built and rebuilt settlements amid volcanic landscapes. In the Zapotitán Valley, excavations at San Andrés show builders incorporating fresh deposits from Ilopango’s eruption into a monumental platform. The evidence dates construction and gives clues about how people mobilized labor after a disaster. At nearby Joya de Cerén, later ash sealed a farming settlement and preserved houses, work areas and other details of ordinary life. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Houses and ceramics on Lake Nicaragua

The lakeshores of Pacific Nicaragua permit comparison between communities with different material and architectural histories. At El Rayo, deposits span changes in ceramics that archaeologists use to recognize the Bagaces and Sapoá periods. At Santa Isabel, a set of radiocarbon dates revised an interpretation that had rested heavily on the appearance of pottery. The results help separate the dates of households from assumptions about which ethnic groups made particular vessels. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Where excavation could still take place

At Tepetate, excavations explored surviving mounds and urn burials after modern development had removed much of the site. These local studies extend the regional atlas beyond El Gigante and Copán in Honduras. Their dates support the story of repeated activity, damage and rebuilding in particular places without claiming a single shared sequence across the region. Source 1 ↗

Uneven survival around the region

The present landscape exposes some parts of the past much better than others. Ash at Cerén preserved detail that is ordinarily lost. At Tepetate, rescue excavation could study only the surviving areas. In both places, dating and excavation establish observations with specific contexts. The map leaves room for people using sites between those windows and for places still unstudied. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
