Hadrian’s Wall

From Wallsend on the Tyne to Bowness on the Solway Firth, Hadrian’s Wall crossed river valleys, uplands and low coastal ground. Its curtain wall was one part of an evolving system of fortifications, roads and communities that can still be traced through excavation and surviving earthworks.

Northern Roman Britain, from the start of construction around 122 CE through the late Roman period and evidence of later occupation at Birdoswald. The guide map covers the Tyne–Solway corridor.

Building across the isthmus

Construction began on Hadrian’s orders around 122 CE. Military roads and forts already served the region, including along the Stanegate to the south of much of the new line. Legionaries built much of the frontier. Plans changed as the work went on, including the addition of forts directly on the line and an eastward extension to Wallsend. The western part initially used turf in stretches later rebuilt in stone. [13] [14] [15]

The completed curtain ran for roughly 80 Roman miles, about 117 kilometers, between Wallsend and Bowness-on-Solway. Beyond the western end a chain of military installations extended down the Cumbrian coast. The frontier used ridges, rivers and shorelines as well as constructed barriers. Ground plans and excavations reveal its course where the standing wall has disappeared. [2] [16] [15] [1]

Gates, towers, forts and the Vallum

Regularly spaced gateways occupied small fortified enclosures known as milecastles. Two smaller turrets generally stood between neighboring milecastles. At Poltross Burn, the surviving stair steps help archaeologists estimate the level of an upper walkway. At Chesters, fortifications overlooked the North Tyne crossing, and excavated structures include a bathhouse down by the river. [14] [5] [9]

A ditch lay north of the wall where the ground called for one. The Vallum lay to the south and consisted of a broad ditch with banks. Its causeways channeled movement through the military zone. A road known as the Military Way linked installations behind the curtain in a later phase. These features show how the army used depth, communication and controlled passage along the line. The people around the line

Farmers lived in northern Britain before the wall went up, and farmsteads and settlements continued around the military zone. Excavation near Brampton preserves traces of enclosed Romano-British farmsteads. Near the forts, civilians lived close to military households. The archaeological record captures some of the people moving across long distances into this region as well as people with local roots. [18] [7] [19] [20]

At South Shields, beyond the wall’s eastern end, a gravestone commemorates Regina. Its Latin text identifies her as a woman of the Catuvellauni and the freedwoman and wife of Barates, who was from Palmyra in Syria. A second inscription uses Palmyrene Aramaic. One surviving object thus records enslavement, freedom, marriage and migration in the society around the frontier. [20] [2]

Abandonment, return and later occupation

In the 140s CE Antoninus Pius pushed the frontier north to the Forth–Clyde line, building the separate Antonine Wall. About two decades later Roman forces returned to Hadrian’s Wall and brought installations back into use. The frontier continued to be rebuilt and occupied through the later Roman centuries. [13] [15]

Birdoswald gives unusually detailed evidence for the period after formal Roman rule in Britain ended. Excavations found timber structures built over parts of the Roman fort, including former granary areas. This sequence shows continued use of the site and a change in the form of occupation. Archaeologists infer how the new buildings may have functioned from their postholes and relationship to the former fort. [3] [17]

Latin · Supply request

The soldiers have no beer

97–105 CE · Vindolanda, Northumberland, England
Tab.Vindol. 628, front, column ii, lines 4–6

Masclus, a cavalry officer, asks his commander for instructions about the next day. He adds the request for beer after signing off. This wooden tablet predates Hadrian’s Wall.
