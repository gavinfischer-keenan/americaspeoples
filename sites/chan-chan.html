The first compounds in an expanding city

Chan Chan’s adobe walls belong to successive urban projects. Kolata’s brick-seriation model places the primary occupation of Chayhuac and Uhle around AD 900–1100. Their modern names, Chayhuac An and Xllangchic An, connect older scholarship with the named archaeological compounds. Walls, entrances and water-related features could be altered within and after these broad phases. Courts, storage and ceremonial spaces formed settings in which access and activities were organized. The estimated primary-occupancy phases are not radiocarbon foundation dates. Neither compound has a securely identified first ruler in the evidence considered here, and the royal-palace interpretation does not supply written biographies of its occupants. New walled compounds and controlled movement

Tello and Laberinto, now Tsuts An and Fechech An, belong to the next estimated primary-occupancy phase, approximately AD 1100–1200. Their architecture organized movement through courts, approaches to U-shaped structures and access to storage spaces. These arrangements support questions about administration, but do not identify the exact function of every room or status of every occupant. Topic interprets U-shaped buildings through information flow and a changing relationship between stewards and bureaucrats. That remains an attributed architectural interpretation, distinct from the estimated compound chronology. The physical organization of entrances and spaces provides stronger direct evidence than an assumed administrative title for each person who worked there. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

1100 is the opening anchor of the estimated 1100–1200 compound phase. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

A city of differently organized complexes

Gran Chimú and Squier occupy the Middle Chimú phase, approximately AD 1200–1300, in Kolata’s reconstruction. Their modern names are Utzh An and Fochic An. Different internal layouts and interpretations of unfinished features complicate any image of identical palaces repeated across the city. Those differences can reveal changing projects, access and priorities without proving that each compound belonged to one named king. Beyond the large compounds, small irregular agglutinated rooms contain evidence for crafts. The great walled complexes and these smaller working spaces formed a heterogeneous urban setting; neither every artisan’s administrative affiliation nor identical simultaneous use of every compound can be established. Storage, craft and the organization of labor

Velarde and Bandelier, now Ñing An and Ñain An, are assigned an estimated primary-occupancy phase around AD 1300–1400. They belong to a city whose built spaces supported different kinds of work. Courts, audiencias, storerooms, wells and funerary platforms formed distinct settings, while craft evidence also comes from densely arranged smaller rooms outside the major compounds. Metalworking is specifically documented in the urban discussion. This does not make every resident a specialized metalworker or establish uniform control over production. Scholars interpret administration through the spatial pattern, but the organization of labor remains less directly observed than the surviving walls, tools and production debris. Nik An and Chol An in the late capital

Tschudi and Rivero, known today as Nik An and Chol An, belong to Kolata’s latest primary-occupancy phase, approximately AD 1400–1470. They give architectural detail to the broader description of Chan Chan’s fifteenth-century apogee. Later projects also replaced and reworked older parts of the city, so earlier buildings did not all survive unchanged. Nearby Huanchaquito-Las Llamas provides separate evidence for a mass killing during cal AD 1400–1450. It is a different locality, not an event inside either compound. Heavy-rain mud is associated with that deposit, while the specific motive and an ENSO-disaster explanation remain interpretations rather than established facts. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

1400 anchors a compound phase; nearby sacrifice has its own 1400–1450 calibrated range and is not mapped at this point. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Political conquest without an invented empty city

The official conservation history places the Inca conquest of Chimor around AD 1470. That convention marks a political transition, rather than proving that every household vanished or every compound was destroyed in the same year. Kolata’s final primary-occupancy phase also ends at 1470, but a scheme organized around a political boundary does not directly date the last activity in each building. Incorporation into a new political order and the later survival of architecture are different historical processes. The evidence considered here does not establish a precise battle, a route taken by invading forces or a citywide destruction layer at the conventional conquest date. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Brick-seriation dates are estimated primary-occupancy phases, not laboratory dates for every wall. Preserve the model’s attribution and do not invent precise compound coordinates.

The palace-per-ruler interpretation and information-control reading are scholarly models. Topic’s full paper was not inspected.

Modern compound names differ from older literature; official master-plan overview has a spelling variation for Gran Chimú, while its entry uses Utzh An.

Huanchaquito-Las Llamas is a separate place. Its mass killing, rain-related mud and calibrated range are supported, but a specific ENSO-sacrifice motive remains conjectural. No new observation is proposed until its public coordinate is independently verified.

Inca conquest around 1470 is not proof of immediate total abandonment.

Chan Chan: monumental capital during the fifteenth-century Chimú apogee

Chan Chan’s earthen palatial compounds and infrastructure form the archaeological record of the Chimú capital. UNESCO associates the kingdom’s apogee with the fifteenth century before Inca conquest.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: UNESCO identifies the Chimú kingdom’s apogee in the 15th century, shortly before Inca conquest, and Chan Chan as its capital.
Dating method: Archaeological heritage synthesis connecting the capital’s monumental remains with the historical fifteenth-century apogee; the page supplies no calibrated sample dates.

A kingdom-level apogee does not independently date each building. The record identifies the capital’s associated phase only; it gives no precise conquest or abandonment year.

Chan Chan — local community evidence

Chan Chan’s monumental and residential remains record a large Chimú city whose development spans the ninth through fifteenth centuries. Inca conquest changed its political position; that is not a date when all people disappeared.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximate archaeological phase: 800 to 1499; negative years are BCE. See scope below.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; approximate bounds retain the source’s chronological resolution.
