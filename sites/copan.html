Before the royal Acropolis

Excavation beneath the Northern Court Complex reaches constructions assigned to approximately 350–400 CE, before the royal Acropolis took its later form. Investigators reached these levels below the later buildings around Court 4 B, including Loro, Tucán and Pato. Their names locate the excavated context but do not give those later buildings the dates of the deposits beneath them. The opening scene establishes a place already being built and used, allowing the subsequent appearance of royal architecture to be understood as a transformation of an earlier landscape. Source 1 ↗

Viewing year within report's approximate Division VI, 350–400 CE. Source 1 ↗

A new architectural center

Within the early Acropolis sequence, Hunal stands out for its red-painted talud-tablero façade and north-facing stair. Nearby Tartan marked a boundary between an early ballcourt complex and the Acropolis, while other buildings and drains expanded the constructed setting. These features belong to the report's broad 400–450 CE division. Hunal's architectural form invites comparison with central Mexico, but resemblance alone cannot demonstrate conquest or a person's ethnicity. The building and associated tomb support investigation of dynastic beginnings while leaving their more specific identifications open to additional evidence. Source 1 ↗

Viewing frame within Division V, 400–450 CE; not exact Hunal construction or founder accession. Source 1 ↗

Oropéndola inside the later pyramid

Oropéndola survives inside Structure 10 L-16, making the later pyramid a container for earlier architectural histories. Its carved stone mosaic façade differs from the modeled stucco associated with other early buildings, and its arrangement can be compared with nearby Rosalila. Stratigraphy suggests construction around 550–600 CE; lintel samples and ceramics provide additional checks. The Spanish dating section and full context resolve an erroneous BCE label in the English abstract. This scene follows a particular temple's construction rather than assigning the same age to every buried building in the Acropolis. Source 1 ↗

Viewing year within suggested construction window 550–600 CE. Source 1 ↗

A stairway remembers a ruler

In 710 CE, according to Fash's research account, Ruler 13 dedicated the first Hieroglyphic Stairway to commemorate Ruler 12, who had died in 695. The inscription and architectural sequence connect a public stairway with a royal burial and remembered reign. Archaeological investigation beneath the structure supports the distinction between this first version and its later extension. The stairs thus record an act of making history visible in stone, while the current date belongs to the dedication rather than to every event recalled in its text. Source 1 ↗

Epigraphic dedication year in Fash's 2017 research account. Source 1 ↗

Rewriting dynastic continuity

The expanded Hieroglyphic Stairway received another dedication under Ruler 15, dated to 755 CE in Fash's account. Different carving styles help distinguish the earlier and later inscriptions, even though fallen and rearranged blocks complicate their reconstruction. References to Ruler 13's death at the hands of Quiriguá place conflict within the monument's political story. The renewed building effort also demonstrates that defeat did not simply stop dynastic representation. A one-year difference in some institutional summaries is retained as a chronological discrepancy rather than silently resolved. Source 1 ↗

Later dedication year in Fash's 2017 account; one-year variants occur in institutional summaries. Source 1 ↗

Households, rainwater and a changing court

Copán's residents relied on water collection and distribution as well as the royal monuments that dominate surviving views. Research interprets the East, West and Hieroglyphic Stairway courts as parts of a rainwater-collection system, and investigation near Las Sepulturas supports an ancient pond. Índigo and Temple 22 connect drainage and water imagery; Structure 9 N-82 brings maize and water symbolism into a residential setting. These features link subsistence, community organization and authority, although their exact operating dates and the proposed social relationships are not all independently established. After royal monument building

An influential 1990 settlement model separates the decline of royal authority around 800 CE from continuing nonroyal elite activity over roughly two more centuries. Its evidence came from a large program of survey, excavation and obsidian-hydration dating. This makes the wider settlement landscape central to the question of what followed the royal court. The result remains an older, method-dependent model, accessed here through its abstracts. It supports a carefully labeled later-activity scene rather than a claim that a particular palace was still occupied in 950. Source 1 ↗

Viewing year within an older model of subroyal activity, approximately 800–1000 CE; confidence explicitly limited. Source 1 ↗

Copán: Classic-period urban and ceremonial development

Copán’s Classic-period development includes successive temples, plazas, altars, and ball courts. UNESCO places its principal florescence in approximately 300–900 CE.

Source date guidance: UNESCO Brief Synthesis identifies Copán’s great period as AD 300–900 and describes multiple stages of architectural development.
Dating method: Authoritative archaeological heritage synthesis of the local Classical-period sequence; no individual radiocarbon determinations are provided in the page.

The synthesis spans multiple rulers and building episodes. It does not provide a uniform occupation interval for the valley or sample-level dating uncertainties.

Copán — local community evidence

Copán’s major Classic-period urban development dates approximately 300–900 CE. This city-core phase is distinct from much earlier habitation in the surrounding valley.

Source date guidance: Approximate archaeological phase: 300 to 900; negative years are BCE. See scope below.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; approximate bounds retain the source’s chronological resolution.

The interval describes the cited archaeological phase; its end does not by itself establish abandonment. Later and earlier use may exist beyond this record. Published World Heritage property reference point; city/site scale, not an individual building or surveyed perimeter.
