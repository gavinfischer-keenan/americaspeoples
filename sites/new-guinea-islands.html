New Guinea and neighbouring islands

New Guinea’s history combines ancient highland occupation, independent cultivation, island settlement and maritime exchange. The Bismarcks and south coast were connected to wider Pacific movements without sharing one chronology. Colonial ports and boundaries arrived much later, amid societies whose land, food systems and partnerships already had deep histories.

Highlands and offshore islands before Lapita

Archaeology places human adaptation and plant use in New Guinea’s highlands between roughly 49,000 and 44,000 calibrated years BP. These were inhabited landscapes long before pottery associated with Lapita appeared offshore. Mololo Cave on Waigeo supplies another early local record near western New Guinea, with plant processing and use of rainforest and marine resources. Neither locality alone establishes a complete route into Sahul. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Buang Merabak on New Ireland adds an offshore history around 42,000 calibrated years BP. Its cave deposits include food remains and stone-working evidence; reaching New Ireland required sea travel. The map intentionally locates the island broadly, rather than claiming an exact cave coordinate. Early settlement establishes maritime capability, but it does not reveal the form of the vessels or the itinerary of the first successful crossing. Source 1 ↗

Making productive landscapes at Kuk

Kuk’s buried cultivation surfaces document people changing wet ground through mounds and drainage. The existing atlas separates early cultivation, mounded systems and later ditches rather than displaying one permanently operating farm. This sequence is central to the evidence for an independent development of agriculture in New Guinea. Work with plants and water preceded the arrival of Lapita pottery by millennia. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

These changes were practical achievements in a highland environment: the position of cultivated surfaces and the movement of water mattered to food production. Their survival below later deposits lets archaeologists recover activities that left no monumental buildings. A ditch’s date concerns that part of the landscape; it does not establish the foundation of every nearby settlement or the continuous use of every earlier field. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Waim: craft and social life in the highlands

At Waim in the Jimi Valley, an upper cultural horizon dated to 5050–4200 calibrated years BP preserves a carved stone face, formally made pestles, axe-adze production and a tool interpreted as evidence of fibercraft. These finds broaden the picture beyond food production. They show technical and expressive activities developing in the highlands before the later Lapita expansion. Source 1 ↗

The study links this combination of practices with regional agricultural intensification. It does not require every innovation to have arrived as one imported package. The map’s broad Jimi River reference also keeps a public explanatory marker distinct from the modern village and excavation. A community’s place should not become a precise archaeological destination merely because its discoveries are scientifically significant. Source 1 ↗

Lapita settlements and several directions of movement

Mussau and other Bismarck sites document the early appearance of Lapita pottery, with modeled onset ranges rather than exact founding days. At Kamgot, an individual charcoal determination dates activity beside a changing lagoon edge. These records belong to particular settlements and environmental settings; decorated pottery is not a surveyed border around one ethnically uniform people. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Later settlement extended east through the island chains toward Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga and Samoa. The atlas already contains those staged chronology records, so this guide links them rather than duplicating them. Their connecting lines are schematic: the dated arrival evidence is generally stronger than the identification of a particular departure island, and neighbouring destinations need not have been reached in one compulsory sequence. The south coast was part of the story too

Caution Bay’s Lapita ceramics reveal another direction of connection, onto New Guinea’s south coast. They belong to a ceramic sequence within an already inhabited region, not the first arrival of humans there. Obsidian sourced to Fergusson Island reached Caution Bay over a long span, showing material movement across a maritime network. The source of a stone and its destination are better established than the number of intermediaries who carried it. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The south coast therefore complicates a simple west-to-east migration arrow. People made connections along and toward the mainland as well as farther into the Pacific. A ceramic transition or a decline in the import of one material can signal changes in demand and partnership; neither automatically means that a settlement was abandoned or that voyaging ended. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Agila, Motupore and the work of exchange

Agila in Hood Bay lay among overlapping maritime networks. Excavation places its establishment within 770–550 calibrated years BP, with pottery resembling ancestral Motu material farther west. Later vessels, in contexts dated 490–300 calibrated years BP, have closer affinities with ancestral Mailu pottery to the east. Motupore supplies a valuable comparative sequence. This change reveals shifting connections without demonstrating a complete replacement of Agila’s inhabitants. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Historical hiri exchange involved Motu crews taking pottery and shell valuables westward and returning with sago and canoe hulls. Large lakatoi combined hulls into sailing vessels capable of carrying substantial cargo. Such voyages depended on seasonal conditions, knowledge and enduring relationships with partners ashore. Hood Bay communities participated in making connections between Motu and Mailu spheres; they were active negotiators rather than an anonymous gap between two better-known centres. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Visits, colonial claims and a built waterfront

John Moresby’s naval visit in February 1873 gave European names to Port Moresby and Fairfax Harbour. It did not discover an empty coast. In 1884, British and German claims divided the eastern part of New Guinea into different colonial administrations. British New Guinea was annexed in 1888 and became the Australian-administered Territory of Papua in 1906. Those legal changes should not be confused with first local habitation or equal colonial reach everywhere. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

A dated photograph of Rabaul’s waterfront in 1910 documents a colonial port with a newly erected wharf. In September 1914, Australian forces landed at Kabakaul and advanced toward Bita Paka, where German reservists and Melanesian police defended the wireless station. The fighting changed colonial control and involved islanders on the ground. The 1921 mandate created another administrative arrangement; Papua and mandated New Guinea remained distinct through this guide’s 1934 endpoint. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

The highlands continued to change

Kuk’s more recent agricultural history includes the adoption of sweet potato, changes in pig husbandry and new scales of production and exchange. These developments unfolded over generations, rather than beginning when Europeans reached the interior. The source distinguishes several local sequences and warns against turning a new crop into a single instantaneous social revolution. Source 1 ↗

In 1933, Europeans described Kuk’s wetland as grass-covered and uninhabited after the Kawelka had moved away. That local observation does not imply that the wider Wahgi Valley lacked people, or that customary relationships to Kuk had disappeared. Ending here holds two histories together: expanding colonial observation and continuing Indigenous decisions about land, cultivation, movement and belonging. Source 1 ↗
