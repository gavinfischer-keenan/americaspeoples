The Roman Empire

Roman power reached across Europe, North Africa and western Asia. From Rome to Alexandria, Carthage, Palmyra and Constantinople, people experienced it through different institutions and ways of life. These sites show how imperial rule changed over time and why the history of Rome has several turning points.

The late Republic and Roman Empire, with emphasis on the first centuries of imperial rule and on changes from the third to seventh centuries CE. The medieval eastern Roman Empire appears in outline through 1453.

How republican expansion became imperial rule

Rome governed subject territories well before it had an emperor. Republican armies and diplomacy established provinces around the Mediterranean. In the last century BCE civil wars transformed who held power at Rome. Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BCE, took control of Egypt in 30 BCE, and received the name Augustus from the Senate in 27 BCE. The last of these events is the conventional start of the imperial period. [19] [9] [1]

Augustus later claimed that he had returned public affairs to the Senate and people. His account presents the new order through the ruler’s own eyes. Augustus retained decisive power and republican titles and institutions remained in use. The early imperial arrangement is often called the Principate, from the emperor’s position as Rome’s leading citizen. [20] [1]

How the provinces worked

Subjects encountered the empire in the demands of soldiers, tax collectors, local authorities and courts, and in opportunities offered by commerce or military service. The same imperial system could leave quite different local records. At Dendur in Egypt, Augustus appears in the visual role of a pharaoh in a temple honoring Egyptian gods and two local figures. Farther north, a bronze military diploma is evidence of how service in the Roman army could confer citizenship before Caracalla’s broader grant in 212 CE. [21] [22] [23]

The Mediterranean made long-distance movement possible, while harbours, roads and settlements organized who and what could move. The evidence for a sea journey, a road segment or a commercial community comes from different sources. An excavated harbour records the infrastructure that made shipping possible. Inscriptions and goods reveal particular links, while researchers combine archaeological and documentary evidence to reconstruct a wider road system. [24] [25] [26]

A single object can capture more than one kind of connection. The gravestone of Regina, found at South Shields in northern Britain, records a woman connected with a British people and a man from Palmyra. Its two inscriptions use Latin and Palmyrene Aramaic. The monument gives a human scale to the empire’s geographic extent and to the migrations and legal relationships discussed in the linked studies. [27]

When Roman rule changed

The empire had more than one political center by Late Antiquity. Constantinople was dedicated in 330 CE and became the enduring eastern capital. The western court moved to Ravenna in 402. In the fifth century, armies and kingdoms took over parts of the West. Some successor rulers continued to govern through institutions and elites inherited from the empire. The change looked different from one province to the next. [28] [17] [29] [14]

The removal of Romulus Augustulus in 476 marks the end of a western imperial court in Italy. The eastern court continued to rule from Constantinople, and in the sixth century it reestablished direct control in parts of the former West. The eastern state, often called Byzantium, endured through further territorial losses, recovery and a period of exile before the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453. [28] [29] [14] [30]

Roman Censuses, Citizenship & Empire-Wide Demography (550 BCE–258 CE)

Few pre-industrial states counted their human population as relentlessly as Rome. Beginning with five-year Republican lustra (used to assign tax tiers and legionary centuries), Augustus publicized three exact Imperial citizen counts in the Res Gestae (28 BCE: 4,063,000; 8 BCE: 4,233,000; 14 CE: 4,937,000), rising to 5,984,072 registered citizens under Claudius in 47/48 CE. Provincial non-citizens were tracked separately via direct provincial registrations: Syria's Apamea stele (6 CE: 117,000 cives) and over 300 surviving Egyptian 14-year household census papyri listing names, exact ages, family ties, trades, and enslaved laborers. [38] [34] [35] [36]

When Caracalla extended Roman citizenship to virtually every free resident across Europe, North Africa, and West Asia in 212 CE (Constitutio Antoniniana), the legal citizen body merged with an imperial population modern scholars estimate at roughly 50 to 70 million inhabitants, including an ~800,000–1,000,000 metropolis in Rome fed by Egyptian and African grain fleets. [37] [39]

The Imperial Road Network: 80,000+ km of Paved Trunk Highways

Beginning with the 312 BCE Via Appia ('Queen of Roads') and extending across Via Flaminia (220 BCE), Via Egnatia (146 BCE across the Balkans), Via Domitia & Via Augusta into Spain, Watling Street in Britain, and the 106–114 CE Via Traiana Nova down to the Red Sea, Rome built more than 80,000 km of graded, drained, milestone-marked paved highways supplemented by ~200,000+ km of secondary provincial feeder roads mapped by the Itiner-e 2025 project. [26] [33]

Equipped every 10–15 miles with Imperial courier relay stations (mutationes) and lodging inns (mansiones) under Augustus' cursus publicus, these roads allowed imperial dispatches to travel ~80–100 km/day while permanently anchoring the spatial urbanization footprint of medieval and modern Europe and the Mediterranean. [33] [40]
