Mesopotamia and the lives written on clay

Someone counted barley. Someone borrowed silver. A pupil copied the signs on the other side of a clay tablet. These objects record the work of people who rarely appear by name in monuments, as well as the ambitions of the merchants, officials and kings who employed scribes.

From counters and signs to writing

The earliest large group of surviving clay records in southern Mesopotamia comes from Uruk in the late fourth millennium BCE. Tablets marked with numerals and signs recorded commodities such as grain. The system is called proto-cuneiform. Many signs probably denoted goods, quantities or officials, but readings of individual signs and the language underlying the earliest records remain uncertain. One tablet attributed to Uruk appears to account for malt and barley groats. [1] [2]

Scribes increasingly impressed the tips of their styluses into moist clay, leaving the wedges that give cuneiform its name. Signs could represent words, syllables or categories such as divine names. Sumerian and Akkadian are different languages that used cuneiform. Akkadian also had varieties, including the Assyrian and Babylonian forms found in letters and records from different regions and centuries. [1] [13] [8] [19]

Counting work, equipment and food

The cities of the lower Tigris and Euphrates grew in a landscape of channels and wetlands. Their institutions collected and issued goods, and the accounts show what administrators chose to measure. One early tablet records individual allocations of copper knives. A Girsu tablet calculates categories of barley rations, including those assigned to enslaved women and children. Later accounts from Umma record barley and bread for female workers and their sons, or distinguish labour spent ploughing and harrowing fields. Such records tell us more about how institutions assigned work and provisions than about how recipients experienced it. [3] [4] [5] [6]

Work could also be counted in days. A tablet attributed to Umma calculates the time needed to clear grass and thornbush and sets barley wages for hired workers. It names a foreman and identifies a scribe through a cylinder seal. The account ties a particular field, a work schedule and a rate of pay together on a tablet that fits in one hand. [23] [24] [5]

Merchants, households and disputes

In the early second millennium BCE, merchants based in Ashur traded with communities across Anatolia. At Kaneš, a major trading centre, merchants’ archives contain loans, commercial memoranda and letters to business partners and family members. Caravans carried tin and textiles. Clay letters carried questions and orders. For some documents, seals impressed on a clay envelope identified participants or witnesses. Opening the envelope exposed the tablet inside. [8] [9]

Two merchants in a lawsuit from Kaneš each claimed a private archive and accused the other of stealing its contents. The documents and cylinder seals inside were worth fighting over, and some tablets had been entrusted to the archive by other people. A household’s clay records could establish debts, transactions and claims long after the goods had moved on. The surviving text records testimony and a request to transfer the case to Ashur. The outcome remains unknown. [9]

Centuries later, records found at Nineveh preserve other kinds of business. A delivery contract lists what is due if the recipient never receives a promised donkey mare and her young. In a separate letter, blacksmiths list what they have delivered and explain that they have run out of firewood. The contract sets terms in advance. The letter documents an obstacle the workers wanted someone in authority to address. [25] [26]

How a scribe learned

A small round exercise tablet preserves a teacher’s model on one face and a pupil’s practice on the other. More advanced exercises from Nippur and other cities included word lists, metrology and sample contracts before students copied literary compositions. Model contracts taught the language of loans, leases, marriages and other transactions. A contract on a school tablet may therefore be evidence of instruction, even when its formula resembles a real agreement. [10] [11] [12] [13]

Libraries and the survival of stories

Cuneiform preserved proverbs, incantations, reports and literature alongside everyday documents. Akkadian poems about Gilgamesh and stories about a great flood are attested in the Old Babylonian period. Much later, Ashurbanipal acquired and copied texts for royal collections at Nineveh. Short scribal notes called colophons can identify ownership or link a tablet to a particular collection. Other collections belonged to temples or private individuals. At Nineveh, nineteenth-century excavations recovered many fragments whose exact findspots were rarely recorded. [15] [16] [17] [18]

Reading the wedges again

Darius I’s inscription at Bisotun presents related accounts of his rise to power in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. In the nineteenth century, scholars including Henry Rawlinson and Edward Hincks tested readings of cuneiform against multiple texts. In 1857, a comparison of independently prepared translations of an Assyrian inscription helped establish that the methods worked. Faded signs, broken edges and uncertain names still produce different readings. A translator’s brackets often identify words lost or restored rather than intact writing. [19] [20] [17]

Clay can survive where other writing materials decay. Some archives, including houses at Kaneš, were preserved by destruction and fire. Archaeologists and conservators document findspots, photograph each surface and sometimes scan tablet fragments or sealed clay envelopes. Digital catalogues let readers trace an edition to the object and compare different readings. An image presents the marks on an object. A transliteration identifies signs using another writing system, and a translation offers an interpretation of the text. [9] [21] [22] [13]
