Late Antiquity and the Eastern Roman Empire

Ravenna, Carthage and Constantinople followed different paths through the fifth and sixth centuries. Ravenna passed from a western imperial court to Ostrogothic and eastern Roman rule. Constantinople sustained a Roman imperial government for centuries after the western court disappeared.

The third through seventh centuries CE, especially Italy, North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean, with an outline of eastern Roman continuity to 1453.

More than one imperial center

Emperors in Late Antiquity governed through different courts. Under Diocletian, a four-ruler arrangement known as the Tetrarchy distributed imperial responsibilities. Constantine later established his imperial city at Byzantium, on the Bosporus between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. Dedicated in 330 CE, Constantinople became the durable seat of the eastern Roman court. The western court established itself in Ravenna in 402. [1] [11] [4]

Christianity gained imperial support under Constantine, and later emperors strengthened its standing. Changes in official policy left time and space for considerable religious diversity. Fourth- to seventh-century communities around the Mediterranean included several Christian traditions as well as Jewish communities, and the pace of change varied across the provinces. [11] [8]

The end of the western court in Italy

The Vandals captured Carthage in 439, taking a major North African city from the western empire. In 476 the military commander Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus in Italy. Historians use 476 to mark the end of the western imperial court in Italy. Governance in the former western provinces developed through different arrangements, as rulers worked with locally rooted elites and legacies of imperial institutions. [1] [6] [14]

The Christian monuments of Ravenna span successive governments. The Mausoleum of Theodoric recalls an Ostrogothic ruler. San Vitale preserves portraits of Justinian and Theodora in mosaic from the era of restored imperial control. Their proximity makes government under different rulers visible in the same urban landscape. [4] [5]

Rome’s maritime port preserves the changing economic setting. Researchers at Portus found evidence for a decline in some North African imports after the fifth century and suggest food supplies became more reliant on sources closer to Rome. The excavations show changes in consumption over several generations, including the years after the Vandal sack of Rome in 455. [3] [1]

Justinian’s empire across the sea

In 533 Justinian launched a campaign against the Vandal kingdom in North Africa. Eastern Roman forces took Carthage and later reestablished imperial rule in Italy after prolonged war. The reconquests placed new demands on a state whose military interests also extended east. In Constantinople, construction of the present Hagia Sophia under Justinian gave the capital a new imperial church. [1] [6] [11] [12]

Justinian also ordered the assembly and revision of major collections of Roman law. The Codex arranged imperial enactments for use by the government and legal practitioners. In Ravenna a mosaic could represent the power of an emperor ruling across the Adriatic. The legal compilation shows a different kind of reach, through rules carried in texts and applied by officials. [15] [4]

Territorial change and a longer eastern history

By the start of the seventh century, Syria, Egypt and North Africa included many of the eastern empire’s wealthiest provinces. During and after the seventh-century conquests they passed under Islamic rule at different times. Objects gathered in The Met’s Age of Transition exhibition trace the continuation of trade, craft and religious life amid that change. A silver plate showing the biblical battle of David and Goliath, dated 629–630, combines a Christian subject with figures in late Roman military dress. [8] [16]

Byzantine is a modern convention for the eastern Roman Empire. Its people called themselves Romans. The imperial state centered on Constantinople continued with changing territory, a large Greek-speaking population and shifting political institutions. Crusaders took the capital in 1204. An eastern Roman government recovered it in 1261, and the Ottomans took it in 1453. [11] [17]

Roman Continuity Through 1453 CE: From Justinian to the Theodosian Walls

While Rome's Western Imperial court dissolved in 476 CE into Italy, Gaul, Hispania, and Africa successor states, citizens of Constantinople never stopped calling themselves Romaioi ('Romans') and maintaining Via Egnatia, Via Militaris, and paved post relays. Surviving the 674–718 CE Arab sieges behind the triple Theodosian Land Walls, reorganizing provinces into defensive military Themes, and fostering the Macedonian and Palaiologan renaissances preserved classical Greek-and-Roman law (Corpus Juris Civilis) unbroken until Mehmed II's heavy artillery breached Constantinople on 29 May 1453. [18] [12]
