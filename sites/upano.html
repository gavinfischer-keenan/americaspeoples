Upano

People built settlements, roads, and engineered fields in the Upano Valley. Excavation reveals local construction and occupation, while nearby lake sediments preserve cultivation and forest use. The proposed regional chronology remains disputed.

A closer reading

A landscape of platforms, gardens and roads

At Sangay, also called Huapula, platforms surround low plazas and a path connects the excavated complex with a stream. Kilamope has a different arrangement, including sunken plazas and evidence of a house on a platform. These named places give domestic and communal activities a concrete setting within the much larger landscape revealed by survey.

Evidence: doi.org [1] ↗

Lake Cormorán supplies another kind of history. Crop microfossils and vegetation change record cultivation in its catchment, with an early period and a later renewal of land use. These signals complement the excavated settlements. They show that landscape history extends beyond the famous mound-building phase, while leaving individual town chronologies and the identities of later farmers open.

Evidence: doi.org [1] ↗

Excavations in the Upano Valley uncovered earthen platforms that raised buildings above damp ground. Plazas, paths and roads connected these inhabited spaces. The later lidar survey expanded the picture from individual sites to hundreds of square kilometres, revealing thousands of platforms. These features show a landscape repeatedly shaped by construction rather than isolated buildings set within an otherwise untouched forest.

Evidence: news.cnrs.fr [1] ↗

At Copueno, a main street ran through groups of rectangular platforms arranged around lower plazas. Elsewhere, broad straight roads crossed uneven terrain and linked settlements. Cutting these routes required planning and labor beyond an individual house. Their regular layouts gave the valley a connected urban geography in which movement between neighborhoods and communities was built into the ground.

Evidence: news.cnrs.fr [1] ↗

The valley lies between mountain ranges beside the active Sangay volcano. Volcanic ash contributes to fertile soils, making agriculture part of the story of settlement as well as a source of environmental change. Rostain’s account places major occupation from around 500 BCE into the middle of the first millennium CE. Reading gardens, roads and domestic platforms together restores everyday production and movement to this early Amazonian urban history.

Sangay and Kilamope are different places

At Sangay, also called Huapula, the excavated Complex XI lies beside the Huapula stream. Platforms surround low plazas, and a path links the complex to the stream. This local arrangement sits within a much larger settlement of platform groups and streets. Source 1 ↗

At Kilamope, the excavated western complex has two sunken plazas and a central platform bounded by elongated mounds. Excavation on the southern platform revealed a housing structure. This is evidence of domestic activity at a particular centre, not merely a diffuse zone of cultural influence. Source 1 ↗

How strong is the timeline?

Rostain and colleagues proposed a sequence based on stratigraphy, pottery and radiocarbon work. A 2024 scholarly e-letter challenges consistency between the narrative and supporting chronology. The atlas retains the mapped earthworks while explicitly qualifying the dates and cultural assignments; it does not replace one uncertain sequence with another invented one. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

LiDAR describes surviving landforms. It cannot tell us by itself when each structure was built, how many people occupied every platform, what language they spoke, or whether a mapped road was active throughout a regional phase. The current movie shows named excavated centres; detailed surveyed road geometry and building-specific chronology remain expansion work. Source 1 ↗

A lake record adds a different kind of continuity

A 2025 study of Lake Cormorán identifies crop microfossils and changes in vegetation. It places local cultivation from about 570 BCE and a decline ending around 550 CE. Because the evidence comes from a lake catchment, it cannot establish that all settlements across the Upano Valley were simultaneously abandoned. Source 1 ↗

The same study identifies a later wave of land use beginning around 1500 CE. This provides evidence of activity after the famous mound-building period. It does not identify the farmers’ language, ancestry or relationship to colonial government, and its environmental signal is not an excavated town. Source 1 ↗

Keeping the wider world present

Upano’s named centers formed part of a wider Amazonian history of settlement and landscape engineering. Roads and fields connected built places, while environmental evidence records changing land use. The sequence of individual centers and the periods when particular roads were used remain incompletely dated. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
