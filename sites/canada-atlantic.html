Long coastal histories

Debert in Nova Scotia preserves an early landscape of stone-tool use. A modern calibrated synthesis places its pooled age around 12,630 calendar years before 1950, roughly 10,680 BCE. The excavations did not recover animal bones, so the familiar image of a caribou-hunting camp is an interpretation rather than a direct faunal observation there. Farther north, the Atlantic coast supported very different communities through time. At Phillip’s Garden on Newfoundland, dwellings, middens and abundant seal remains document Dorset settlement and hunting during a later sequence extending approximately from 40 BCE to 770 CE. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗

Metepenagiag in New Brunswick brings community knowledge into the history of archaeological recognition. Elder Joe Augustine remembered what earlier generations had told him about a sacred place; this helped identify the mound now bearing his name. Textiles, basketry, copper ornaments and distinctive pipes preserve ceremonial practices and distant material connections. The community’s history places the cemetery before 600 BCE, while heritage accounts also use a rounded age of 2,500 years. Neither estimate dates the beginning of Mi’kmaq presence. The nearby Oxbow settlement and the living community give the mound a broader human setting. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Voyages, fisheries and lived settlements

Norse woodcutting at L’Anse aux Meadows is securely fixed to 1021 CE by tree-ring evidence. That precise year dates an activity, not the full lifespan of the settlement or an unlimited European presence. Later Basque crews at Red Bay built a seasonal whaling industry in Labrador: ovens rendered blubber, coopers assembled barrels, and vessels carried oil to European markets. The surviving shore structures and wrecks make this work unusually tangible. Both places belong within a longer Atlantic world, whose Indigenous histories are not suspended when a European ship appears. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

French colonists established the Port-Royal habitation in Mi’kma’ki in 1605, with Mi’kmaq relationships and Membertou central to its circumstances. At Ferryland, Calvert’s plantation began in 1621 and continued as a fishing and commercial place after he left. These colonial ventures did not exhaust the island’s settlement history. Boyd’s Cove preserves Beothuk housepits attributed to roughly 1650–1720, showing an Indigenous community living through the age of expanding fisheries. Buildings, seasonal work and local knowledge followed different rhythms at each place; a colonial foundation date should not stand for the beginning of human occupation. Military ports, treaties and forced removal

Britain’s Halifax expedition entered Kjipuktuk in June 1749, bringing a fortified colonial settlement into a harbour already used by Mi’kmaq people. Diplomacy continued amid conflict. Jean-Baptiste Cope and Governor Hopson concluded the 1752 Peace and Friendship Treaty at Halifax; its written provisions addressed hunting, fishing, exchange and peace. This relationship differed from a blanket surrender of Mi’kma’ki. On Epekwitk, Skmaqn/Port-la-Joye was a meeting place where Mi’kmaq and French representatives renewed their alliance. Colonial administration and military competition grew around these existing relationships, rather than replacing Indigenous politics with an exclusively European story. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗

The fall of Fort Beauséjour on 16 June 1755 intensified British control at Chignecto. At Grand-Pré on 5 September, Acadian men and boys were confined and informed of deportation. They belonged to a society that had turned tidal marsh into farmland using dykes and wooden sluices. Removing households therefore also severed relationships to an engineered agricultural landscape. After Louisbourg fell in 1758, British authorities organized another mass removal from Île Saint-Jean, now Prince Edward Island. More than 3,000 French and Acadian inhabitants were deported; shipwreck and illness made the sea journey deadly. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗ Source 6 ↗

Continuing communities under new pressures

The effects of conquest were different for different peoples. Mi’kmaq history on Epekwitk continued after the Acadian deportation, even as British surveys failed to reserve island land for Indigenous people. Acadian return, dispersal and renewed settlements also continued beyond the departures. In Labrador, Moravian missionaries established themselves at Nain in 1771, where Inuit were already living. Nunatsiavut’s own account keeps Inuit language, work and community at the centre of that history. A mission brought new trade and religious institutions to an existing place; it did not create Inuit society or erase the people living there. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

By 1930, the colonial intervention into Indigenous family life included the opening of Shubenacadie residential school. Many Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqkew children were compelled to attend this federally funded, church-run institution. Constructed in 1928–1929, it enrolled students from 1930 and continued long after this atlas’s 1934 endpoint. The account developed with Survivors and descendants describes an assimilation project directed at cultures and identities, not simply a school building. Ending the map’s chronology in 1934 leaves that history ongoing, alongside communities whose fishing, language, kinship and cultural life persisted through profound disruption. Source 1 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

L’Anse aux Meadows — Norse woodworking in 1021 CE

Kuitems and colleagues identified the 993 CE radiocarbon signal in three different trees and counted annual rings to their outer edges. Agreement among the resulting cutting dates anchors Norse activity to 1021.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: AD 1021
Dating method: Annual tree rings matched to the 993 CE radiocarbon anomaly

This precise date does not date the settlement's founding or abandonment. It also does not show that every Norse activity occurred in that year. No arrival route is inferred from this record.

Metepenagiag — Augustine Mound ceremonial and exchange evidence

Metepenagiag’s own history places the Augustine Mound cemetery before 600 BCE. The mound preserves textiles, basketry, copper ornaments and pipes whose materials and forms connect this Mi’kmaw place to wider eastern North American networks.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Community account: cemetery dates back to before 600 BCE; official heritage account: about 2,500 years ago
Dating method: Approximate horizon matching community (<600 BCE) and heritage (~2,500 BP) accounts.

No timeline year assigned: the community source gives an open-ended date before 600 BCE, while Parks Canada gives a rounded age of about 2,500 years. Neither is silently transformed into an exact construction year.

Red Bay — Basque seasonal whaling industry

Red Bay preserves rendering ovens, cooperages, wharves, temporary quarters and wrecks from sixteenth-century Basque whaling. Whale oil was processed ashore and shipped to European markets during repeated summer expeditions.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1530s and approximately seventy years of seasonal use
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1530–1600 is a rounded display phase derived from the source’s 1530s establishment and roughly seventy years of use, not exact endpoints or permanent year-round settlement.

Port-Royal — French habitation in Mi’kma’ki

The French habitation at Port-Royal was established in 1605 in Mi’kma’ki. Building, trade and political relationships brought colonists into sustained contact with Membertou and other Mi’kmaq people.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1605, establishment of the habitation
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This dates the historic habitation, not today’s reconstructed buildings. Port-Royal’s 1605 establishment did not begin Mi’kmaq occupation of the harbour region.

Ferryland — plantation and Atlantic fishery

George Calvert established a plantation at Ferryland in 1621. The archaeological site preserves a seventeenth-century settlement tied to fishing and commerce; it remained important after Calvert’s departure.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1621, Calvert’s plantation establishment
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The date marks this plantation, not the first fishery or human presence at Ferryland. Colony of Avalon is a historical colonial designation, not an Indigenous territorial name.

Boyd’s Cove — Beothuk housepits in a changing coast

Archaeological investigations identify a Beothuk occupation at Boyd’s Cove around 1650–1720 CE. Eleven housepits are documented, with portions of five excavated and studied.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1650–1720 CE, excavated occupation attribution
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This is the dated occupation of one place, not the total duration of Beothuk history. Regional comparisons and house construction do not establish a complete population count.

Kjipuktuk / Halifax — British settlement expedition

Edward Cornwallis’s British settlement expedition arrived in Chebucto Harbour on 21 June 1749. The harbour was already a Mi’kmaq fishing and hunting place, commonly known today as Kjipuktuk.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 21 June 1749, Cornwallis expedition arrives
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The event dates a British colonial arrival, not the founding of human life at the harbour. The generalized Halifax centre does not locate the landing vessel or early fort wall.

Halifax — Cope and Hopson’s Peace and Friendship Treaty

Jean-Baptiste Cope and Governor Peregrine Hopson concluded a Peace and Friendship Treaty at Halifax in 1752. Its written provisions recognized hunting and fishing and addressed trade and peaceful relations.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 22 November 1752, treaty date in the official fact sheet
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The official fact sheet gives 22 November; some older government research says 24 November. The map uses the uncontested year. This is historical context, not a modern legal interpretation.

Fort Beauséjour — siege and surrender

British forces under Robert Monckton captured Fort Beauséjour on 16 June 1755 after a siege. Renamed Fort Cumberland, it became an instrument of British control over the Chignecto corridor.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 16 June 1755, capitulation
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This occurrence dates capitulation, not all construction or fighting. Aulac supplies coarse locality context and does not reconstruct troop positions.

Grand-Pré — confinement and deportation order

At Grand-Pré on 5 September 1755, assembled Acadian men and boys were confined and told they would be deported. Orders to military officers show the organized removal of inhabitants from the Minas district.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 5 September 1755, order read to assembled men and boys
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The date is the confinement and announcement, not a single departure day for all families. The modern village anchor is not the exact church interior or embarkation point.

Skmaqn / Port-la-Joye — Acadian deportation from Epekwitk

After the British conquest of Île Saint-Jean in 1758, officials organized the forcible removal of more than 3,000 French and Acadian inhabitants from Port-la-Joye. Shipwrecks and illness made the crossing deadly.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1758, British occupation and deportation organization
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The marker dates the deportation operation, not every ship’s departure. Epekwitk’s Mi’kmaq community history is distinct from the Acadian removal and did not end with British conquest.

Nain — Moravian mission among an existing Inuit community

Moravian missionaries established a mission at Nain in 1771, where Inuit were already living. Trade and religious institutions became part of the community’s history without creating Inuit presence there.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1771, Moravian mission establishment
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1771 dates the mission, not the foundation of Inuit society or the construction of all surviving mission buildings. The town centre is a public reference point.

Shubenacadie — residential school opens to children

Shubenacadie residential school opened to students in 1930. Many Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqkew children were compelled to attend a federally funded, church-run institution aimed at assimilation.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1930, school opens to students following construction in 1928–1929
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1930 dates opening to students; establishment and building work occurred earlier. The school continued beyond this atlas’s 1934 endpoint, so the marker must not imply closure in 1934.

Phillip’s Garden — Dorset settlement and seal hunting

Dwellings, middens and a seal-dominated faunal assemblage document Dorset use of Phillip’s Garden. Published calibrated dates place the settlement sequence at approximately 1990–1180 cal BP. Bone measurements support hunting in both early winter and spring, making this a dated local example of coastal subsistence rather than a general distribution boundary.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Hodgetts (2005), Introduction: approximately 1990 to 1180 cal BP.
Dating method: Published calibrated radiocarbon chronology from archaeological contexts, including dated middens; original calibration was not rerun.

The dates span numerous features and episodes. Individual determinations and changes in calibration can alter boundaries; no new age model is claimed. The map uses the nearby town as a public proxy. A settlement interval does not prove that the same population occupied every feature continuously.

Debert — calibrated chronology and archaeological context

Lothrop and colleagues report a pooled Debert age of 12,630 ± 135 cal BP at 2σ, distinct from the uncalibrated 10,600 ± 47 radiocarbon BP result. The early excavation account describes disturbed deposits and no recovered faunal bones; proposed caribou hunting therefore exceeds direct faunal evidence at Debert.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 12,630 ± 135 cal BP, published 2σ summary; raw pooled age 10,600 ± 47 radiocarbon BP
Dating method: Lothrop et al. Table 3: IntCal13 / OxCal 4.2 published summary; no independent recalibration

A pooled radiocarbon summary does not establish uninterrupted residence. Initial excavation interpretations and later calibrated summaries have different evidentiary roles.

New Brunswick — leaf-shaped stone points

Researchers identified long, leaf-shaped stone projectile points in two older New Brunswick collections and at one collector-reported findspot. Their shapes are characteristic of the Late Palaeoindian period.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Late Palaeoindian regional period 11,600–9000 cal yr BP
Dating method: Reviewed approximate source chronology; occurrence/context envelope, not continuous occupation

The accessible abstract does not identify all find locations or provide direct dates for the individual points. The map marker represents New Brunswick broadly.

Louisbourg — siege capitulation

The French garrison surrendered to British forces after a siege. This pin dates the capitulation, not the beginning of all fighting.

Source dates: 1758-07-26
