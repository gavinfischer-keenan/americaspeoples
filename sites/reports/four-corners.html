Four Corners Architecture and Settlement Gazetteer

Four Corners architecture records overlapping settlement histories—not a single “cliff-dweller” population or a uniform Chacoan political territory. The gazetteer below separates archaeological regions, modern administrative units, settlement clusters, individual sites, and buildings, while preserving uncertainty in dates, functions, and connections.

Reading conventions: FT = supplied full-text excerpt; A = abstract-only evidence; R = review or secondary summary; U = unsupported by readable material supplied here. “Unknown” means unavailable in this evidence set, not necessarily unknown archaeologically. Coordinates and resident population estimates are unknown for every entry below unless explicitly stated otherwise. Room counts describe architecture, never inferred residents.

4. Site Narratives and Pages

Lowry and Castle Rock

Lowry’s page should separate masonry, kiva finishes, and modern conservation. White-paint recipes changed from zinc/lead-bearing glossy layers to calcium-carbonate matte paint, documenting repainting rather than one static mural program [18.3] [18.4]. Integrated digital and hand documentation produced HABS records for stabilization and archival use [51.1] [51.2].

Castle Rock requires a settlement page and a separate community page. Recent dating challenges assumptions that surrounding small settlements necessarily preceded the center [17.3]. Mapping should connect architecture, rock art, shrines, and landforms without making every association contemporaneous [17.4]. Older Basketmaker imagery occurs within much later Pueblo III architectural settings [2.9].

Sand Canyon and Mesa Verde

Sand Canyon’s architecture supports both residence and integration, rather than a purely ceremonial or purely defensive designation [12.3]. Public buildings across neighboring communities have also supported a proposed supracommunity planning model, which should remain an attributed interpretation [52.1].

Mesa Verde’s cliff dwellings must remain embedded in their agricultural landscape. Most late regional inhabitants lived in Great Sage Plain villages and dispersed communities, not within the modern park’s famous alcoves [13.2]. Check-dam yield modeling suggests substantial household surplus potential, but those modeled yields are not measured harvests [53.1] [53.2].

Chaco and Northern Connections

Pueblo Bonito needs a phased building biography. Its chronology combines stratigraphy, wall relationships, ceramics, tree rings, radiocarbon, and archaeomagnetism, all complicated by repair and reused wood [22.3] [22.4]. Canal evidence belongs in a separate landscape-infrastructure record: the proposed system was destroyed during paleochannel entrenchment approximately 1075–1150 CE [54.1] [54.2].

Aztec, Salmon, and Chimney Rock are not interchangeable “outliers.” Middle San Juan evidence supports both migration and emulation, varying among river valleys [55.1] [55.2]. Chimney Rock comparisons distinguish the great house from nearby smaller settlements and show predominantly local ceramic procurement alongside wider exchange access [32.2].

Canyon de Chelly and Continuities

Canyon del Muerto survey connected canyon and adjacent plateau archaeology and distinguished Ancestral Pueblo and Navajo settlement models [56.1] [56.2] [56.3]. These regional findings cannot replace missing White House, Antelope House, or Mummy Cave building histories.

Homol’ovi evidence combines local production of ancestral Hopi ceramic forms with ceremonial-architecture comparisons [10.2] [10.3]. Its Hopi connections involve movement in more than one direction, not simply northern refugees arriving after 1300 [10.4].

Isotopic evidence favors local origins for most sampled Pueblo Bonito burials, but does not identify every resident’s birthplace or language [62.1]. Architectural similarity likewise cannot establish ancient spoken identity. More than 20 contemporary Pueblos and tribes claim affiliation with Chaco’s wider world, making exclusive attribution inappropriate [27.5].

Migration pathways include longstanding social relationships across complementary rainfall regimes and successive settlement in intermediate areas, not just single arrows from origin to destination [9.2] [63.1]. Rio Grande ceramic continuity coexisted with later plaza-oriented town formation and organizational change [11.2] [11.3].

6. Gaps and Source Limits

The largest limitation is building-level source access, especially for Hovenweep, individual Mesa Verde dwellings, and Canyon de Chelly. Modern digital documentation is strong, but cannot independently supply missing occupation sequences, ancient populations, or functional identifications [64.1] [51.3].

For reusable map records, store separate construction, occupation, remodeling, closure, and later-use events, each with its own method, source-access status, and uncertainty. Preserve original fabric versus modern intervention: historical Mesa Verde presentation and restoration affected how building plans and surviving surfaces were perceived [65.1] [65.2]. Condition surveys and conservation records therefore belong alongside archaeological plans [8.2].

Additional relevant coverage remains subregional rather than settlement-specific: Cedar Mesa mural dating identifies twelfth–thirteenth-century communities of practice; northwestern Chaco borderland research questions close material connections to the canyon core; and Grand Canyon settlement research distinguishes Virgin, Kayenta, and Cohonina traditions [66.1] [67.1] [68.1]. These findings should prevent oversimplified cultural polygons, not fill absent site dates.

Bottom line: the supplied evidence supports a substantial phased gazetteer, but not a complete building-by-building settlement map through 1500 CE. Unknown dates, coordinates, populations, and connections should remain visible research fields rather than reconstructed certainties.
