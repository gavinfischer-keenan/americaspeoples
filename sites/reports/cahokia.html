Cahokia and Mississippian Urban Networks

Cahokia and the American Bottom supported a changing constellation of settlements, monuments, farming communities, and ceremonial connections between roughly 500 and 1600 CE—not one homogeneous people or demonstrably unified Mississippian empire. The review below separates physical remains, chronological models, and interpretations, while identifying where the supplied evidence cannot support atlas-level precision.

Access notation: FT means substantive full-text excerpts are supplied; A means findings are available only through an abstract; R means a review or secondary summary. Planning and labor. Building one-quarter to one-third of the Grand Plaza potentially required more than 10,000 person-days; this is a labor estimate, not a population count [11.4]. Regional clustering and intervisibility support coordinated placement, but the authors present shared decision-making as an interpretation rather than proof of a particular government [28.1] [28.2].

Neighborhoods and storage. Architectural diversity cautions against treating every building concentration as residential: some zones contained specialized craft or religious architecture [29.1]. Household research explicitly includes storage/processing pits, debris distributions, and domestic–ceremonial arrangements, but the supplied passages cannot establish capacities, citywide granary totals, or centralized surplus administration [30.1] [30.2].

East St. Louis. The compound burned in one conflagration; ritual commemoration, aggression, and accident remain alternatives, with circumstantial evidence favoring ritual commemoration [31.3]. Subsequent reductions in construction place the event within a wider transformation, not an independently demonstrated military destruction of Greater Cahokia [31.4].

St. Louis. Surviving plats, lithographs, and drawings permit probable mound-location reconstruction, but celestial interpretations remain a separate analytical step [32.3]. Big Mound’s copper long-nosed mask supplies an artifact connection rather than a political boundary [37.1].

Mitchell. The preserved post base lay in an extraction ramp within a four-meter-deep pit [9.4]. A 1124 cutting date fits best, but 1122–1126 and 1129–1132 cannot be statistically excluded; neither cutting nor inferred later removal directly dates mound construction [9.5] [9.6].

Emerald. Ceramic and architectural evidence identifies at least five short occupations around 1020–1200, including rebuilding associated with approximately 1050 [38.1] [38.2]. Short-term domiciles, shrines, feasting, and renewed monuments underpin the pilgrimage interpretation; lunar-standstill scheduling is a further hypothesis [38.3] [38.4].

Washausen. Bayesian modeling suggests major occupation lasted potentially thirty years or less, with rapid departure beginning around 1100 or shortly afterward [39.1] [39.2]. Its contraction while larger centers approached population maxima argues against mapping all regional sites as simultaneously flourishing [39.3].

Kane and rural communities. Legacy analyses resolve occupation and mortuary histories extending from approximately 500 BCE into the fourteenth century, including local people and immigrants [40.1]. Rural planning has been interpreted as comparatively logistical and autonomous, illustrating disagreement with strongly centralized regional models [30.5] [30.6].

Moundville. Augering located dense debris between plaza and palisade, while residences also occurred outside the fortifications [41.4] [41.5]. Minor mounds include summit architecture, accumulated domestic deposits, and differing mortuary histories [49.1] [49.2] [49.3]. Fifteenth-century earth-lodge evidence demonstrates continuing architectural activity after the town-to-necropolis transformation [50.1].

Spiro. Craig Mound’s regalia supports sustained ritual interaction, but competing interpretations dispute whether particular shell and copper traditions originated at Cahokia or within Spiro-centered networks [10.2] [23.2]. Dental evidence suggests a distinctive, incompletely maize-dependent subsistence trajectory [45.3]. Aztalan. Modeling uses 68 Aztalan determinations alongside 52 from nearby Lake Koshkonong settlements [6.3]. Prolonged bastioned fortification supports a contested multiethnic landscape, while Oneota occupation continued nearby after Aztalan’s Mississippian occupation ended [6.4].

Etowah. A defensible entry here is necessarily limited to location and participation in comparative fortification chronology; detailed Mounds A–C plans, population figures, and construction sequences remain unverified.

Agriculture was diverse: native seed crops remained important alongside maize, and reviewed archaeobotanical syntheses challenge maize-only explanations [63.1] [64.1]. Conversely, directly dated maize and human/dog isotope evidence supports subsistence-level maize introduction around 900 and rapid subsequent expansion [65.1] [65.2]. These positions differ principally over timing, relative dietary weight, and causal importance—not whether maize existed.

Feasting deposits reveal collective participation through food, pottery, craft debris, and valued objects [26.2]. Shell-workshop distributions and labor estimates support specialized production, although individual beads cannot be assigned securely to specific workshops [16.3] [16.4].

Religion, Language, and Persistence

Ritual interpretations include pilgrimage, public posts, mortuary transformation, and celestial landscapes, but specific deity identifications require qualification [38.5] [9.10]. Milky Way and solar/lunar alignment arguments combine landscape measurement with ethnographic analogy; they are interpretations, not surviving Cahokian doctrinal texts [24.3] [66.1].

Picture Cave research incorporates conversations with Dhegiha-speaking elders, including Osage, Quapaw, and Kansa, and interprets imagery through southern Siouan traditions [67.1] [67.2]. This does not supply a site-wide linguistic identification for Cahokia; the available evidence supports neither one ancient language nor one homogeneous ethnicity.

One-third of sampled individuals in the strontium study were nonlocal, with multiple apparent origins; that is a sampled mortuary result, not an exact immigrant proportion for the entire city [68.1]. Isotopic provenance is not genomics, and neither should be presented as Indigenous political identity. Descendant-community collaboration and sovereignty should govern heritage interpretation rather than archaeological classification alone [69.1].

Later occupation is especially vulnerable to diagnostic-artifact, sedimentation, access, and research-focus biases [13.5] [13.6]. Modern remote sensing also detects demolished modern houses, so anomalies require historical discrimination rather than automatic Mississippian attribution [76.1]. Radiocarbon plateaus further limit precision around 1480–1630, even with Bayesian modeling [77.1].

Atlas takeaway: represent Cahokia as a changing urban and ceremonial landscape within diverse regional networks. Map documented features separately from proposed routes, inferred affiliations, and political hypotheses—and describe depopulation as transformation within continuing Indigenous histories, not disappearance.
