Moche: Huacas de Moche and a long-lived urban landscape

Follow particular north-coast communities and construction contexts, rather than assigning every place to one uniform Moche state or one shared ending.

A closer reading

An urban history beyond the temple façades

Food remains in Architectural Compound 9 connect Huacas de Moche with the coast. Clams, hake and other marine resources reached an urban setting of kitchens, storage rooms and residential spaces. These deposits make eating and preparation part of the city’s history, alongside the large temples that dominate its surviving appearance.

Evidence: academia.edu [1] ↗ · pure.psu.edu [2] ↗

The monumental setting also changed. An Old Temple and a later New Temple belong to different phases, while superposed floors and craft areas reveal transformations throughout the settlement. Later residential contraction did not end every activity: funerary and ritual use continued in parts of the older landscape. The city’s history includes reuse as well as growth.

Settlement before the monumental city

Huacas de Moche has a history longer than the monumental Moche phases that make the place famous. Rengifo and colleagues identify early settlement evidence on Cerro Blanco, including components designated PCB 1 and PCB 2 and stone-built rectilinear spaces. Their synthesis places Salinar within a broad regional phase of about 400 BCE to AD 50. That range describes an archaeological tradition and context, not the continuous life of one excavated house. The same study distinguishes the absence of diagnostic Gallinazo negative-painted pottery at Huacas proper from Gallinazo evidence at nearby Huaca Las Estrellas. Early inhabitants therefore belong in the story, with differences between neighboring places preserved rather than folded into an uninterrupted generic occupation. Source 1 ↗

400 BCE is a broad phase anchor; integer -399 uses astronomical numbering. Source 1 ↗

Marine foods in an urban household compound

Architectural Compound 9 connects the city to daily preparation, storage and eating. The studied complex covered about 656 square meters and contained 48 spaces with residential, kitchen, storage and burial functions. Roselló and colleagues assigned its sampled contexts to AD 470–600 using the chronology available in 2001. Donax clams and hake dominate the marine assemblage, alongside many less common taxa. Those remains show that coastal resources reached an urban community, while the authors’ proposals about selection and redistribution remain interpretations of the pattern. The dates here are the study’s contextual chronology, not newly calibrated measurements; the original radiocarbon BP ages must not be converted to calendar years by simple subtraction. A new temple and a changing urban sequence

The site synthesis distinguishes the Old Temple from a New Temple phase placed around AD 700–850. This was a landscape repeatedly transformed: superposed urban floors and contexts of craft and food preparation record changes beyond the monumental façades. A recent textile study summarizes the Old Temple’s cessation closer to 650, illustrating why one exact rebuilding date would be misleading. Both sources point to architectural change, but their broad summaries and chronological purposes differ. Later residential contraction around 850 also does not mean every activity stopped. The synthesis identifies subsequent funerary and ritual reuse, including architectural compounds 45 and 47 and parts of Huaca del Sol, preserving the distinction between a changed city and an empty site. Textiles extend the history of Moche practice

Textiles from Huaca del Sol’s Section 4 reveal a later history than older narratives of abrupt Moche disappearance allowed. Twenty AMS measurements on selected pieces from an assemblage of more than 200 fragments concentrate in the tenth century, with some distributions extending across AD 1000. Techniques and imagery include locally S-spun cotton Moche forms, Highland Moche combinations, Moche-Wari forms and Wari material. These are useful categories for practices and objects, not direct labels for each person’s ethnicity or language. Dates measure fibre growth rather than death or burial; reopened tombs and possible heirlooms complicate deposition. The evidence nevertheless supports continued, changing traditions in a place whose major urban and temple phases had already shifted. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

950 anchors the tenth-century concentration; individual calibrated ranges and burial histories differ. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Chimú families use the older sacred landscape

Later cemetery evidence shows that the significance of Huacas de Moche did not end with the contraction of its Moche-period city. Rengifo and colleagues assign an Early Chimú group of 25 individuals to approximately AD 1100–1250, supported by two radiocarbon determinations. This does not date every person to the same moment, but it establishes another phase of funerary use in the inherited landscape. The authors distinguish a subsequent Middle Chimú group of eighteen burials, dated relatively rather than by radiocarbon. Keeping those methods separate matters: an archaeological phase can organize the narrative without creating laboratory precision that the evidence does not have. The continuity is one of place and practice, not proof that political institutions or personal identities remained unchanged. Source 1 ↗

1150 is a contextual anchor within the 1100–1250 phase supported by two dates. Source 1 ↗

Late Chimú activity in an enduring place

A hearth in Architectural Compound 43 supplies a specific late Chimú observation within this long-lived landscape. The published charcoal determination has a calibrated component of AD 1390–1451 representing 90.2 percent probability; it is not the complete 95.4 percent distribution, nor a direct date for every activity in the compound. The broader site synthesis also describes late offerings involving miniature objects, Spondylus and feathered garments, followed by evidence associated with the Chimú-Inca period. These different contexts show continued use but must not be merged into one ceremony. Nearby Huanchaquito-Las Llamas is a separate archaeological locality, and no evidence here makes its mass killing an event inside Compound 43 or identifies the hearth’s particular users. Source 1 ↗

1420 anchors a reported 1390–1451 calibrated component; probability and charcoal-context caveats remain explicit. Source 1 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Valley-specific Moche chronologies remain beyond what can be established from the available abstract of Koons’s 2024 study.

Rengifo places the Old Temple in 50–700 and the New Temple in 700–850. Quilter instead summarizes major building in the third and fourth centuries and a transition around 650. These accounts do not establish one agreed rebuilding year.

The dated textiles concentrate in the tenth century, with some possible sample dates extending beyond 1000. Their distributions do not establish a single ending year for textile use or deposition.

Fibre-growth dates, reopened tombs and possible heirlooms do not give exact deaths or a single simultaneous cemetery event. Textile categories are not direct ethnic/language assignments.

CA 43 and CA 42 reported calibrated components carry 90.2% and 84%, not complete 95.4% distributions.

Some official visitor descriptions differ from the primary studies on dimensions, temple names, and the extent of Gallinazo occupation. Those differences remain unresolved.

A wooden-model burial has a broad date range of 1449–1648, spanning periods before and after Spanish arrival. Elsewhere at the site, later activity continued after residential contraction around 850.

The location represents the area containing both huacas, with an approximate two-kilometer radius. It does not pinpoint an excavated room or deposit.
