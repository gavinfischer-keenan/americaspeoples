A river valley inhabited before the chronicles

The St. Lawrence connected coasts, tributaries and interior routes long before a colonial city stood beside it. At Rimouski, projectile points, bifaces and drills preserve early stone-working activities; at Kruger 2 beside the Saint-François River, numerous points, fragments and unfinished pieces show another working place. These assemblages do not identify every ancient language or nation. They do establish a long human history in landscapes reorganized after the ice retreated. The later settlements encountered by French travellers grew within an inhabited northeast, not at the edge of a human absence. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

By the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries, the Saint-Anicet area held successive St. Lawrence Iroquoian villages. McDonald contained at least three longhouses and three middens; Droulers was substantially larger, with seven excavated longhouses in the study consulted here; Mailhot-Curran represents a later settlement. Bone needles, awls, harpoon heads, projectile points and manufacturing waste make skilled work visible. These were communities supported by maize, beans and squash alongside fishing, hunting and gathering. Changing village locations therefore need to be understood through households, food and regional relationships, rather than as a sequence of empty archaeological dots. Source 1 ↗

Villages, visitors and colonial footholds

Cartier’s 1535 visit to Hochelaga encountered a settlement where people offered fish, beans and bread and exchanged gifts. At Stadacona, Donnacona showed the visitors houses supplied for winter. Communication depended partly on Taignoagny and Dom Agaya, whom Cartier had earlier taken to France. Their experience makes clear that exchange and coercion could occur within the same encounter. The village evidence also matters for food history: farming did not make fishing disappear. Recent residue research at Montréal’s Dawson site independently examines the combined cooking of aquatic foods and cultivated plants in pottery. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗

In 1541, Cartier attempted a colonial establishment at Cap-Rouge near Stadacona. Later French projects depended on another political setting downstream. At Pointe aux Alouettes on 27 May 1603, Innu leader Anadabijou received Champlain and Gravé du Pont with Anishinabeg and Etchemin allies. Tadoussac was already a trading and meeting place; the French entered those networks through negotiation. Québec’s establishment in 1608 and Ville-Marie’s in 1642 then created enduring colonial centres. These dates identify French projects, while Indigenous travel, diplomacy and community presence continued around and within the growing towns. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

New communities and the work of peace

Warfare and displacement in Wendat country did not end Huron-Wendat history. After movements through other settlements, the community established itself at present-day Wendake in 1697. Its history connects the St. Lawrence to the Great Lakes, yet Wendake also became a place with its own continuing life. Montréal likewise served as a diplomatic destination rather than only a colonial military centre. In 1701, Indigenous delegations from many nations negotiated with French authorities. The Great Peace, concluded on 4 August, owed much to Wendat leader Kondiaronk and to the political work of the participating nations. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Peace agreements did not end every danger. Odanak was an Abenaki community shaped partly by refugees from conflicts farther south. On 4 October 1759, Robert Rogers’s Rangers attacked and burned the settlement, killing inhabitants, including civilians remembered in community accounts. A wooden church was rebuilt the following year, one sign that destruction was followed by reconstruction. The same war brought the British victory at the Plains of Abraham in 1759 and a French victory at Sainte-Foy in 1760. Military outcomes and the subsequent change of imperial rule transformed the political setting without erasing Indigenous communities. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Colonial land administration and continuing homelands

Nineteenth-century reserves imposed administrative boundaries on older relationships to land. At Kitigan Zibi, a Crown Lands distribution list dated 8 June 1853 proposed about 45,750 acres in Maniwaki township for Indigenous residents along the Desert River. That bureaucratic step should not be read as the birth of the Anishinabe community. Families’ connections followed rivers, harvesting places and kinship beyond a surveyed block. The legal-survey record documents what officials allotted; it provides a narrower kind of evidence than the full history of people who already belonged to the region. Source 1 ↗

At Pointe-Bleue, now Mashteuiatsh, Pekuakamiulnuatsh chose in 1856 to exchange two previously reserved tracts for land in Ouiatchouan township. Their own history situates this decision within pressure on land and efforts to preserve a viable community. The lakeshore had long been a gathering place. Later reductions of reserved land and settlement schemes changed its shape without making the reserve identical to the wider Innu homeland. By 1934, Québec contained colonial cities, industries and imposed institutions alongside enduring Wendat, Abenaki, Anishinabe and Innu communities. Each history requires places and dates of its own. Saint-Anicet — McDonald longhouses and bone tools

The McDonald village contained at least three longhouses and three middens. A primary study attributes it to approximately 1335–1380 CE and reports 438 bone tools, alongside production waste and animal remains.

Tsiionhiakwatha / Droulers — longhouses and worked bone

A primary bone-artifact study places Droulers around 1490–1515 CE and describes seven excavated longhouses, two middens, and a large worked-bone assemblage. It proposes aggregation of smaller communities as one explanation for the village’s size.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1490–1515 CE, 2021 study; visitor centre uses circa 1450
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The visitor centre gives circa 1450; the newer research estimate is displayed explicitly rather than blended into an invented range. Seven excavated houses do not prove all proposed fifteen houses.

Cap-Rouge — Charlesbourg-Royal establishment

On 23 August 1541, Cartier established Charlesbourg-Royal near the confluence of the Cap Rouge and St. Lawrence rivers, close to Stadacona. This was an attempted French colonial foothold within an Indigenous-inhabited river valley.

Pointe aux Alouettes — Anadabijou’s alliance gathering

At Pointe aux Alouettes near Tadoussac, Innu leader Anadabijou received Champlain and Gravé du Pont on 27 May 1603 amid a gathering with Anishinabeg and Etchemin allies. The encounter established a French alliance within existing Indigenous diplomacy.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 27 May 1603
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The marker is Tadoussac as a nearby orientation point; the meeting took place across the Saguenay mouth. An alliance is not evidence that Indigenous participants surrendered all their lands.

Wendake — establishment of the enduring community

The Huron-Wendat community established itself at the present Wendake locality in 1697. Its own public history presents this settlement as part of an ongoing civilization and community, not a new people created by colonial settlement.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1697, community establishment at Wendake
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1697 dates establishment at this locality, not the beginning of Huron-Wendat history or the arrival of every family. No single route from Huronia is reconstructed.

Odanak — Rogers’ raid and rebuilding

Rangers under Robert Rogers attacked Odanak on 4 October 1759, killing inhabitants and burning the fortified Abenaki community. Community accounts remember women, children and elders among those harmed.

Dates and evidence notes

Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

Casualty figures differ and none is asserted here. Destruction of the village is not the extinction of its people; the community’s history continues through rebuilding.

Mashteuiatsh — Pointe-Bleue reserve exchange

In 1856, Pekuakamiulnuatsh exchanged the two previously designated reserve tracts for one at Ouiatchouan, including Pointe-Bleue, now Mashteuiatsh. Community history describes the new tract as 23,040 acres.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1856, exchange for the Ouiatchouan / Pointe-Bleue reserve
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This is a change in colonial land administration at an older Innu gathering place. It does not delimit Tshitassinu or establish that all families immediately became sedentary.

Rimouski — stone points, bifaces and drills

At the Rimouski site in Québec, the published inventory lists 13 stone projectile points, 77 bifaces (stones shaped on both faces), and seven drills. The tools are studied as evidence of Late Paleoindian activity.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Paleoindian classification; no direct calibrated date adopted
Dating method: Editorial period guide; approximate context only

The inventory does not establish how many people used the site. Earlier dating reviews questioned material mixed within a dated feature, so the broad display range is not a direct site date.

Kruger 2 — stone points and unfinished tools

At Kruger 2 in Brompton, Québec, archaeologists recovered 129 stone projectile points and point fragments, plus 14 unfinished points. These shaped stone tips occur in different styles at this site beside the Saint-François River.

Hochelaga — food, gifts and communication during Cartier’s visit

In 1535, Cartier’s party visited Hochelaga in the Montréal area. The voyage account describes being welcomed in an open square, receiving woven mats, exchanging gifts and being offered fish, beans and bread. It also preserves words and phrases attributed to Hochelaga and Canada.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: October 1535 CE encounter
Dating method: Historical document, manuscript, monument or encounter date as reported by the cited source. This is not a first date of speech or a census of speakers.

The description comes from the visiting Europeans’ account and reflects their interpretations. The modern commemorative marker does not identify an excavated village footprint.

Stadacona — interpreters and aid during Cartier’s winter

At Stadacona near present-day Québec City, Donnacona received Cartier’s party and showed houses stocked for winter. Taignoagny and Dom Agaya helped communication after Cartier had taken them captive to France in 1534. The expedition returned with them in 1535 and wintered nearby.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1535–1536 CE second voyage; October 1535 visit documented
Dating method: Historical document, manuscript, monument or encounter date as reported by the cited source. This is not a first date of speech or a census of speakers.

These events survive in the expedition’s account. Its sweeping claims about the remedy reflect the narrator’s interpretation; this record does not identify the tree species.
