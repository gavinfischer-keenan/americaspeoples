Iliuliuk and Unalaska

The U.S. Coast Survey chart issued in 1875 shows Iliuliuk as a strip of buildings between steep ground and the water. A church appears among them. The chart records the village as surveyors found it in 1871 and 1872, during one period in a much longer Unangan history on Unalaska and the surrounding islands.

Reading the 1875 chart

Find the name Iliuliuk on the lower right of the chart. On the shore beside it, surveyors marked a church and a line of small building symbols. Other symbols follow the shore around the point. Close together on the page are two kinds of marks used for different purposes. Numbers in the water give measured depths in fathoms. Marks on shore help identify recognizable places. The survey made the harbor legible for mariners while leaving the names and activities of most households outside its purpose. Source 1 ↗

Across the water, the chart names Amaknak Island and locates an astronomical station used for the survey. Expedition Island lies within the harbor. The detail in the water reflects the time surveyors spent measuring it, including observations of tides described in the margin. The true north and magnetic variation markings help a reader orient the sheet. As a source for the nineteenth-century settlement, its strength is the relationship between shoreline, church and other visible buildings. The individually unnamed symbols cannot be matched to the households that lived and worked near the shore. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

An earlier house on another bay

Reese Bay, west of Unalaska Bay, preserves evidence of coastal households from the early Russian period. Archaeologists mapped two large longhouses, smaller house depressions and older deposits. Longhouse 2 had a main floor extending about thirty metres east to west with smaller spaces off it. Archaeologists excavated part of it and recovered more than two thousand glass beads. Their size, shapes and colors helped place the use of that house in the early contact period, when glass beads had become part of exchanges between Unangan people and Russian traders. Source 1 ↗

Different parts of the floor yielded different concentrations of beads. The interpretation has to account for uneven excavation and for the fact that only part of the house was examined with water screening. Beads lost near a family work area or a well-lit entrance might remain in a floor, while cleaning could move others away. Historical accounts describe related families occupying shared houses and using spaces along their sides. The Reese Bay excavation makes the scale and some of the interior arrangement visible. The exact allocation of space among its residents remains uncertain. Source 1 ↗

Three generations of the Holy Ascension church

The first Holy Ascension church was built in 1826, in an existing community. Construction took place during Ioann Veniaminov’s years of missionary work in the region, when the church became associated with local teaching and the writing and translation of Unangam Tunuu. The shoreline village around the church provided the setting for both the new religious institution and continuing Unangan households. The earlier Reese Bay longhouse belongs to a different village and period, and offers another example of the skill and organization that people brought to building at the water’s edge. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

A second church followed in 1858. It is the building phase that fits the church symbol on the Coast Survey chart. The chart was drawn from observations more than twenty years before construction of the standing church in 1894–1896. The later builders incorporated timber from earlier churches. Its cross-shaped building plan, bell tower and side chapels express a different spatial organization, even as reused material tied the building to its predecessors. The survey, building history and surviving building each give a different view of the village as it changed. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

A settlement linked by water

William H. Dall’s field notes record sea otter, fox and seal skins traded at Iliuliuk from a number of islands, including Akun and Tigalda. His observations place the houses shown on the survey in a network of trade, transport and work. The same harbor that protected boats on their approach to the shore linked residents to more distant harvests and markets. Institutions visible to surveyors occupied only some of the waterfront. Its fisheries, small craft, landings and individual households matter to the history even where the survey leaves them unnamed. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The dates help hold these layers apart. Reese Bay’s beads describe one excavated contact-era house. The church records describe several generations of construction at Iliuliuk. The Coast Survey describes the harbor as it was observed in the early 1870s. Taken together, they show how a settlement can keep its relationship to water while buildings, materials and kinds of work change. Neither the church nor the harbor chart supplies a founding date for Unalaska’s Unangan community. Their more direct evidence is the location and form of nineteenth-century buildings within a much older inhabited landscape. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

Reese Bay — a longhouse and its side rooms

An excavation at Reese Bay documented two large multifamily houses, smaller house depressions and an older midden. Longhouse 2 extended about 30 metres along its east–west axis and had side spaces off a shared floor.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximate second half of the eighteenth century
Dating method: Relative dating of historic glass beads and historical evidence of early Russian contact.

Excavation covered part of the house and was uneven across the floor. The bay point indicates the larger locality. The occupation window is broad.

Iliuliuk — the village on the Coast Survey chart

The Coast Survey chart marks a church and small building symbols in a narrow strip beside the bay at Iliuliuk, now Unalaska. The village occupied the shoreline below a steep hill, close to the channel and anchorage.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Surveyed in 1871 and 1872; chart issued July 1875
Dating method: Dated inscription on the U.S. Coast Survey chart.

A harbor chart documents features visible and useful to surveyors. Building symbols identify neither households nor complete building footprints. The church shown predates the standing Holy Ascension church.

Unalaska — construction of the first Holy Ascension church

Local builders working with Ioann Veniaminov constructed the first Church of the Holy Ascension in 1826. Mission teaching and translation took place in an existing Unangan community; this is a building date, not the arrival of people at Unalaska.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1826 CE
Dating method: Dated historical account in the cited source.

The 1826 church was replaced in 1858. Veniaminov’s 1824 arrival is already represented separately in the atlas.

Unalaska — the church is rebuilt

A second Holy Ascension church replaced the original building in 1858. Its timbers would later be reused in the larger church built during the 1890 s.

Unalaska — building the present Holy Ascension church

The present Holy Ascension church was built between 1894 and 1896, incorporating timbers from its predecessors. Its cruciform plan, chapels, tower and Unangan craftsmanship make successive generations of work visible in one building.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Construction 1894–1896 CE
Dating method: Dated historical account in the cited source.

The interval dates construction, not the start or end of Unangan life or Orthodox belief.

Amaknak Bridge — stone houses and elaborate hearths

On a knoll above the channel, builders terraced stone houses, repaired walls and laid new floors and hearths across older ones. Most houses were round or oval, about 3–4 metres across. A few were larger and rectangular.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Published charcoal-date envelope 3922–2358 cal BP; approximately 1973–409 BCE
Dating method: Outer envelope of calibrated charcoal ranges reported by Rogers; not a new pooled radiocarbon model.

The envelope spans samples from different contexts. It does not date every structure or establish continuous occupation throughout the whole range.
