Shorelines and homes before the trading posts

The Pacific coast opens with people’s footsteps. At Calvert Island, researchers identified 29 impressions made by at least three people, including a juvenile. Pine wood associated with the track surface places this episode between 13,317 and 12,633 calibrated years before 1950. The impressions preserve a brief encounter between feet and wet ground; they cannot tell us how long those people stayed. The setting lies within Heiltsuk and Wuikinuxv homelands, where changing shorelines have shaped which early traces survive and where archaeologists can find them. Source 1 ↗

Farther south, people remade intertidal beaches. On northern Quadra Island, rock walls retained sediment and created flatter terraces suitable for clams. Research in Kanish and Waiatt bays places some gardens at least 3,500 years ago, with subsequent work extending the construction history to at least 3,800 years. These structures belong to Laich-Kwil-Tach and northern Coast Salish homelands. They show food production through close knowledge of tides, shellfish and sediment, rather than a simple division between farming societies and people who merely collected wild foods. Villages, households and ocean connections

In the interior, Keatley Creek’s housepits preserve another form of settled life near the Fraser River. A published radiocarbon model places the aggregated village approximately 250–1150 CE, with interruptions inside that span. Its large houses and household deposits have prompted debate about how people lived together and how inequalities developed. Brian Hayden’s reading of the dates differs from that model. The disagreement concerns particular structures and sequences: the evidence for substantial houses is firmer than a single, uncontested story of when the whole village grew or declined. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The coast also contained large wooden houses, public art and centres of authority. SG̱ang Gwaay preserves nineteenth-century Haida cedar-house remains and carved poles within a place of continuing cultural importance. At Yuquot, Mowachaht leaders dealt with ocean-going traders and competing European powers in the late eighteenth century. Yuquot was a centre of the sea-otter trade in 1785–1795 and the setting for the Vancouver–Quadra negotiations of 1792. These were encounters at established communities whose political and ceremonial lives cannot be reduced to the visiting ships. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Commerce becomes colonial government

Fort Langley, established by the Hudson’s Bay Company in 1827, brought a different commercial presence to the lower Fraser. Its business included salmon, agricultural produce and overseas shipments as well as furs. The fort’s later moves, rebuilding and changing economic role matter: a modern reconstructed enclosure is not the original 1827 post. Across the river and surrounding valley, Kwantlen and other Stó:lō communities remained essential to the region’s economy. The proclamation of the mainland colony at the fort in 1858 connected commercial infrastructure with a new form of colonial authority. Source 1 ↗

In Tŝilhqot’in country, expansion brought armed conflict. During the 1864 war, colonial officials invited leaders into discussions that were presented as peace negotiations. Five war chiefs were subsequently imprisoned, tried and executed at Quesnel on 26 October. Tŝilhqot’in histories remember their defence of land and people, and later provincial statements acknowledge the wrongful hangings. Ahan’s execution at New Westminster in 1865 was a separate event. Keeping the places and dates distinct makes responsibility clearer than treating the entire conflict as one undifferentiated frontier episode. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Children, ceremony and continuing communities

By 1894, St. Michael’s at Alert Bay appears in the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation’s register as an operating Anglican residential school. A school is a different kind of historical place from a village or trading station: its institutional presence signals intervention in children’s lives and community relationships. The register supplies a local chronology for an institution whose effects reached across families and generations. Its operation continued far beyond this atlas’s 1934 endpoint, which is a limit of the historical view rather than a closing date. Source 1 ↗

At ʼMimkwa̱mlis on Village Island, Dan Cranmer hosted a major potlatch in 1921 despite Canada’s ban. Prosecution and the confiscation of regalia followed, sending treasured belongings away from their owners. U’mista’s account explains that songs, dances and masks carried histories and obligations; taking them was an intrusion into how communities transmitted knowledge and authority. The gathering therefore belongs beside houses and food-producing landscapes in this regional history. It also prevents a story ending with colonial institutions from obscuring the people who continued to hold and practise their traditions. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Keatley Creek — housepit village and a debated chronology

Keatley Creek preserves large housepits and household deposits. Prentiss and colleagues place the aggregated village approximately 250–1150 CE, with interruptions within that span.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: c.1700–800 cal BP, Prentiss et al. 2003 model
Dating method: 1950 minus published calibrated-BP endpoints; dates retained from the named model.

A model envelope containing a break around 500–600 CE and a possible later break; not continuous habitation. Hayden offers a different reading of the house and date sequences.

Fort Langley — Hudson’s Bay Company post established

The Hudson’s Bay Company established Fort Langley on the lower Fraser in 1827. The post became involved in salmon packing, farming and Pacific exports as well as the fur trade.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1827
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

This dates the original establishment. The fort subsequently moved and was rebuilt; the modern town reference does not mark the 1827 building footprint.

Quesnel — execution of five Tŝilhqot’in war chiefs

Five Tŝilhqot’in war chiefs were executed at Quesnel after entering colonial custody during the 1864 war. Their nation commemorates them as leaders defending their homeland.

Calvert Island — human footprints in coastal sediment

McLaren and colleagues report 29 human footprints representing at least three people, including a juvenile, on Calvert Island. The conservative published envelope is 13,317–12,633 cal BP; narrower rounded wording elsewhere in the paper should not replace that full envelope.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 13,317–12,633 cal BP, conservative associated-wood chronology
Dating method: Associated pine wood; Calib 7.0.2 / IntCal13 reported by McLaren et al.

This is Pacific coastal presence. It is not evidence for the route into Atlantic Canada, uninterrupted coastal movement, or a village population.

Northern Quadra Island — early clam-garden construction

Rock walls and terraces engineered productive clam habitat. Radiocarbon and sea-level evidence places some garden construction at least about 3500 years ago.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: At least approximately 3500 years ago
Dating method: Approximate 3500-year age from calibrated radiocarbon and sea-level interpretation; 1950 BP base used for map placement.

This approximate earliest reference is not the date of every wall. The much longer history of human–clam relations must not be substituted for construction age.
