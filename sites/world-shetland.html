Shetland’s early farms, standing stones and brochs

The low banks at Scord of Brouster, the massive stone at Bordastubble and the tower at Mousa tell stories separated by centuries and sometimes millennia. Shetland’s islands preserve evidence of coastal food gathering, farming, stone quarrying, burial and later drystone construction. The map links places for comparison across the archipelago.

This guide follows Shetland’s earliest dated coastal deposits, Neolithic and Bronze Age fields, burials and stones, then selected Iron Age and Norse settlements on and around Mainland.

From the coastal middens to the Norse farmstead

Coastal deposits contain some of the earliest dated traces of human activity in Shetland. They preserve shells, animal remains and, in later deposits, pottery. On shore and farther inland, other sites yield house floors, burial deposits and the waste left by stone tool making. Since each site preserves a different part of the record, the best dated layers can be centuries away from the visible stone buildings. [1] [2] [4] [11]

Houses, fields and the Stanydale hall

At Scord of Brouster the field banks and heaps of stone cleared from cultivation sit beside oval houses. Excavation showed that the houses do not form a single building episode. Some traces are Neolithic and much of the dated settlement belongs later. At nearby Stanydale, a large heel-shaped building shares aspects of local houses and burial cairns. The size, central hearth and unusual contents of the excavated structure support several readings of its use. Temple is its historical label and the building’s purpose remains open. [5] [4] [7] [8]

Standing stones, cairns and journeys across the islands

The uprights at Bordastubble stand high on Unst. Their size and construction differ, and the smaller stone may have been built into a burial mound. Elsewhere the heel-shaped cairns have a concave front and a passage leading to one or more chambers. Felsite offers separate evidence for island connections. Tools made from material quarried in North Roe have been found as far south as Sumburgh and as far north as Unst. Those findspots show movement of objects and materials, while particular sea routes remain reconstructions. [9] [10] [11]

Later towers, roundhouses and wheelhouses

Mousa preserves the height and wall construction that is reduced to low courses at many other broch sites. At Old Scatness, archaeologists can date a broch and see how later residents remodeled or built around it. At Jarlshof, the superimposed structures include round buildings and wheelhouses whose radial walls organized space around a hearth. Norse builders later left a different pattern of longhouses. The building sequence spans millennia, with breaks in occupation, and gives little basis for linking Iron Age towers to the people who erected Neolithic or Bronze Age stones. [12] [15] [13] [16]

Why a coastal site can be both exposed and preserved

Windblown sand buried successive building phases at Jarlshof. Storms later exposed the structures. At West Voe, coastal erosion brought the shell middens to light but also removed part of their setting. Inland, peat can mask walls or old fields. Reading these landscapes therefore depends on excavations, surviving ground traces and the histories of the modern shores as well as the visible monuments. [13] [1] [5] [4]
