Trade on the Mediterranean Sea: ports, shipwrecks and connected civilizations

Far from dividing Africa, West Asia, and Europe, the enclosed Mediterranean Sea served for five thousand years as the world’s cheapest heavy-freight highway. Carrying grain, copper ingots, olive oil, timber, glass, silk, enslaved people, and scripts from port to port, merchant hulls transformed shore towns across all three continents.

Archaeology of Bronze Age shipwrecks, Phoenician and Greek colonies, Hellenistic/Roman concrete harbors, and medieval–modern shipping networks across the Mediterranean, c. 3000 BCE–1900 CE.

Bronze Age hulls to Phoenician and Greek container networks

Maritime logistics enabled metallurgy and urbanization long before iron tools. Third-millennium Byblos cedar convoys supplied treeless Egypt’s temples and royal hulls, while the fourteenth-century BCE Uluburun shipwreck off southwest Turkey reveals ten tons of Cypriot copper paired with Central Asian tin, Syrian resin, raw cobalt glass ingots, and African ivory—all carried in a single mortise-and-tenon hull. [2] [1] [3] [19]

Following the Late Bronze crisis, Phoenician merchants from Tyre and Sidon and Aegean sailors from Corinth, Euboea, and Ionia knit the entire basin together from Nile Naukratis to Carthage, Syracuse, Massalia, and Atlantic Cádiz. Standardized clay transport amphorae ("containerization"), silver coinage, and the West Semitic–Greek alphabets spread swiftly along these commercial shipping lanes. [5] [9] [7] [18]

Hydraulic concrete empires, Yenikapı hulls and the Geniza merchants

Integrating Campanian volcanic-ash hydraulic concrete allowed Roman engineers at Caesarea Maritima and Imperial Portus to pour massive artificial harbors directly into open seas, supporting grain fleets (annona) of up to 1,200 tons per ship alongside Baetican and African oil cargoes stacked sky-high in Rome’s Monte Testaccio. [13] [10] [12] [25]

Maritime exchange remained polycentric rather than halting with western Rome: Constantinople’s 37 Yenikapı hulls trace structural frame-first shipbuilding evolution; the 1025 CE Serçe Limanı vessel carried three tons of Islamic Syrian glass cullet for Byzantine recyclers; Cairo Geniza letters chronicle dense eleventh-century Muslim–Jewish business agency between Egypt, Tunisia, and Sicily; and Amalfi, Pisa, Genoa, and Venice institutionalized marine insurance, partnerships, and convoy shipping before the 1869 Suez Canal united Mediterranean waters directly with Indian Ocean steamships. [14] [15] [16] [17] [24]
