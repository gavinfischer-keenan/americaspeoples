Great Lakes and Ontario: waterways, villages and treaty country

Waterways, fish fences and gathering places

The Great Lakes were connected working landscapes long before colonial forts appeared. Two tiny obsidian flakes from a now-submerged Lake Huron setting were traced chemically to a source in central Oregon, more than 4,000 kilometres away. Their early-Holocene context gives material evidence of far-reaching connections. The objects do not tell us that one person travelled the entire distance: repeated exchange could also move stone across great stretches of country. A lake that now looks like a barrier once held different shorelines and places where people worked, travelled and made tools. Source 1 ↗

At Mnjikaning, the narrow channel between Lakes Simcoe and Couchiching concentrated fish and human activity. Wooden stakes survive beneath the water from a fishing system whose earliest dated evidence is placed around 3300 BCE. Fish fences required knowledge of water, seasons and animal movement as well as physical construction. Later Huron-Wendat use and Anishinaabeg stewardship connect the material remains to a sacred gathering place. Around Rice Lake, the Serpent Mounds complex adds pottery, pipes, worked bone, antler and food refuse to the regional story between roughly 50 BCE and 300 CE. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Fields and longhouse villages

Maize cultivation and longhouse villages changed the organization of life in southern Ontario over many centuries. A regional archaeological synthesis describes maize farming in the lower Grand River area by about 500 CE and substantial longhouse villages after 1000. Houses brought multiple families into shared domestic spaces; fields, fishing places and hunting grounds supplied a varied economy. These developments did not produce one uniform Great Lakes society. Different communities continued to use waterways, maintain trading relationships and make choices about where to gather and when to relocate. Source 1 ↗

Jean-Baptiste Lainé, also called Mantle in archaeological literature, makes village scale tangible. Longhouses were arranged around a plaza, with a palisade, ditch and embankment defining the defended settlement. The village’s few European objects once encouraged an early-sixteenth-century date. Radiocarbon modeling instead places its most likely date around the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. This revision matters: communities with broad Indigenous networks did not all acquire European goods at the same pace. A village could be highly connected while following a different commercial history from another settlement nearby. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

War, dispersal and new centres

The seventeenth century brought French missions into an already organized Wendat world. Sainte-Marie began as a Jesuit headquarters in 1639. A decade later, a Five Nations Haudenosaunee force attacked Saint-Ignace and Saint-Louis on 16 March 1649; the latter was a Wendat village with its own homes, hearths and defences. Warfare, captivity and displacement transformed communities. The Jesuits burned and left Sainte-Marie in 1649. These endings must be followed by the histories of the people who survived them: the linked St. Lawrence guide traces the community established at Wendake in 1697. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Later wars also redrew settlement without removing Indigenous political actors. Haudenosaunee families built communities along the Grand River after the American Revolution, with the 1784 Haldimand Proclamation central to that history. In 1812, Haudenosaunee allies fought alongside British regulars and Canadian militia at Queenston Heights. Farther west, Fort William operated as the North West Company’s inland headquarters from 1803 to 1821. Its rendezvous joined Indigenous suppliers, Métis and French voyageurs, and company partners in a system that moved furs toward Montreal and supplies toward western trading posts. Treaties, schools and control of livelihoods

The upper lakes remained inhabited homelands as mining and settlement interests expanded. The Robinson-Huron Treaty was signed at Sault Ste. Marie on 9 September 1850 after negotiations concerning land, payments and reserves. A reserve’s surveyed area was not the full extent of a nation’s movements, harvesting or obligations to place. The documentary signing belongs to a relationship with continuing consequences. Farther south, the Williams Treaty signing at Rama on 7 November 1923 concerned claimed land and harvesting rights in country already extensively used by settlers, governments and resource industries. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Schools became another means of imposing colonial authority. The rebuilt Shingwauk Home opened at Sault Ste. Marie in August 1875, following the destruction of the earlier Garden River building. Shingwaukonse’s hopes for useful education for his people differed from a residential-school system directed toward assimilation. Children’s labour was part of institutional life: the school’s print shop operated between 1876 and 1883, producing missionary publications. By the atlas endpoint of 1934, restrictions on land, livelihoods and family authority coexisted with enduring communities. Survivor-led archives make individual experiences visible within that larger history. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

Mnjikaning — early wooden fish-weir evidence

Surviving wooden stakes beneath the Narrows between Lakes Simcoe and Couchiching preserve a fishing system whose earliest dated evidence is placed around 3300 BCE. Later Huron-Wendat use and Anishinaabeg stewardship belong to its long history.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: About 3300 BCE, official early-use estimate
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

A rounded early attestation, not a founding year or proof that every stake remained in continuous use. Cultural affiliation of the earliest stake-makers is unknown.

Rice Lake — Serpent Mounds and material life

The Serpent Mounds complex preserves earthen mortuary structures, pottery, pipes, stone tools, worked bone and antler, and food refuse. Its documented Point Peninsula phase spans approximately 50 BCE–300 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 50 BCE–300 CE, official dated archaeological phase
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This dates an archaeological phase, not all activity around Rice Lake. Point Peninsula is an archaeological grouping and should not replace the identities or stewardship of living First Nations.

Jean-Baptiste Lainé / Mantle — a later Wendat village chronology

Radiocarbon modeling places the Jean-Baptiste Lainé/Mantle site in the late sixteenth to early seventeenth century. Excavated longhouses surrounded a central plaza; palisades, a ditch and an embankment formed defensive works.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1596–1618 CE, 68.2% highest-posterior-density modeled estimate
Dating method: Published Bayesian radiocarbon model, independent Mantle phase, 68.2% highest-posterior-density range; no recalibration here.

1596–1618 is a modeled probability range for the site’s date, not start and abandonment boundaries or a 22-year lifespan. Older circa 1500–1550 estimates are not adopted.

Saint-Louis — attack on a Wendat village

On 16 March 1649, a Five Nations Haudenosaunee force attacked Saint-Ignace and then the Wendat village called Saint-Louis by the Jesuits. The episode brought destruction, deaths and captivity within a wider crisis in Wendat country.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 16 March 1649
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

One named attack is recorded; the designation’s sweeping language about the fate of a whole people is not adopted. No casualty estimate or exact battle line is inferred.

Fort William — a canoe-trade headquarters

Fort William served as the North West Company’s inland headquarters from 1803 to 1821. Montreal trade goods and western furs met here, carried through networks involving Indigenous suppliers, Métis and French voyageurs, and company partners.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1803–1821, North West Company headquarters phase
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This interval is the company-headquarters phase, not the lifespan of the surrounding communities. The marker uses Thunder Bay, not the later reconstructed historical park or an original building footprint.

Sault Ste. Marie — Robinson-Huron Treaty negotiations

The Robinson-Huron Treaty was signed at Sault Ste. Marie on 9 September 1850 after negotiations over northern lake-shore lands, payments and reserves. It followed the separate Robinson-Superior agreement.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 9 September 1850
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

A historical signing event, not a map of ceded land or a modern legal conclusion. Reserve provisions did not define the whole geographic life of each signatory nation.

Sault Ste. Marie — Shingwauk Home opens

A rebuilt Shingwauk Home formally opened at Sault Ste. Marie on 2 August 1875. It became part of the residential-school system that sought to assimilate Indigenous children and separate education from community authority.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 2 August 1875, opening of rebuilt Shingwauk Home
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The 1875 marker concerns the rebuilt institution in Sault Ste. Marie, following the short-lived 1873 Garden River school. It does not date the later surviving Shingwauk Hall building.

Rama — Williams Treaty signing

The reproduced Williams Treaty text records signatures at Rama on 7 November 1923. Its written terms sought surrender of claims to land and harvesting rights, extending colonial control across country already occupied and used by settlers.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 7 November 1923, Rama signing
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

Rama is one signing place within a sequence involving seven First Nations; this marker does not locate the whole treaty territory or resolve differing understandings of its terms.

Lake Huron — obsidian from Oregon

Two obsidian flakes recovered from an early Holocene submerged Lake Huron context were geochemically sourced to Wagontire in central Oregon, more than 4,000 km away.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Associated occupation context: 9000–8800 cal yr BP (7051–6851 BCE)
Dating method: O’Shea et al. 2021 full text, Archaeological context: radiocarbon determinations at the recovery depth/elevation. Calendar conversion is 1950 minus cal BP.

Contextual age rather than a date measured on the stone. Source and find location establish material movement, not a mapped travel route, direct trade between endpoints or a journey by one person.

Huronia — Wendat and French language encounter

Sagard’s 1632 dictionary records French phrases and Wendat equivalents, drawing on his stay in Wendat country in 1623–1624. It documents a language encounter within a region of villages, travel and exchange.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1632 CE — documentary milestone
Dating method: Source-specific archaeological chronology or documentary event chronology; see cited passage.

Broad Huronia reference. The book’s publication date is 1632; it was printed in Paris. Colonial judgements in the original are not adopted as descriptions of the language.
