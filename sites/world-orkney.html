Neolithic Orkney’s houses, tombs and stone circles

Between the lochs of Harray and Stenness, a short stretch of ground connects the Stones of Stenness, the Ness of Brodgar and the Ring of Brodgar. Houses at Barnhouse and the tomb at Maeshowe enlarge that picture. Farther across the islands, settlements and cairns show how widely people built in stone and how differently they used it.

The guide follows domestic, ceremonial and burial evidence across Neolithic Orkney, along with selected later reuse and the changing setting of the Brodgar isthmus.

The whole area in view

Approximate locations across the Orkney archipelago. The four World Heritage monuments are on Mainland, while other mapped examples extend to Rousay, Westray, Papa Westray and Sanday.

  1. Skara Brae, Bay of Skaill

    Later 4th–3rd millennia BCE

    Linked drystone houses, passages and fitted stone furnishings preserve a settlement near the coast. [4] [17]

    59.05°, -3.34°

  2. Stones of Stenness

    Around 3000 BCE

    A small ring of tall slabs stood inside a bank and ditch. Excavation recovered evidence of a central arrangement. [9] [17] [3]

    58.99°, -3.21°

  3. Barnhouse settlement

    Early 3rd millennium BCE

    Houses and the unusually large Structure Eight stood north of Stenness toward the shore of Harray. [5] [16]

    59.00°, -3.21°

  4. Ness of Brodgar

    From around 3300 BCE, with major activity in the 3rd millennium

    Excavation exposed several generations of large stone buildings on the neck of land between the lochs. [6] [8] [7]

    59.00°, -3.21°

  5. Ring of Brodgar

    Probably mid to late 3rd millennium BCE

    A large stone circle with a rock-cut ditch. Several nearby mounds represent separate construction events. [10] [17] [16]

    59.00°, -3.23°

  6. Maeshowe chambered cairn

    Neolithic, with medieval reuse

    A long passage leads to a corbelled chamber and side cells. Norse visitors later cut runes into the walls. [11] [3]

    59.00°, -3.19°

  7. Loch of Harray

    Landscape context

    The eastern loch frames Barnhouse and the isthmus. Water levels and local shorelines changed during prehistory. [19] [3]

    59.03°, -3.21°

  8. Loch of Stenness

    Landscape context

    This loch lies west of the isthmus. Sediment cores record a transition from fresh to brackish water. [19] [3]

    58.98°, -3.24°

  9. Midhowe chambered cairn, Rousay

    Neolithic

    Upright slabs divide an elongated burial chamber into compartments along the Rousay shore. [14] [16]

    59.16°, -3.10°

  10. Links of Noltland, Westray

    From around 3300 BCE, with Bronze Age phases

    Dunes preserved extensive evidence of farming, houses, burials and changing settlement layouts. [13] [16]

    59.33°, -3.01°

    Later 4th–3rd millennia BCE

    Two thick-walled buildings stand adjoining one another near the west coast of the island. [12] [17]

The monuments belong to overlapping phases

Radiocarbon measurements from occupation layers, hearths and tomb deposits date particular events. Researchers compare those measurements with the order of layers and buildings to model activity. On Orkney, this work shows periods of overlap as well as shifts between islands and within Mainland. The proximity of two sites on the modern map cannot establish that their main phases were contemporary. [17] [8] [18]

What survives of the houses

At Skara Brae, slabs delineate beds, hearths, storage and the familiar shelves often called dressers. Covered passages link houses built among accumulated settlement debris. Barnhouse has comparable stone houses and an exceptionally large building built around a central hearth. At Knap of Howar, two elongated buildings are joined by a passage. The different layouts provide evidence about how households or activities were organized, and about rebuilding through time. [2] [4] [5] [12]

On Westray, Links of Noltland widens the view beyond the central monuments. Houses and deposits in the dunes record successive phases of farming and occupation. Some settlements are known well because accumulated sand or debris protected walls and interiors. The present distribution of well-preserved houses also reflects where archaeologists have excavated and where features survived coastal change. [13] [18]

From henge to monumental buildings

Stenness was planned as a modest-sized ring with very tall stones and a central feature. The Ring of Brodgar surrounds a much larger open space with a rock-cut ditch. At the Ness between them, excavators recorded unusually wide buildings with piers, colored and incised stones, and an architectural sequence reaching beneath the visible structures. The project interprets some phases as gatherings. The differences between these buildings and ordinary houses help make that case, while the exact activities within them remain under study. [9] [10] [6]

Chambers for the dead

Maeshowe’s passage admits the low winter sun near midwinter, a property of its architectural orientation. The chamber and side cells make a different use of stone from the houses. Midhowe has a long series of stalls, while at Quoyness side chambers open off a central room. Differences in layout offer ways to compare how access and deposition were organized. The runes at Maeshowe are direct evidence of medieval visitors and belong to a separate chapter in its history. [11] [14] [15] [2]

A land bridge in a changing landscape

Surveys of the Brodgar isthmus and samples from the loch bed trace a changing relationship between land and water. The researchers found evidence that Stenness became brackish during the Neolithic and identified now-submerged terrain near the monuments. This work gives the land bridge a history of its own. It also cautions against inferring ancient views, routes or access from a photo of the two lochs as they look today. [19] [18]
