Evidence from the peninsula

In the northern peninsula, the dead at Bajamar–Jatay were buried with stones that archaeologists identify as grinding equipment. The finding concerns a particular local practice many millennia before colonial mission records. Farther south the older remains preserved in the peninsular mountains and islands continue to offer questions about how different communities used land and sea. Source 1 ↗

Building across the Gulf

Across the Gulf of California in Sonora, investigations at La Ciénega and Cerro de Trincheras reveal different ways to build a settlement. At La Ciénega, excavators found earthen architecture along the Cocóspera river valley. At Cerro de Trincheras, communities constructed terraces across a volcanic slope for living, making things and farming. The sites show choices made in particular valleys, even where archaeologists use a common regional label. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Space and water at San Javier

By the eighteenth century, missions involved a new spatial pattern, with colonial authorities moving some institutions to locations better suited to farming. The walls visible at San Francisco Javier belong to the later site and took years to build. Compare the building with the existing Loreto record to follow the growth of the mission system without treating the present church as evidence of an exact founding-era footprint. Source 1 ↗

Published ranges and open questions

The peninsula and Sonoran mainland are both represented, but each has a very different evidentiary record. Work by INAH at La Ciénega was announced with a provisional range. Cerro de Trincheras has a longer excavation and interpretation history. The Bajamar date pertains to an individual burial rather than the beginning or end of a settlement. Follow these ranges alongside existing context for other sites across northern Mexico. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗
