Chaluka and Nikolski

At Nikolski on Umnak Island, the Chaluka mound holds layers of ancestral houses and work areas accumulated through generations of village life. Across the bay, Ananiuliak Island contains an early toolmaking site and a distinct later village. Together their floors, depressions and buildings show several ways residents lived on the coast.

Two places on Ananiuliak

The early Core-and-Blade site holds deposits of discarded cores, long blades and the flakes left by shaping and maintaining tools. A recent lithic reassessment describes an approximate age range of 9,600–8,000 calibrated years before 1950, with the evidence from house floors and hearths potentially representing a much narrower span. The deposits include shallow house depressions as well as extensive stone-working debris. Researchers have debated how long people remained and how much of the site they used at one time. The shapes of the stone tools and their imported materials still give a direct view of decisions about work in this early coastal setting. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

The National Park Service reports up to twenty-four house depressions at the distinct, younger Anangula Village site. They occur in three rows along a bluff, with smaller hollows interpreted as storage pits. The six houses excavators tested were partly sunken and roughly rectangular to oval. Fresh water ran down the bluff toward a narrow beach. The recorded rows establish a relationship among houses, high ground and access to water, while their individual spacing, dates and period of shared use remain unresolved. The landmark account mentions a smaller possible seasonal locality elsewhere on the island. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A house and its work areas at Chaluka

At Chaluka, the deep mound contains floors, discarded tools and accumulated remains from marine food gathering. One stratigraphic study compared excavations from 1938 to 1972 and distinguished eleven major natural and human events. The study reports reef and marine activity through the sampled sequence. Over time, houses were built and replaced and layers of activity accumulated. The excavated areas capture particular parts of the village. This is why the mound preserves a long history even though the layout of the entire settlement is hard to recover for any one date. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A house examined by Jean S. Aigner had an uninterrupted stone perimeter, a hearth lined in slabs and pits sunk beneath its floor. Floor deposits provide a closer view than the mound profile. Near the east corner, archaeologists found a cluster of small ovoid pebbles. Near several slab-lined and unlined depressions they found twenty-two awls and needles. Some pits had covers. Such concentrations can suggest tasks carried out in different areas, even when instruments were put down, stored or swept away in ways the surviving deposits cannot fully distinguish. These observations describe one excavated interior among several recorded walls. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Chapels and the standing church

The Historic American Buildings Survey dates construction of an early chapel directed by John Kriukov to 1806. A successor designed by Ivan Veniaminov was built of driftwood and consecrated in 1828. The church survey also mentions a possible later nineteenth-century church with an uncertain date and position. Driftwood makes the relationship between seafaring and building materials unusually clear. The earlier chapels and the standing church belong to distinct phases, even though no surveyed footprints for the early chapels are provided by those sources. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The standing St. Nicholas Church was erected in 1930. Its square front tower rises before a gabled narthex, a hipped nave and a gabled sanctuary. Intricate interior woodcarving records the work of Sergei Sovoroff and other local makers. The HABS account names Sergei and Joseph Krukoff as carvers of the royal doors. The survey photograph shows the church in the village decades after its construction. The shape it documents includes later work, including a ceiling altered after the atlas’s period. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Village work before 1934

The Aleutian Pribilof Islands Association records sea otter hunting at Nikolski in 1834 and the name Recheshnoe used in Russian records. A fox-farming boom began in 1920, and the Aleutian Livestock Company established a sheep ranch in 1926. Local people bought the vessel Umnak Native, which was wrecked in 1933. These ventures added movements of workers, animals and goods to a community with a much longer maritime history. The old mound, successive chapels and working shores mark different kinds of residence and work within the same village setting. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The local history follows named places at several scales. Ananiuliak’s early tools belong to an island locality. Its younger village has surviving rows of houses. Chaluka’s layered record contains one house with close evidence of activity, and nearby Nikolski has buildings documented in written records and photographs. Read together, they trace long residence around Nikolski Bay without assigning every generation to a single building layout. Community accounts also speak of ties between the island and Nikolski. Those ties belong in the story alongside the dates that archaeologists and building historians can establish. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

Anangula Village — rows of houses above the water

At the distinct Anangula Village site, surveyors recorded up to 24 house depressions in three rows along the bluff. Six tested houses were partly sunken and roughly rectangular to oval. Smaller depressions were interpreted as storage pits.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Younger than the Anangula Core-and-Blade site
Dating method: Relative chronology in the landmark account. Sample ages require context and verified calibration before assigning a calendar window.

The plan evidence describes rows and approximate forms. Published descriptions leave spacing and contemporaneity unresolved. The map point refers to the island.

Chaluka — a stone-walled house and its work areas

One excavated house at Chaluka had a stone perimeter, slab-lined hearth and depressions below the floor. Clusters of pebbles, awls and needles hint at tasks organized along the walls and near the pits.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Reported assays from material in the house: 4028 ± 100 to 3220 ± 52 radiocarbon BP
Dating method: Published uncalibrated range of separate dates reported by Rogers from Aigner. Calendar limits were unavailable in the consulted material.

Six rock walls were identified at the site. Detailed observations refer to the excavated house. The associated samples leave the building’s calendar lifespan unresolved.

Anangula — a coastal stone-tool workshop

Craftworkers at Anangula struck long blades from prepared stone cores and retouched them into cutting and scraping tools. Imported raw materials and the coastal setting show the work behind early maritime life.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approx. 9600–8000 calibrated years BP
Dating method: Published approximate calibrated sample envelope, expressed as astronomical years by subtracting from 1950.

The broadly spaced assemblage dates and much tighter estimates for house-floor or hearth evidence address different questions.

Chaluka and Nikolski — a village landscape across millennia

At Chaluka, layers of houses, tools and food remains built a large village mound beside present-day Nikolski. Archaeology and community history describe roughly four millennia of residence and changing use at this enduring settlement.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 4,000 years of village history; shown from c.2000 BCE through the atlas endpoint 1934
Dating method: Rounded archaeological and community-history synthesis.

The beginning is approximate. Houses and seasonal activities changed over time; this is the village landscape’s history, not one building occupied for four millennia.

Amaknak Bridge — stone houses and elaborate hearths

On a knoll above the channel, builders terraced stone houses, repaired walls and laid new floors and hearths across older ones. Most houses were round or oval, about 3–4 metres across. A few were larger and rectangular.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Published charcoal-date envelope 3922–2358 cal BP; approximately 1973–409 BCE
Dating method: Outer envelope of calibrated charcoal ranges reported by Rogers; not a new pooled radiocarbon model.

The envelope spans samples from different contexts. It does not date every structure or establish continuous occupation throughout the whole range.
