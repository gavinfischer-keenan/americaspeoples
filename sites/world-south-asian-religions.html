Hindu and Muslim histories in South Asia

A copper-plate grant from Malabar, a Persian translation of the Mahabharata and a weaver’s songs offer different views of religious life in South Asia. Merchant records, court documents and survivor testimony show how relations changed with the institutions and politics of each place.

Selected episodes from the early medieval period through the British Raj. A separate epilogue in this guide covers the 1947 Partition, after the atlas timeline ends in 1934.

Ports and inland frontiers

In the early eighth century, Umayyad armies under Muhammad ibn Qasim conquered parts of Sindh in the lower Indus valley. The Persian Chachnama was written centuries later. Historian Manan Ahmed Asif reads it in the context of thirteenth-century Sindh, where its stories of conquest were also arguments about how rulers should govern a religiously diverse society. [5]

Muslim traders settled in Malabar’s ports, which had links to Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean. The Kollam copper plates of 849 record a grant by a local ruler to a Christian establishment. Signatures in several scripts attest to the port’s religious diversity. A twelfth-century letter preserved in the Cairo Geniza asked a Jewish trader in southwestern India to pass news about iron prices to two Hindu associates and one Muslim associate. [1] [18] [19] [20] [21]

Sultanates, conquest and settlement

After the Ghurid conquest of Delhi in the late twelfth century, Qutb al-Din Aibak began a congregational mosque at Mehrauli, reusing pillars from Hindu and Jain temples. Aibak became the first sultan of Delhi in 1206. In the fifteenth-century Bengal delta, Khalifatabad, now Bagerhat, developed mosques alongside roads and reservoirs. Historian Richard Eaton links the later growth of Muslim communities in eastern Bengal to agricultural expansion. Some Mughal land grants required recipients to cultivate the land and maintain a mosque or shrine. [2] [3] [6] [22] [23] [24]

In the sixteenth-century Deccan, Vijayanagara’s ruler Rama Raya exploited rivalries among neighbouring sultanates, dealing with rulers and nobles across religious lines. In 1565 several of those sultanates joined against him at Talikota, defeated his army and sacked his capital. [11]

Mughal rule and the politics of patronage

Akbar’s court drew some Rajput houses into the empire through marriage and military service. Other Rajput rulers faced imperial campaigns. Near Agra at Fatehpur Sikri, his court built a mosque, a Sufi tomb and palaces that drew on different architectural traditions. In 1582 he commissioned the Persian Razmnama, a translation of the Sanskrit epic Mahabharata. Surviving illustrated copies show how the imperial workshop made that epic available in a new language and visual form. [8] [9] [10] [25] [26]

In 1670 Aurangzeb ordered the destruction of Mathura’s Keshava Deva temple. Eaton situates the attack in the context of rebellion near Mathura and reads the targeting of politically prominent temples as an expression of imperial power in religious terms. In the 1660s, two provincial governors under Aurangzeb granted resources to priests who served at the Mahakaleshwar temple in Ujjain. [27] [28] [29]

Shrines, songs and religious practice

At Ajmer the tomb of Muinuddin Chishti drew Muslim and Hindu pilgrims to a shrine of the Chishti Sufi order. Akbar repeatedly offered gifts and alms there between 1562 and 1579. The journeys also gave him opportunities to form alliances and assert authority. At Uch Sharif in southern Punjab, the tomb of Bibi Jawindi commemorates a woman remembered for her piety and influence within a Sufi lineage. [4] [5] [8]

Bhakti, devotion to the divine, took many forms. The songs associated with Kabir, remembered as a weaver from a Muslim family in Varanasi, address a formless divine and criticize caste prejudice and sectarian pride. Communities have passed down different versions of his songs. In Bijapur, Sultan Ibrahim Adil Shah II’s Kitab-i Nauras invoked Saraswati, the Hindu goddess of music and learning, alongside Muslim holy figures. [7] [11] [12] [30]

Work, property and caste

Legal papers from a family of Hindu landholders in Dhar, central India, show them using courts presided over by Muslim judges. In one inheritance dispute a judge apportioned shares without referring to the family’s religion. The surviving papers offer a view from a property-owning family, whose access to courts is easier to trace than that of landless neighbours. [13] [29]

In colonial Punjab, official returns recorded members of some subordinated caste groups as Hindu, Muslim, Sikh or Christian. Religious affiliation offered ways to organize and claim a different social position. In the countryside, dependence on dominant landowners and restrictions on access to land continued to shape what working families could change. [31]

Colonial categories and political coalitions

From the late nineteenth century, British census officials sought to place people in standardized categories of caste and religion. The results gave officials and political organizers figures for the size of communities. The 1909 Morley–Minto reforms gave Muslim voters on designated rolls separate electorates for some legislative seats, giving religious identification a formal role in political representation. [31] [32]

In 1905 British authorities divided Bengal and made Dhaka the capital of Eastern Bengal and Assam. The All-India Muslim League was founded in Dhaka the following year. The division provoked opposition and was reversed in 1911. After the First World War, Gandhi and Congress joined Khilafat activists, who sought to preserve the Ottoman caliphate, in the Non-Cooperation campaign against British rule. [14] [15] [33]

In 1921 Muslim Mappila peasants in Malabar rose against colonial authority amid long-standing conflicts over land and rent. Some rebels attacked Hindu civilians and temples. British forces suppressed the uprising, and colonial courts imposed mass convictions. [34] [35] [36]

Epilogue — Partition in 1947

As British rule ended in August 1947, Partition created independent India and Pakistan and divided Punjab and Bengal. The boundary awards became public after independence. Lahore and Amritsar, neighbouring cities separated by the new border, experienced attacks, forced departures and refugee resettlement. Across the new states millions fled or moved. Hindu, Muslim and Sikh families lost relatives and homes. Survivor testimony also documents abduction, forced marriage and other sexual violence. [16] [17] [38] [39]

In Punjab the upheaval around the transfer of power was especially concentrated. Bengal saw waves of displacement over a longer period, with Hindu refugees leaving East Bengal and Muslims moving east from India. Who could remain or return depended on local security, property and the policies of the new states. [16] [39]
