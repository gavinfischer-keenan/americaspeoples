The children of the high Andes

At high places in the Andes, archaeologists have found children buried with clothing, small figures and other offerings. Several bodies survived because the mountains were cold and dry. The children’s burials can tell us about the Inca Empire, journeys along its roads and rituals at sacred mountains. Differences from site to site also show how much remains unknown about each child’s experience.

Selected Inca-period highland burials in present-day Peru, Argentina and Chile. The guide gives ancient dates or broad periods separately from modern discovery, study and care. It includes museum and region anchors for context.

Capacocha and sacred mountains

The Quechua term commonly written capacocha refers to ceremonies documented by colonial-era writers and investigated through archaeological deposits. Scholars have connected some high-altitude burials to these ceremonies because of their architecture, objects and placement. Offerings could relate to imperial occasions and to sacred places whose significance was local as well as imperial. The burial record and written accounts ask different questions. Archaeologists compare them while allowing different communities and mountains to have taken part in different ways. [2] [3] [7]

Roads and the scale of travel

The Qhapaq Ñan connected communities, administrative centres and regions across the Andes. Cusco sits within that wider infrastructure, and some written descriptions link it to the movement of offerings and children. High sanctuaries were reachable through routes that also depended on local knowledge and labour. Maps of the road system explain how movement was possible. Hair chemistry or a textile can supply evidence of differences in diet or manufacture without identifying a particular itinerary or proving a journey through Cusco. [1] [3] [2]

Three children at Llullaillaco

The 1999 excavation documented separate burial contexts near the volcano’s summit. Researchers often refer to the older girl as the Maiden, a younger girl as the Lightning Girl, and the third child as the Boy. These are modern labels. Age estimates depend on methods such as imaging and dental or skeletal development. The 2013 study gave about 13 for the older girl and four to five for each younger child. The museum presents different estimated ages. Treat them as published estimates for the same three people. [8] [20] [10] [21]

Clothes and objects as evidence

Fine textiles and small figures made from materials such as metals and Spondylus shell were recovered from highland offerings. Colour, weave, fastening and design reveal choices that had meaning within Inca society and show how materials travelled. Their position within the chambers matters because it helps connect objects with specific ceremonies and interments. Scholars compare garments with accounts of specialists and social roles. Dress and grave goods leave uncertainty about an individual child’s exact circumstances at home and during preparation. [8] [20] [2]

Reading hair over time

Hair grows in sequence and can preserve chemical evidence relating to different weeks or months. Carbon and nitrogen isotope measurements help track food changes, including interpretations of more maize-rich diets. The conversion from hair distance to months depends on an estimated growth rate. Local plants, animal foods and water have overlapping signatures. Research at Quehuar and other sites tests some of those alternative sources of isotope variation. Different tissues and markers describe different periods and questions. [3] [10] [22]

Coca, alcohol and the limits of the record

Chemical markers in some Llullaillaco hair samples indicate exposure to coca and alcohol before death, with patterns that differed between the children. The older girl’s later hair segments showed especially marked changes. Separate Ampato samples produced their own toxicological findings and are evidence for those individuals. Concentrations and preserved biomolecules do not give an exact time or dose, prove what each child understood, or independently establish a cause of death. Coca has a much broader history of sacred and daily use in the Andes. [10] [23]

Ampato and the person called Juanita

The girl known today as Juanita was recovered in 1995 after exposure of the Ampato find. She is one of several young people documented on Andean mountains near Arequipa. An anatomical study compared people buried at Ampato and Pichu Pichu and discusses injuries and childhood evidence. A facial reconstruction displayed in Arequipa in 2023 used imaging and anatomical modelling. It offers one researched interpretation of her appearance while her clothing, body and recovery context remain the archaeological sources. [4] [5] [6]

El Plomo and the archive of a 1954 find

The child from Cerro El Plomo was recovered in 1954, before the more controlled recovery and laboratory documentation of some later discoveries. Museum staff recorded clothing and associated objects and continue to care for the assemblage in Chile. The mountain’s cold helped preserve the child’s remains, but time outside the burial environment brought different risks. The original discovery context and successive museum studies need to be read together when comparing El Plomo with the more recent Llullaillaco excavation. [17] [18] [19]

Aconcagua, below the summit

Hikers found a roughly seven-year-old child in 1985 at about 5,300 metres on Aconcagua. The altitude is lower than the mountain’s summit, a distinction that matters when looking at a topographic map. Genetic studies traced separate maternal and paternal lineages and compared them with reference samples elsewhere in the Andes. Those comparisons provide evidence about ancestry links. Modern comparison groups, family ancestry and a child’s birthplace are different pieces of evidence. [14] [15] [16]

Chuscha and the loss of context

The person found at Nevado de Chuscha was removed in the early 1920s and passed through several hands. Salta’s museum records both the modern movements and the return to the province in 2006. Early photographs and later study help identify parts of the associated clothing and material. Some details of placement and fine chronology are beyond recovery. Treat the Chuscha story as a record of collection and custody as well as of a high mountain burial. [11] [12]

Why preservation varies between mountains

Llullaillaco’s cold dry conditions preserved soft tissues, textiles and other organic materials. Preservation depends on deposition, temperature, moisture and later disturbance at each site. Excavation on Misti recovered offerings and human remains under different conditions. Radiography and CT on well-preserved remains allow some observations while continuing physical conservation matters after recovery. A record dominated by tissues from the coldest sites mostly tells us which tissues survived there. [7] [21] [13] [18]

Reading conquest-era accounts with care

Written descriptions of capacocha were made after the Spanish conquest, in situations shaped by colonial governance, travel and translation. Authors drew on different informants and sometimes wrote for readers hostile to Indigenous religion. Some reported practices have archaeological parallels and others remain hard to connect to a particular structure or person. The word “sacrifice” names a scholarly interpretation of the context. It gives us little basis for filling in thoughts, dialogue or consent for any one child. [2] [3] [10]

Custody, visibility and community voices

Museums in Salta, Arequipa and Santiago care for remains and associated collections under different circumstances. Institutional policies describe aspects of conservation and public access. Questions about display, research and return also depend on the people and communities who hold relationships to the dead and to these mountain places. Public sources document museum practices in more detail than they document the views of every community. This guide uses mountains, archaeological context and carefully bounded evidence to centre the children without displaying photographs of their bodies. [24] [13] [18] [11] [5]
