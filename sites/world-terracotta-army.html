The Terracotta Army and Qin imperial mausoleum

The rows of clay soldiers in Lintong occupy the eastern side of a much larger necropolis. Moving from the army pits toward the burial mound brings other parts of the First Emperor’s world into view. Horses, chariots, officials, performers and water birds were represented in separate pits. The objects, buildings and workers’ graves preserve different kinds of evidence about the Qin state.

The guide covers the late Warring States and Qin construction project at Mount Li, the associated workforce and materials, and the archaeology of the complex since the army’s discovery in 1974.

From Qin king to First Emperor

UNESCO dates the start of the mausoleum to 246 BCE, around the time Ying Zheng became king of Qin. His armies completed the conquest of the rival states in 221 BCE. By the time he died in 210 BCE, the burial project had spanned his transformation from one king among several to the ruler of a unified empire. The monument’s nested enclosures and differentiated spaces have often been read alongside the layout of Xianyang, the Qin capital farther west in the Wei valley. [1] [4] [9]

The army belonged to a buried court

The army pits lie more than a kilometer east of the imperial burial. Excavators found brick floors and earth-walled corridors spanned by heavy timber roofs. Pit 1 held long ranks of infantry and chariot groups. Pit 2 arranged several types of troops in distinct formations. The smaller Pit 3 is commonly interpreted as a representation of command. These arrangements show how the army was staged for burial. The positions of figures can be observed directly, while their precise ritual roles are interpretations. [2] [4] [9]

The pits closer to the mound represent other parts of the emperor’s establishment. Bronze carriages suggest ceremonial travel. Civil officials, stables and stone armor invoke service and administration. Other deposits include figures interpreted as performers and bronze birds found in a setting that researchers compare to an imperial pleasure park. Excavators can identify objects and their associations more confidently than they can establish a uniform meaning for the whole necropolis. [4] [5] [12]

Material supply and workshop labor

Potters assembled many figures from repeated parts and then varied their hair, armor and facial details. A study of thin slices of pottery compared the clay bodies of warriors, architectural ceramics and other figures. The fabrics are consistent with clay from near the site, with differences in added material suited to different products. Stamped and incised marks provide another trace of workshop organization. Some name makers or workshops, and the recurrence of marks across objects helps researchers track production practices. [6] [7]

The pit architecture consumed large volumes of wood. A study of preserved timbers found low-elevation cypress relatives in one earlier pit and more high-elevation conifers in the army pits. The researchers propose that sourcing shifted up the mountains as work advanced. The woods point to a supply network reaching beyond the immediate foothills, while the locations of specific logging grounds and transport routes remain uncertain. [9]

The people who built the necropolis

The historian Sima Qian described labor mobilized from across the empire. Archaeologists can examine that account against graves near the complex. At Shanren, people buried together included individuals who still wore iron leg shackles, material evidence for coerced labor. Bones from that burial group recorded diets different from those of the people sampled at nearby Liyi, where millet was much more prominent. The isotope evidence is compatible with some Shanren individuals having come from regions farther south. It cannot identify a person’s birthplace or give the project a reliable head count. [1] [8]

Water, burial soil and the unopened chamber

The necropolis occupies the transition from the Mount Li slopes to the Wei plain. Site plans record a dike near the nested enclosures, and the bronze water birds show a different role for water in the funerary landscape. Burial conditions also changed what survived. Analysis of excavated bronze weapons linked their preservation more closely to the local soil than to the chromium once proposed as a protective coating. The study traced chromium on some weapons to nearby lacquer. [4] [1] [11]

Sima Qian’s account describes mercury simulating waterways inside the tomb. Measurements above the mound detected elevated atmospheric mercury in parts of the survey area, consistent with a possible source below. The measurement and the text are different kinds of evidence. The anomaly cannot show how mercury was arranged within the unexcavated chamber. [10] [1]
