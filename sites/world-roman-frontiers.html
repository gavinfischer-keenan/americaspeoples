Roman Expansion and Frontiers

At Birdoswald, the Roman army placed a fort above a river. In Dacia, a bridge and fort helped secure a crossing of a much larger river, the Danube. These were different answers to local terrain, military priorities and the movement of people and goods.

What conquest added

Imperial conquest extended a system of provincial rule built during the Republic. Augustus inherited an empire in this geographic sense. Under Claudius, the invasion of Britain in 43 CE began the conquest of what became the Roman province there. Trajan’s war in Dacia brought land north of the lower Danube under Roman control in 106. Different dates mark the campaign, the creation of a province and the work of keeping it supplied. [12] [13] [1] [5]

Trajan also took territory in his war against Parthia. The extent reached late in his reign was short-lived. His successor Hadrian abandoned much of the new eastern territory. The brief advances and later withdrawals matter when reading a map labeled with a single year. Cities such as Palmyra could remain part of Roman Syria while maintaining relationships across the Euphrates with people under other rulers. [1] [10] [14] [15]

Britain’s built frontier

Work on Hadrian’s Wall began around 122 CE. Its design changed during building, and forts were incorporated into the system. Excavations at Birdoswald distinguish an early turf barrier from subsequent stone building. The fort itself occupied high ground over a river and housed troops. The remains show how a wall line connected to a larger landscape of posts, routes and settlements. [16] [3]

Controlled crossings and observation mattered alongside defense. Later building and occupation altered the frontier again. The stone visible at any one locality represents part of that history, and English Heritage’s site accounts distinguish features built at different times. [16] [3] [17]

The Danube and the Dacian uplands

At Drobeta, a fort and the remains of Trajan’s bridge mark a connection across the Danube. The Dacian limes farther inland combined legionary and auxiliary forts, temporary camps, watchtowers and earthworks. In a landscape broken by hills and valleys, sites helped control approaches to the province and access to resources such as gold and salt. [5] [6]

The Dacian royal center at Sarmizegetusa Regia lay in the Orăștie Mountains. The Roman colony with the name Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa was founded at a separate site. Archaeological remains of the two places record different political centers. The Roman system north of the river lasted into the later third century, and the UNESCO account dates the operation of the Dacian limes to 106–271 CE. [7] [5] [6]

Communities around forts and colonies

The North African colony of Timgad had a planned grid and a forum. Founded under Trajan, it illustrates a civic form of imperial expansion in the Aurès region. In Britain, a frontier fort and the communities near it depended on connections for food, goods and labor. Military diplomas show one way long service could affect the legal status of auxiliaries. These forms of settlement and mobility were part of governing the provinces. [8] [3] [18]

Walls, Limes & Frontier Fortifications Across Three Continents

Beyond Britain's Hadrian's and Antonine Walls, Rome fortified thousands of kilometers of frontier across mainland Europe, North Africa, and the Syrian/Arabian desert: the 550-km Upper Germanic–Rhaetian Limes palisade and watchtower curtain linking Rhine to Danube, Carnuntum and Trajan's 20-pier Danube Bridge at Drobeta leading into Dacian fortifications, North Africa's 750-km Fossatum Africae customs ditch regulating Sahara pastoral migrations, and the Qasr Bshir quadriburgia along the Via Traiana Nova (Limes Arabicus). [19] [20] [21] [23]

When 3rd–5th century Germanic, Sarmatian, and Hunnic invasions pierced linear frontiers, Emperors Aurelian (271–275 CE, 19-km brick Aurelian Walls of Rome) and Theodosius II (408–413 CE, triple land walls of Constantinople) shifted imperial security inward to monumental urban circuits that protected the Eastern capital through 1453 CE. [22] [19]

Latin · Supply request

The soldiers have no beer

97–105 CE · Vindolanda, Northumberland, England
Tab.Vindol. 628, front, column ii, lines 4–6

Masclus, a cavalry officer, asks his commander for instructions about the next day. He adds the request for beer after signing off. This wooden tablet predates Hadrian’s Wall.
