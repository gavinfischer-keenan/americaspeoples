Creating a monumental mountain

The Sun Pyramid presents a long building history behind its apparently unified mass. Excavators distinguish the earlier landscape, construction of the main pyramid, and the adjoining adosada platform. An artificial tunnel adds another architectural layer below the monument. Sugiyama and colleagues proposed a main-building date of 170–310 CE and a tunnel date of 140–240 CE; Sload argues for an earlier chronology. The scene therefore introduces a growing monumental center while showing how excavated sequences can support competing construction histories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Neighbourhood households and work

La Ventilla brings the view away from the pyramids and into a neighborhood of compounds. Archaeologists distinguish an elite and administrative compound, LV 2, from LV 3, associated with lapidary workers. These settings make households, specialized production and unequal access to space part of the city's history. The later dating study also shows why a ceramic label is not a direct label for a political regime. At this viewing year the existing city record supplies context, while the compound evidence gives the guide its smaller scale. Source 1 ↗

Contextual Classic-period view anchored by existing urban record. Source 1 ↗

Burned surfaces, competing clocks

Burned architectural surfaces provide a different clock from pottery or inscriptions. At the Superimposed Buildings, archaeomagnetic measurements estimate the last strong heating experienced by sampled materials. Two models place it in the fifth century, while another yields an earlier range. Other sampled places produce still different or multiple possible dates. The city consequently should not change from flourishing to empty in one synchronized frame. This scene follows one measured burning context and keeps its chronological disagreement visible alongside the continuing urban landscape. Source 1 ↗

Last-heating estimates, not one proved city-wide destruction. Source 1 ↗

A neighbourhood reused

At La Ventilla, later burials cut through earlier walls and floors after buildings had fallen into disrepair. Burial 27 in LV 2 supplies a calibrated probability component of 540–610 CE, placing activity within a compound whose earlier organization had changed. The deposits show people engaging with an existing built environment rather than occupying a newly cleared blank space. LV 3 has a separate, later burial window. These observations support a local reuse sequence without making one neighborhood's history the timetable for the entire city. Source 1 ↗

Calibrated burial evidence; reoccupation inference. Source 1 ↗

Lives inside the old quarry landscape

The old quarry tunnels of Pirul and Varillas, behind the Sun Pyramid, became places of domestic, craft, burial and ritual activity. Their later occupants used spaces created by earlier extraction, adding another inhabited setting to the transformed city. The modeled Coyotlatelco phase spans approximately 640–820 CE. At this viewing year, the separately dated LV 3 burials also provide neighborhood evidence. Together the records reveal multiple places of activity after major political change, although they do not establish one unbroken citywide population or a single new community. Source 1 ↗

Modeled local occupation phase. Source 1 ↗

Mazapa occupations

The tunnel sequence includes a Mazapa occupation phase modeled to 940–1230 CE. This takes the story well beyond the conventional endpoint of Teotihuacan's principal urban-development phase. The studied underground spaces preserve working and living activities, including evidence of bifacial tool production in Pirul. The workshop's precise position in every chronological phase is not independently established in this introductory report. The useful comparison is between a place's changing uses over centuries and the much shorter history of the government that built its great monuments. Source 1 ↗

Modeled local phase 940–1230 CE. Source 1 ↗

Aztec-period return and ritual

Aztec III occupation evidence returns the studied tunnel landscape to view in approximately 1390–1460 CE. The intervening local hiatus matters: later use is a new episode in an old place, not proof that every earlier neighborhood remained continuously occupied. Teotihuacan's monumental remains and excavated spaces continued to offer settings for activity long after the Classic city's political system had ended. This final scene closes with a layered landscape, allowing the later record to remain visible without extending the original urban government's lifetime. Source 1 ↗

Modeled evidence window after a local hiatus. Source 1 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Sun Pyramid construction models disagree; keep 170–310 proposal in guide with opposition, rather than publish it as settled construction.

Do not combine six different last-heating results into a fabricated 400–650 Great Fire event.

No language or ethnicity can be assigned to all La Ventilla residents solely from ceramic style.

Dedicated food/water chapter needs primary archaeobotanical and hydraulic reports before more specific crop or canal claims.

Exact internal coordinates require a published, georeferenced public plan; retain canonical city anchor.

No independently checked pre 1934 community-authored continuity source in this packet.

Individually reviewed atlas evidence

Teotihuacan: first- to seventh-century urban development

Teotihuacan’s major urban development is placed by UNESCO in the first through seventh centuries CE. Monumental planning and residential remains demonstrate an extensive city within the Teotihuacan Valley.

Source date guidance: UNESCO: the settlement developed into a major city between the 1st and 7th centuries A.D.; the valley also has earlier occupation.
Dating method: Archaeological heritage synthesis of urban and architectural sequence; the page supplies no sample-level radiocarbon or calibration details.

Century-scale synthesis cannot assign every neighborhood or monument the same dates. Teotihuacan is an archaeological label here and does not identify a single language or ethnicity. Current coordinate matches UNESCO property 414 DMS metadata and is already suitable as a public archaeological-site reference. Rename only the precision phrase: numeric decimals do not certify field accuracy.
