A settlement before the monumental capital

People established a settlement at Tiwanaku before its best-known stone monuments were built. Later construction and immigration transformed it into a planned center in the Titicaca basin. A statistical model combining radiocarbon dates estimates foundation around AD 180; the broad 400–900 urban phase describes the later city. The earlier estimate does not identify a first house or a founding ethnic group. Settlement, monument building, and the growth of a political center unfolded through related stages of the place’s history. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Making Pumapunku

Stoneworkers at Pumapunku shaped blocks into carefully worked forms and assembled a major monument within Tiwanaku. Marsh and colleagues estimate the start of construction near AD 580. Later damage and displacement separated many blocks from their original positions. Vranich uses the surviving pieces to explore how the building may once have fitted together. The blocks themselves preserve the builders’ work; the reconstructed arrangement remains a hypothesis where the original masonry no longer survives in place. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

580 is a boundary median, not a precise construction ceremony. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Reworking stone in a growing ceremonial city

A surface within Pumapunku’s sequence around modeled median AD 630 separates the last phase using newly quarried and carved stone from subsequent reuse. Builders continued to work with an inherited stock of masonry. Nearby monuments, including Akapana, Kalasasaya, the Semi-subterranean Temple and Putuni, have their own architectural histories rather than one shared foundation date. The model places Pumapunku’s main construction end around 710 and the citywide major construction boundary around 720. Those endpoints concern a particular scale and category of work. Later people still altered spaces and reused stone, so an end to major new monument building does not mean an end to all communal construction. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

630, 710 and 720 are approximate modeled medians; scene anchor only. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Later communities remake older places

The ninth and tenth centuries included new communal work in an already old city. The current synthesis identifies later projects around AD 920–970 that reused inherited stone and spaces. At Putuni, earlier kitchens, stone-lined drains and mortuary activities were followed by razing, filling and construction of a large courtyard. Dated burials and a burned beam constrain parts of that sequence, but neither dates every wall or proves one destructive fire. People were selectively rebuilding and changing uses after the main period of new monument construction. A wood sample may predate the action associated with it, making context as important as the laboratory measurement. Source 1 ↗

940 is an orientation point within the contextual 920–970 range, not a directly dated project. Source 1 ↗

Permanent neighborhoods come to an end

The combined residential model places a major change near AD 1010: permanent residential activity in the sampled central neighborhoods ends within a main-text 95 percent interval of 970–1060. The abstract instead prints 970–1050. This is a bounded claim about permanent residence, not evidence that every person left on the same day. Individual neighborhoods have wider uncertainty ranges, and later small camps and burials represent different forms of presence. Long-lived house floors and rebuilt walls preserve evidence of continuing households; a short-lived hearth in an older ruin records another kind of activity. The end of the capital’s permanent neighborhoods was therefore not the end of all human connection to the place. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

1010 is the combined modeled median; main text 970–1060 at 95%, abstract 970–1050. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Different practices end at different times

The last permanent houses and the last sampled redware discard have different modeled endings. Redware discard has a median around AD 1040 and a 1010–1080 95 percent interval. A vessel could remain in use, survive as an heirloom or enter later deposits, so this is not a universal last manufacturing date. The center’s contraction also differed from the wider basin’s history. Ceramic practices and raised-field activity continued at Lukurmata and Tiraska, while genomic work finds continuity among a small set of sampled Lukurmata individuals. Continued farming and sampled ancestry do not establish continued imperial government, and neither identifies a community’s language. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

1040 is a modeled last-discard median; regional continuity is discussed without inventing new local dates. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Monuments become an inherited and recorded landscape

An early Spanish description in 1549 belongs to the later history of Tiwanaku’s monumental landscape. By then, its buildings already carried histories of reuse, damage and interpretation. Vranich’s architectural study draws on documentary evidence to explain why displaced and incomplete masonry requires careful reconstruction. The account is evidence of people encountering the remains, rather than an unfiltered picture of the original city. Subsequent looting and movement of blocks further changed what survives, although the study does not date every disturbance. Standing and fallen monuments continued to matter long after the ending of permanent residence in the ancient capital. Source 1 ↗

Documentary year, not an ancient architectural phase. Source 1 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Use corrected current 2023 article, read with 2024 correction; original figures and supplements were replaced.

Residential boundary discrepancy: body 970–1060 95%, abstract 970–1050. Keep it explicit; do not invent a corrected confidence bound.

Model medians 180, 580, 630, 710, 720, 1010 and 1040 are not exact dated events. Bayesian priors, calibration mixture and sample context matter.

Major-monument construction ending near 720 does not contradict later projects using reused stone around 920–970.

Do not infer whole-population disappearance, language or migration motives from seventeen genomes or end of central residence.

The sources do not establish a single conquest or drought as a complete explanation; local violent deaths and climate correlations are not a demonstrated empire-wide mechanism.

Tiwanaku: development of the planned city

UNESCO places Tiwanaku’s development into a planned city between about 400 and 900 CE. Surviving ceremonial architecture supplies the archaeological context for this urban phase.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Brief synthesis: planned city between 400 A.D. and 900 A.D.; overview separately places its apogee at 500–900 AD.
Dating method: Authoritative archaeological heritage synthesis of settlement and architectural development; no individual radiocarbon determinations or calibration model are supplied on this page.

The source is a heritage synthesis, not a sample-level chronology. The reference point generalizes an archaeological zone partly overlain by the modern town.

Tiwanaku — local community evidence

Tiwanaku developed as a planned urban center between approximately 400 and 900 CE. The phase predates the later political decline and does not assert that residence ceased in 900.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximate archaeological phase: 400 to 900; negative years are BCE. See scope below.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; approximate bounds retain the source’s chronological resolution.
