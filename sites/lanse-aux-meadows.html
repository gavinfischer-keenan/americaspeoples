L'Anse aux Meadows and the Norse North Atlantic

On Newfoundland's northern peninsula, across the Strait of Belle Isle from Labrador, turf walls and workshop debris preserve a brief Norse presence. Three cut trees place people here in 1021. The surrounding story reaches east to Greenland and Iceland and south to the sources of materials brought to the site.

Find the place

L'Anse aux Meadows is on the island of Newfoundland, in the Canadian province of Newfoundland and Labrador. The site overlooks Epaves Bay and the Strait of Belle Isle; Labrador lies across the water. Source 1 ↗

Start with the close site plan to see the building complexes, brook and working areas. Then widen the view to the North Atlantic to understand the distances between Iceland, Greenland and Newfoundland. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A precise year, with open questions

An unusual rise in atmospheric radiocarbon in 993 left a recognizable mark in tree rings. Researchers found that mark in three pieces of wood and counted to their outer edges: all three trees were cut in 1021. Source 1 ↗

Metal-tool marks and the archaeological contexts connect the wood to Norse activity. The result does not tell us the first arrival, the last departure, a leader's name or the date of every building. Source 1 ↗

Living and working at the base

Three building complexes contain eight separate remains, including dwellings and work spaces. The nearby brook provided fresh water, while the bog supplied iron ore. This was a place to shelter, make repairs and prepare for movement by sea. Source 1 ↗

Cut and clenched nails, ironworking remains and woodworking debris make boat repair a particularly strong interpretation. Some finds also speak to domestic work, rather than exploration alone. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The communities behind the crossings

Norse settlement in Greenland began around 985 with people arriving from Iceland. Farms stood where freshwater and grazing were available, and their inhabitants combined livestock keeping with hunting. The 'Eastern' and 'Western' settlements both lay on Greenland's west side. Source 1 ↗

Greenland already had a much longer Indigenous history. The atlas's Saqqaq, Independence, Dorset and Inuit records provide that context; medieval Norse farming was one part of the island's human history. Inuit farming from the late eighteenth century was a distinct development. Source 1 ↗

Follow materials without inventing a route

Butternut nuts and worked wood at L'Anse aux Meadows came from farther south, within the plant's range in New Brunswick and the St Lawrence region. They show that material moved beyond Newfoundland. They do not identify a particular southern camp or establish whether collection or exchange brought it north. Source 1 ↗

At Igaliku in Greenland, wood analysis identifies a small imported component from North America and Europe among much larger supplies of local wood and driftwood. The sampled contexts span 1100–1400, so these finds should not be presented as cargo from the 1021 visit. Source 1 ↗

Walrus remains in twelfth-century Kyiv provide a different connection: genetic and archaeological evidence points to the western Arctic and trade through Norse Greenland. The atlas's line joins source and find regions; the intervening transport chain remains unresolved. Source 1 ↗

Indigenous histories and encounters

Indigenous hearths, camps and objects around L'Anse aux Meadows belong to periods before and after the Norse occupation. Their presence makes this a layered human landscape. An object above a later collapsed building cannot by itself prove a meeting with its earlier inhabitants. Source 1 ↗

The sagas recount exchange and conflict with people they called Skrælingar. Archaeology at this site does not securely identify a particular meeting, named Indigenous community or barter transaction in 1021. The butternuts should not be made to carry that unproven story. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Read the sagas beside the archaeology

The Greenlanders' Saga and Erik's Saga were written centuries after the journeys and arrange voyages differently. They are valuable accounts of remembered places and people, but they cannot supply an unquestioned day-by-day itinerary. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Helluland, Markland and Vinland belong to this historical geographic discussion. L'Anse aux Meadows is an excavated Norse site; identifying every saga place with a modern bay, or assigning the camp to a particular leader, remains a separate interpretive step. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Greenland: arrivals, two regional endings, and continuing Inuit history

Norse households reached southwestern Greenland around 985. Both settlements were on the west side: the Western Settlement occupied fjords in the present Nuuk region; the Eastern Settlement lay farther south around the Qassiarsuk, Igaliku and Qaqortoq districts. Their farm histories overlap, but their regional endings differ. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The Western Settlement was abandoned around the mid-1300s. In the Eastern Settlement, the Hvalsey wedding of 16 September 1408 is a dated written event; archaeological evidence continues afterward, supporting an approximate mid-1400s abandonment. The map’s 1350 and 1450 markers summarize those regional interpretations. They do not identify the last person’s departure. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The causes of retreat remain debated. Environmental and economic pressures interacted; the sea-level model cited here proposes another contribution rather than a single explanation. Norse settlement abandonment also does not mean Greenland became uninhabited: the Thule Inuit history and later Greenlandic communities require their own continuing records. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Iceland’s population continues

The Greenland retreat should not make Iceland disappear. Iceland’s settlement began around 870, with people arriving from Norway and Norse communities in the British Isles. Its population continued through the atlas’s 1934 endpoint. Religion, political authority, institutions and daily life changed across that long history; the continuing label is Icelanders, not an unchanged Viking-age culture. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A house, bishopric or assembly can have its own finite dates while the island remains inhabited. The island-wide continuity record therefore sits alongside local archaeology and institutional events. Its end at 1934 is a limit of this atlas’s historical coverage. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Before Norse settlement? The papar tradition

Ari Þorgilsson, writing Íslendingabók around 1122–1133, described earlier Irish Christian residents called papar. His account says they left when pagan newcomers arrived and explains their Irish identity through objects reportedly left behind. This is an important medieval tradition, recorded roughly two and a half centuries after settlement. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The evidence does not provide a secure arrival date, monastery location or sailing track. Scholars discuss Dicuil’s northern islands, names such as Papey, and possible archaeological traces, but the checked sources do not establish a precisely located pre-Norse monastic community in Iceland. The marker therefore dates Ari’s text, while its discussion preserves the possibility of earlier Irish religious voyaging. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

L’Anse aux Meadows — Norse woodworking in 1021 CE

Kuitems and colleagues identified the 993 CE radiocarbon signal in three different trees and counted annual rings to their outer edges. Agreement among the resulting cutting dates anchors Norse activity to 1021.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: AD 1021
Dating method: Annual tree rings matched to the 993 CE radiocarbon anomaly

This precise date does not date the settlement's founding or abandonment. It also does not show that every Norse activity occurred in that year. No arrival route is inferred from this record.
