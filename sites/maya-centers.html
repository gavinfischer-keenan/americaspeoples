Maya centers and networks

These four public archaeological centres belong to a much larger Maya world. Their temples, palaces, inscriptions, reservoirs, and open spaces record distinct dynasties and changing regional relationships, rather than one capital governing an unchanging empire.

Four centres to compare

A site's broad occupation span is not the date of every surviving monument. Buildings could cover earlier structures, and carved texts could commemorate events long before their own dedication. Compare the material sequence, named rulers, and dated inscriptions at each centre before assigning a single founding or ending year to the whole Maya world. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Tikal, GuatemalaOccupation extends from the Preclassic into the early tenth century; the principal monumental ensembles accumulated across many episodes.The Great Plaza, Lost World complex, twin-pyramid groups, palaces, ball courts, causeways, and reservoirs form parts of a much wider settlement.The park boundary, monumental core, and residential landscape have different extents. Reservoirs and peripheral settlements are essential context; visible pyramids are only part of the city.

Palenque, Chiapas, MexicoA major Classic-period capital, especially prominent during the sixth through eighth centuries.The Palace, Temple of the Inscriptions, and Cross Group combine masonry buildings, modeled decoration, and extensive dynastic and mythological texts. Pakal's seventh-century reign is central to its monumental history.A ruler's reign does not date every wall. Tombs, temple superstructures, texts, and later additions need separate archaeological histories.

Copán, HondurasA Classic dynasty began in the fifth century; the centre's political and monumental history changed substantially before early tenth-century abandonment.The Acropolis, plazas, sculpted stelae, and Hieroglyphic Stairway connect public space, royal monuments, and written accounts of dynastic authority.The inscribed stairway is a historical monument as well as architecture. Interpretations must consider displaced blocks and reconstruction, rather than assuming the visible stair text survives entirely in original order.

Chichén Itzá, Yucatán, MexicoMonumental building spans different phases, including important northern ensembles around the Terminal Classic and Early Postclassic transition.El Castillo, the Temple of the Warriors, columned spaces, the Great Ball Court, and El Caracol occupy a landscape with cenotes and earlier building groups.Architectural connections with central Mexico do not by themselves prove a single invasion date or the ethnicity of every builder. Later written traditions and archaeological construction sequences should remain distinguishable.

Tikal: dated Corriental reservoir sediments and filtration interpretation

Corriental reservoir sediments at Tikal contain zeolite and coarse crystalline quartz within an AMS-dated sequence. The authors interpret these minerals as evidence of an ancient water-filtration practice.

Source date guidance: Tankersley et al. report approximately 2185–965 cal yr B.P. for six Corriental AMS ages.
Dating method: AMS radiocarbon dating of charcoal in reservoir sediments, with Bayesian calibration using OxCal 4.3; X-ray diffraction for mineral composition. Calibration curve is not named in the consulted main-text methods; no recalibration performed.

Charcoal ages date sedimentary contexts, not every installation or maintenance event. The proposed filter arrangement is reconstructed; the mapped UNESCO point does not locate the sampled sediments.

Tikal: the 378 CE Entrada and military-intervention interpretation

Epigraphic and archaeological studies interpret Tikal’s Entrada of 378 CE as a military intervention involving Teotihuacan and Maya participants. The marker locates the affected city broadly.

Source date guidance: 378 CE to 378 CE; see claim locators
Dating method: Maya inscription chronology links the Entrada with 378 CE. Epigraphic studies interpret it as military intervention, while archaeological work documents Teotihuacan-related material and architecture at Tikal. No intervening route is reconstructed.

The date identifies an inscribed episode; it does not give the duration or mapped extent of a campaign. Architecture and stylistic similarities do not independently establish conquest, ethnicity, or every participant’s origin.
