Eastern Aleutians · Unalaska Bay · PLACE / COMPLEX

Amaknak Bridge

At Amaknak Bridge in Unalaska Bay, partly sunken houses once lined a knoll above the channel. Unangan builders laid stone walls against the slope, put hearths next to walls and ran narrow channels beneath floors. The houses overlapped as people repaired and replaced them. Those intersections reveal a village changing over generations.

A village built in stages

Excavations in 2000 and 2003 exposed houses on the knoll, and work in 2006 and 2007 examined most of what remained. The later team documented portions of at least 36 structures. At least 14 were complete or nearly complete, and excavators identified 17 hearths or channels. Some structures survive as little more than an inner curve of stones where the hillside sheltered the wall from erosion. Near the top of the knoll, deeper deposits preserve foundations that cut into or sit directly over one another. Excavation plans show the accumulation of these remains across time. Source 1 ↗

The builders dug pits roughly a metre deep and set stone around their edges. Most houses were round or oval and about three to four metres across. A few were rectangular and reached seven metres in length. Construction varied even within the settlement. Structure A was almost round, four metres across and built in courses of small stones above large uprights. One fragment called Structure 10 used horizontal slabs set into layers of sod. The surviving segment is just over three metres long. The complete outlines of many structures and the materials of their roofs remain uncertain. Source 1 ↗

An older structure beside a newer one

Structure 7’s main building measured about six by four metres. It had an east-wall hearth, a shaft rising about 1.5 metres through the wall and a channel branching beneath the floor. Stone slabs and whale vertebrae in lines were interpreted as bases for roof supports. The 2003 excavators interpreted three features on the west as rooms within this house, partly because they found step-ups toward the northwest and southwest spaces. The northwest and west features had their own distinctive construction. The west feature also contained a hearth and a pair of floor channels. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The 2006–2007 excavators returned to these intersections. They found a smaller hearth underneath the main building and older structures cut by its construction. On the north, the wall of Structure 7 and the adjoining northwest wall abutted and partly overlaid Structure A. The pressure had pushed Structure A’s south wall inward. Rogers favored reading the west and northwest structures as separately built, with the southwest feature also predating construction of the main room. The earlier team had already encountered a replacement south wall parallel to the collapsed one. Taken together, the observations establish an order for some rebuilding while leaving the pattern of shared rooms open. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Fuel, draft and work at floor level

The hearths sit between the stone walls and the center of household activity. In larger houses, a channel from the hearth sometimes branched into two arms, often under slabs. Structure 63 had two parallel paths from the hearth along its long axis. Structure V had a wall shaft and a channel system that split into smaller branches. The excavators considered how the channels might draw air to the fire or distribute warmth. Charcoal and hearths show that fires burned inside the houses. The buried parts of the installation leave room for different interpretations of how smoke, air and heat moved. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

People also used the space under floors for storage. Structure 2 had a slab-covered storage pit alongside its hearth system, and Structure 3 preserved caches of cooking stones and a bundle of albatross long bones. The investigators thought the latter could have supplied material for needles. Within Structure 3, posts supported the roof near the center of the room. These floor traces show the labor of furnishing and maintaining a house in a setting where exposed wood and bone seldom keep their original form. Source 1 ↗

What the dates and animal remains show

Charcoal from Structure 7 West has a published calibrated range of 1950–1620 BCE. Charcoal from the main room has a range of 1280–820 BCE. Structure 2’s floor charcoal falls in 1770–1520 BCE, Structure 5’s in 1410–1010 BCE, and Structure 3’s hearth sample in 910–420 BCE. Floor, hearth and fill are different deposits. A branch could have died before someone burned it, while an already-empty pit could have filled with refuse. Crossing walls and overlapping floors offer an independent way to order events that charcoal alone cannot resolve. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Shell-rich refuse preserved bones of cod, seabirds, porpoises, seals and other animals. Some of the ringed seal bones were identified as belonging to recently weaned animals. Crockford and Frederick inferred access to seasonal pack ice in spring or early summer. Knecht and Davis also discussed open-water fishing and hunting, based on the animals present and their ages. Those seasonal signals show the range of waters and weather conditions residents had to know. The village’s stonework records another part of that knowledge, built into floors that people returned to, repaired and sometimes replaced. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Amaknak Bridge — seals and seasonal sea ice

Ringed seal bones in house and midden deposits include many recently weaned animals. The zooarchaeologists infer access to spring or early summer pack ice, alongside hunting in open water during other seasons.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Published charcoal-date envelope 3922–2358 cal BP; approximately 1973–409 BCE
Dating method: Outer envelope of calibrated charcoal ranges reported by Rogers; not a new pooled radiocarbon model.

Animal bones date to excavated archaeological contexts and act as indirect climate evidence. The site envelope supplies the calendar context. The faunal study reports the occupation in uncalibrated radiocarbon years.

Chaluka — a stone-walled house and its work areas

One excavated house at Chaluka had a stone perimeter, slab-lined hearth and depressions below the floor. Clusters of pebbles, awls and needles hint at tasks organized along the walls and near the pits.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Reported assays from material in the house: 4028 ± 100 to 3220 ± 52 radiocarbon BP
Dating method: Published uncalibrated range of separate dates reported by Rogers from Aigner. Calendar limits were unavailable in the consulted material.

Six rock walls were identified at the site. Detailed observations refer to the excavated house. The associated samples leave the building’s calendar lifespan unresolved.

Amaknak Bridge — stone houses and elaborate hearths

On a knoll above the channel, builders terraced stone houses, repaired walls and laid new floors and hearths across older ones. Most houses were round or oval, about 3–4 metres across. A few were larger and rectangular.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Published charcoal-date envelope 3922–2358 cal BP; approximately 1973–409 BCE
Dating method: Outer envelope of calibrated charcoal ranges reported by Rogers; not a new pooled radiocarbon model.

The envelope spans samples from different contexts. It does not date every structure or establish continuous occupation throughout the whole range.
