A closer reading

How water and stone made an eel-farming landscape

Channels, weirs and dams worked with wetlands to manage aquatic resources, including kooyang, or short-finned eels. At Muldoons, excavation distinguishes successive construction episodes. The system depended on relationships among features and water conditions, so an isolated wall makes more sense when read within the larger engineered setting.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗ · academia.edu [2] ↗

Budj Bim’s volcanic terrain supplied the ground on which that history unfolded. The eruption, the earliest dated deposits in a channel and later wall construction are different events. Their separate chronologies make human decisions visible within a much older landscape. Gunditjmara knowledge and continuing relationships to Country extend beyond what any one excavated date can describe.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗ · sciencedirect.com [2] ↗ · gunditjmirring.com [3] ↗

The aquaculture system worked by changing how water moved across the lava landscape. Builders used local volcanic stone for barriers and channels, guiding flows into places where kooyang could be trapped and retained. Storage mattered alongside capture: keeping eels alive extended the usefulness of a harvest beyond the moment when animals entered a trap. The network developed over millennia of alteration and maintenance. Its productivity depended on knowledge of wetlands and eel movement as well as the physical labor of shifting stone.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗

Kurtonitj shows these relationships at a human scale. The community’s site account describes two weirs and a dam within an area of lava flow, together with channels feeding swampy depressions. One channel runs for about seventy metres and is partly lined with basalt walls. Another barrier held floodwater in a larger depression, creating a place to retain kooyang. These connected features turned naturally uneven ground into managed water spaces. A channel was useful because of the wetland and retaining structures with which it worked.

Evidence: budjbim.com.au [1] ↗

Food processing and residence belonged to the same landscape. Kurtonitj preserves stone house sites and trees associated with smoking eels, alongside aquaculture features. This arrangement connects the capture of food with keeping, preparing and consuming it. It also helps explain why settlement cannot be separated from engineering here. The surviving stonework marks repeated choices about where to live and how to use water. The community identifies Kurtonitj as a crossing place with continuing cultural significance, at the centre of the broader Budj Bim landscape.

Evidence: budjbim.com.au [1] ↗

World Heritage recognition in 2019 brought international attention to a place whose cultural importance had long been maintained by Gunditjmara people. The nomination followed land return and decades of advocacy by Traditional Owners. That modern history forms another layer in the landscape. Channels and house sites now also support education about Aboriginal engineering, while continuing community knowledge gives meaning to the physical remains. The long use of this Country and the recent work of recognition belong to the same history of care and responsibility.

Evidence: digital-classroom.nma.gov.au [1] ↗

Country provides the setting; engineering has its own chronology

Budj Bim’s lava landscape gave later communities a particular terrain of rock and water. The eruption, the earliest dated channel infill and later wall building are different events. None should be used as a substitute date for the others. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Reading a system rather than an isolated wall

Channels, weirs and dams worked together with wetlands to manage and harvest aquatic resources, including kooyang, short-finned eels. Excavation at Muldoons identifies successive local construction episodes; the whole landscape cannot be assigned one uniform building year. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Three components, broad public references

The World Heritage property comprises a northern component around Tae Rak, central Kurtonitj and southern Tyrendarra. The public component coordinates orient the reader within a large cultural landscape. They do not identify individual channels, private places or excavation grids. Source 1 ↗
