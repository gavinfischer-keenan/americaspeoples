Greenland / Kalaallit Nunaat — homelands, farms and colonial harbours

Greenland has several intersecting human histories. Norse farms are one part of an Arctic landscape occupied by earlier peoples and by Inuit whose histories continue. Follow camps, fjords, exchange, changing settlement and colonial institutions without turning the island into a single European possession on every date.

Earliest supported regional evidence through 1900. Modern discovery, conservation and reference-map information retain their actual dates. This is selected evidence, not a complete census of every community or event.

The whole area in view

The whole regional area, with modern terrain and coastlines. Point dates refer to historical evidence; the relief does not reconstruct ancient ice, sea level or political control.

  1. Norse Eastern Settlement, southwestern Greenland

    Early eleventh-century context; short-lived archaeological samples at L'Anse aux Meadows broadly 990–1050, with cutting activity independently fixed to 1021

    Norse seafarers connected Greenland's settlements with L'Anse aux Meadows on Newfoundland's northern peninsula. The line is a broad source-region connection. It does not date arrival to 1021, identify the crew as Leif's, or confirm a sequence of Labrador/Baffin landfalls. The 990–1050 window brackets context, not a sixty-year voyage. [16] [19]

    61.15°, -45.52°

  2. Igaliku/Garðar, southern Greenland

    Igaliku wood contexts: phase 1, 1100–1300 CE; phase 2, 1300–1400 CE

    Small amounts of imported wood reached the bishop's farm at Igaliku alongside local wood and driftwood. A particular American forest, landing, ship, intermediary or annual shipment cannot be identified. The later phase contains potentially mixed deposits. No named transatlantic route is plotted from this point. [15]

    60.99°, -45.42°

  3. Western Greenland and eastern Canadian Arctic walrus-source region

    Mid- and late-twelfth-century contexts, shown conservatively at century resolution

    Walrus remains excavated in Kyiv connect its ivory supply with the western Arctic and Norse Greenland's trade network. The biological source is a wide region. Norse Greenland transit is an archaeological-historical inference; individual hunters, intermediaries, European ports and the complete route to Kyiv are not identified. [14]

    70.00°, -55.00°

  4. Qassiarsuk/Brattahlíð and the Eastern Settlement

    Circa 985 to the mid-fifteenth century, regional Eastern Settlement chronology

    Norse farming around Qassiarsuk belongs to the Eastern Settlement, whose regional occupation continued into the mid-fifteenth century. The 1450 marker is an inferred, approximate regional abandonment anchor. It is neither the proven date of the last departure nor a dated ending for each Qassiarsuk farm. [17] [18] [11] [13]

    61.15°, -45.52°

  5. Jørgen Brønlund Fjord, Peary Land

    circa 2400–1800 BCE; museum chronology for the principal Peary Land phase

    Early communities camped around Jørgen Brønlund Fjord and hunted muskoxen, seals and birds. The dates describe a regional archaeological phase. Scattered later determinations do not justify continuous occupation of every camp. This does not identify Greenland's first individual landing. [6]

    82.20°, -30.50°

  6. Qeqertasussuk, southern Disko Bay

    circa 2400–1400 BCE, the site-study chronology cited by the museum

    Qeqertasussuk preserves tools, wood and animal remains from Saqqaq people living from the rich resources of Disko Bay. This broad site phase is not continuous residence by a single household, and the modern point is deliberately generalized. [7]

    68.60°, -51.20°

  7. Nipisat island, south of Sisimiut

    2020–1740, 1860–1325 and 1310–810 cal BCE occupation phases

    Nipisat's hearths and animal remains record repeated seasonal visits by Saqqaq hunters over more than a millennium. The problematic 520 BCE determination is excluded. The encompassing interval is not year-round or uninterrupted residence. [5]

    66.80°, -53.50°

  8. Qilakitsoq, Uummannaq area

    circa 1475 CE

    Two graves at Qilakitsoq preserve the clothing and personal histories of eight Inuit people. The approximate date concerns these burials; it does not date all houses at Qilakitsoq, identify the cause of death, or imply an end to Inuit life. [9]

    70.60°, -52.10°

  9. Historic harbour, Nuuk

    1728, construction date in the protected-building register

    Hans Egede's house at Nuuk dates to 1728, marking a durable colonial mission establishment on this inhabited coast. The house's construction is not the beginning of Indigenous occupation. The 1721 mission is a different locality. [10]

    64.17°, -51.74°

  10. Igaliku, southern Greenland

    1782, family relocation to Igaliku

    Tuperna, Anders Olsen and their family established themselves at Igaliku in 1782 after farming at Upernaviarsuk. This is a dated family relocation and farming anchor; it does not imply direct continuity between the medieval Norse and eighteenth-century farms. [8]

    60.99°, -45.42°

  11. Historic harbour, Nuuk

    1850, construction date in the protected-building register

    A provision store built at Nuuk in 1850 anchors the town's harbour-based supply and trade economy. The building date alone does not establish a specific cargo, overseas source port or trade volume. [10]

    64.17°, -51.74°

  12. Western Settlement, inner fjords of the present Nuuk region

    Late tenth / early eleventh century to approximately the middle of the fourteenth century

    The Western Settlement has both an approximate beginning and an approximate abandonment, around a century before the Eastern Settlement. The point represents a fjord region, not modern Nuuk town or a single excavated farm. No destination is inferred for departing residents. [17] [13] [11]

    64.30°, -50.50°

  13. Hvalsey church / Qaqortukulooq, Eastern Settlement

    16 September 1408

    A precisely dated written event within a settlement whose final abandonment is less precisely known. No departure track or final population count is supplied. The exact date belongs to the wedding; it should not lend false day-level precision to settlement decline. [12] [11]

    60.83°, -45.78°

  14. Disko Bay

    About 600 BCE–200 CE in the Disko Bay middens

    Dorset-associated tools and dwelling evidence record another chapter of life around Disko Bay. [1]

    69.20°, -51.20°

  15. Dove Bugt

    About 200 BCE to the start of the Common Era

    Dove Bugt preserves coastal finds related to the Independence II archaeological tradition. [2]

    76.60°, -20.60°

  16. Northwest Greenland / Thule region

    Around 1200 CE

    The ancestors of present-day Greenlanders reached Greenland around 1200. Approximate museum chronology; no exact landing year or traced route is known. [3]

    76.60°, -69.00°

  17. Kangeq / Håbets Ø

    1721 CE

    Hans Egede’s party landed in Greenland on 3 July 1721. Approximate island anchor; the marker does not imply Danish control of the whole island. [4]

    64.12°, -52.13°

  18. Nipisat Sound near Kangeq

    1722 CE

    Egede recorded large groups traveling north in women’s boats in April 1722. A contemporary observation of people passing, not an annual population count or an exact boat track. [4]

    64.10°, -52.10°

  19. Ivittuut

    Extraction from 1854, displayed through 1900

    Mining at Ivittuut connected Greenland to industrial demand for cryolite. [4]

    61.21°, -48.17°

  20. Peary Land and adjacent northern Greenland

    About 800–600 calibrated BCE

    Early Independence II dates occur in Peary Land and northeastern Greenland. [2]

    82.25°, -29.70°

  21. Northwestern Greenland, Nares Strait reference

    About 1100–1300 CE

    Separate Late Dorset evidence occurs in northwestern Greenland in a later period than the Disko Bay horizon. [1] [20]

    78.20°, -68.80°

  22. Kalaallit Nunaat / Greenland, island-wide reference

    From around 1200 CE, continuing after 1900

    The ancestors of present-day Greenlandic Inuit communities reached Greenland around 1200, and Inuit communities continue beyond this timeline. [3] [21]

    70.00°, -42.50°

    1894 CE

Start with the Arctic evidence

The early camp sites in this guide precede the Norse by millennia. Qeqertasussuk preserves unusually rich organic material, while Nipisat records repeated visits within a dated sequence. Such finds tell us about technologies and resource use without supplying a continuous population count. [7]

Cultures are archaeological categories

Early Dorset and Independence II describe patterns in material evidence. The Disko Bay midden chronology covers approximately 600 BCE to 200 CE. Earlier Independence II evidence near 800–600 BCE belongs to northern contexts, while Dove Bugt has some younger dates. A later Dorset record in the High Arctic belongs to another period. Archaeological categories and dated finds do not give an annual population history. [1] [2] [20]

Thule ancestors and living Greenlanders

The Greenland National Museum places the arrival of Thule ancestors around 1200. This was a broad Arctic movement associated with hunting, technology and travel. Inuit community life continued into the colonial period and beyond 1900. Regional histories include changes in where people lived and how they moved. This continuity is independent of the ending of Norse farming settlements in southwest Greenland. [3] [21]

Eastern and Western are names within southwest Greenland

Both Norse settlement districts lie in the southwest. The Western Settlement was in the Nuuk fjords; the Eastern Settlement lay farther south around present-day Kujataa. Their names do not describe settlements on the island’s opposite coasts. [17]

Farms, church and maritime work

Farming, herding, hunting and imported materials supported Norse households. Church sites and wealthy farms were connected to smaller holdings. Greenland’s museum presents these as communities with varied work, not simply as exploration bases. [13]

What the retreat dates actually mean

Around 1350 and around 1450 are useful regional abandonment anchors for the Western and Eastern settlements. They are not known last-departure days. Hvalsey’s wedding in 1408 is a surviving written event; occupation evidence continued after that document. The movie retains the earlier places while identifying the loss of supported Norse occupation. [17]

Different kinds of explanation

Changes in climate, relative sea level, grazing, maritime access and exchange are investigated with different evidence. They should not be compressed into one demonstrated cause of the settlements’ end. A regional decline also need not have happened identically at every farm. [11]

Clothes and households at Qilakitsoq

The preserved people at Qilakitsoq make clothing and family histories unusually tangible. Their approximate fifteenth-century date belongs to Inuit history, not the chronology of Norse abandonment. The separate cold-preservation study examines what these remains can and cannot tell us. [9]

A colony enters an inhabited landscape

The 1721 venture and the later Nuuk buildings mark new colonial institutions. Egede’s own account also witnessed substantial Indigenous movement near Kangeq. A colonial arrival therefore belongs beside an existing network of gathering places and travel. [4]

Nineteenth-century work and continuity

The provision store at Nuuk, farming at Igaliku and cryolite at Ivittuut represent distinct economic histories. Tasiilaq’s 1894 trade and mission station altered conditions for existing Tunumiit communities in the east. These dates identify institutions or workplaces. The 1900 display ceiling does not describe the ending of the mine or any community. [10] [8] [4] [21]
