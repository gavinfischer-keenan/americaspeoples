Scotland’s standing stones, settlements and tombs

At Stenness, tall uprights stand inside a ditch. At Loanhead of Daviot, two upright stones frame a slab laid on its side. At Clava, rings of standing stones surround burial cairns. Each design belongs to a particular landscape and history. The map brings together examples from the Scottish mainland and islands, including places where houses, fields and burials help explain how people lived alongside the monuments.

A comparative guide to selected prehistoric sites, principally fourth- to second-millennium BCE settlements, tombs and standing stones. Individual guides explore the local sequences and selected later occupations.

Several traditions developed over time

A henge is an enclosure defined by a bank and a ditch. It can contain a stone circle, as at Stenness, while other stone circles stand without a henge. A cairn is a mound of stones. Some cairns cover chambers that were entered through passages. Others cover small stone boxes, called cists, or deposits of cremated remains. At sites like Clava and Temple Wood these categories overlap because builders combined different forms or changed the monument over time. [1] [10] [5] [13]

Regional forms make different parts of a circle prominent

The recumbent circles of northeast Scotland emphasize a horizontal block set between tall flankers, usually in the southern or southwestern arc. Their standing stones often decrease in height away from that side. At Clava, the stones surround cairns and reflect a different approach to the setting of the dead, although the grading of stones invites comparison. Farther west, Calanais uses a long northern approach and shorter rows extending out from the circle. At Machrie Moor the builders used different stones and arrangements even within one group of monuments. [2] [5] [12] [6]

Burial, farming and movement belong on the same map

Excavating a stone setting can expose traces of what people did before or after it was built. The chambers of Cairn Holy preserve an early funerary tradition, while at Loanhead cremations occur both within the wider circle complex and in a separate enclosure. At Machrie Moor, houses and field systems place farming close to the monuments. Those spatial associations matter even when several centuries separated the activities. [8] [3] [6]

The sea gave some communities access to other shores as well as a boundary. Work on past sea levels around Lewis and Argyll shows that today’s coastlines are a poor substitute for a reconstruction of Neolithic approaches. Comparing a place like Calanais with Kilmartin, where some motifs resemble examples from Ireland, can suggest connections worth investigating. The resemblance alone leaves the routes, dates and people involved open. [15] [13]

How archaeology dates a stone setting

Radiocarbon dating usually measures material found around stones, such as charcoal or bone in a socket or ditch. Archaeologists use the position of the sample to decide whether it dates construction, an earlier surface or later use. A gap between two phases can disappear when a site is summarized in a single date. Surveys can add features to the map too. In 2026 a team recorded an oval arrangement of buried anomalies at Machrie Moor, but only excavation could establish what occupied those pits. Even when the orientation of an existing setting is measurable, its significance to the builders remains a separate question. [1] [11] [7]
