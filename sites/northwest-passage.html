Northwest Passage: waterways, Inuit knowledge and the long search

The Northwest Passage crosses a whole Arctic world: the Greenland approaches, Lancaster Sound, branching channels around great islands, the Beaufort coast and Bering Strait. Inuit knowledge, trading relationships, winter camps and separate sledge journeys explain the history as much as the ships do. Explore the full maps, then follow the evidence from the early Atlantic approaches to Franklin, the searches and Gjøa. Modern seasonal measurements appear in their own sections.

An inhabited Arctic: knowledge, trade and translation

Inuit lived, hunted and traveled around King William Island long before the British expedition arrived. Sea ice could be a traveling surface and a hunting landscape, as well as an obstacle to a sailing ship. The label “Northwest Passage” describes an overseas quest through places that already had names, histories and skilled inhabitants. Source 1 ↗

The Franklin searches depended on people who observed the crews, found objects, remembered places and conveyed accounts between languages. William Ouligbuck interpreted for Rae. Rae’s 1854 report describes two Inuit travelers guiding his party and Mistegan making a separate reconnaissance. Those observations belong to Rae’s survey, not to a reconstructed route for Franklin’s survivors. Source 1 ↗

Taqulittuq and Ipiivik were essential interpreters in Charles Francis Hall’s work. In 1869 Inukpujijuk described a wreck near the Adelaide Peninsula and marked its location for Hall, including in a map of his own. Ipiivik also assisted Schwatka’s 1878–80 search. Treating these people as named sources makes the search intelligible: information moved through families, journeys and conversations, not only through naval dispatches. Source 1 ↗

The western Arctic has its own connected history. Inuvialuit traveled to the abandoned Investigator cache at Mercy Bay and reused materials for decades. Those objects later appeared at sites across the region. The vessel’s history therefore continued within Inuvialuit networks after the naval crew had left. Source 1 ↗

From Atlantic approaches to wintering voyages, 1585–1825

Davis’s voyages of 1585–87 explored Greenland and the eastern Canadian approaches, with encounters and exchanges with Inuit. The 1616 voyage of Bylot and Baffin examined the northern end of Baffin Bay and the entrances of Smith, Jones and Lancaster sounds. The atlas preserves selected dated observations from those voyages; it does not turn an inlet sighting into a transcontinental route. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

In 1819 Parry’s Hecla and Griper sailed through Lancaster Sound and Barrow Strait to Melville Island. They wintered at Winter Harbour, where provisions, organized work, a newspaper and performances helped sustain the crews. When westward progress remained blocked the following summer, Parry returned. Reaching farther west was a major survey achievement, but the remaining connection was still unresolved. Source 1 ↗

Parry’s next expeditions show why a single line across a map is misleading. In 1821–23 he searched by Hudson Strait and Foxe Basin, learned from Inuit about a western connection, and encountered ice in Fury and Hecla Strait. In 1824–25 he tried Prince Regent Inlet. Fury was damaged and abandoned, and Hecla carried both crews home. Its stores at Fury Beach later supported other explorers. Source 1 ↗

Franklin: what the surviving dates actually establish

Erebus and Terror left England in May 1845 and continued from Greenland with 129 men. They wintered at Beechey Island in 1845–46. The surviving Victory Point record then places the expedition’s crisis near King William Island: ice entrapment beginning 12 September 1846, Franklin’s death on 11 June 1847, and abandonment of the ships on 22 April 1848. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The annotation dated 25 April 1848 reports 105 survivors under Crozier and an intention to depart for Back’s Fish River. It is a document at a particular place and time, not a daily log of the retreat. Its earlier text also gives the Beechey winter the wrong years. The atlas keeps the firm dates, the geographic uncertainty and the gaps visible instead of manufacturing a smooth final voyage. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Rae’s 1854 report brought Inuit accounts and expedition relics into the British investigation. His informants distinguished what they had heard from what they had seen; the report itself says the people he questioned had received the account from others. Later searchers added testimony, objects and the note found by Hobson in 1859. These sources overlap without resolving every survivor’s movements. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

McClure: a crossing by ships and sledges, 1850–54

Investigator approached from the Pacific in 1850, passed Point Barrow and Cape Bathurst, wintered in Prince of Wales Strait and later became trapped in Mercy Bay on Banks Island. McClure’s 1852 sledge party reached Winter Harbour on Melville Island and left a record that helped the searchers connect the two sides of the archipelago. Source 1 ↗

Relief did not produce a continuous ship passage. The crew left Investigator in June 1853, joined Resolute and Intrepid, and the following spring sledged onward toward Beechey Island before returning in other ships. The atlas treats the ship, the sledge survey and the evacuation as distinct movements. McClure’s trans-Arctic crossing and Gjøa’s later passage in one vessel answer different historical questions. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Gjøa: a small ship, three winters, 1903–06

Amundsen’s expedition took a small converted fishing vessel and six companions into the archipelago. Gjøa reached its harbor on King William Island in September 1903 and remained for two winters. Magnetic observations were a central purpose of the stay, alongside learning and practicing Arctic travel, clothing and hunting techniques with local Inuit. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The harbor stay was not inactivity. Sledge parties worked away from the ship, and the expedition map distinguishes outward and return surveys from Gjøa’s track. The photographs bring the contrast into view: a sheltered vessel, a sledge party, and Nattilik hunters with equipment suited to traveling and hunting on ice. Amundsen’s publication records his perspective; it does not replace the knowledge and voices of the people who taught the visitors. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Gjøa departed Gjoa Haven on 13 August 1905, continued west through the southern waterways, and spent another winter at King Point. The ship reached Nome in August 1906, completing the first Northwest Passage in one vessel and one expedition. Accounts differ on the 1906 resumption of sailing, and the Fram Museum’s pages give different Nome dates, 30 and 31 August. The atlas retains that disagreement rather than hiding it in an invented itinerary. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

This historical account stays within the atlas’s endpoint of 1934. The reference maps and environmental tables below are later sources used to understand the geography and seasonal constraints. They do not describe the weather encountered by a particular historical crew. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Modern seasonal context: different waterways open at different times

The Canadian Ice Service’s 1991–2020 normals give median shipping seasons, with substantial regional differences. Old ice can remain or drift into the archipelago even in summer. In particular, ice moving south from M’Clintock Channel can obstruct the southern route near Larsen Sound. These are modern climatological summaries, not a reconstruction of 1845 or 1903 and not a forecast. Source 1 ↗
