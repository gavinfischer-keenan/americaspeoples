One journey across several regions

The dated lists retain each itinerary’s original descriptions. A documented landfall, a roadstead stay and a passage offshore are different kinds of evidence. Where the source leaves a gap, a linking animation is an illustration rather than a recovered daily ship track. [1] [2] [3]

Read departures and returns together

The same port can occur more than once in a journey, and several expeditions can share a harbor. Keeping those visits separate makes it possible to follow preparation, outbound movement and return without turning a port into one timeless event. [1] [2] [3]

3. Archival Coverage, Monsoon / Seasonal Constraints & Limits of Sea-Lane Maps

Historical route lines connecting Porto Praia, Santiago, La Gomera, departure, Funchal, Madeira aggregate recurring seasonal sailing tracks, diplomatic dispatches, and coastal provisioning harbors documented in Africa and the Atlantic islands: linked voyages rather than claiming every vessel followed identical GPS coordinates in every year. [1]

Unequal archival survival—where European admiralty logs or monumental stone harbors outnumber wooden dhow, outrigger, and domestic reed-papyrus records—makes comparative archaeological shipwrecks and bilingual inscriptions essential for reading ocean history evenly. [2]

Schouten and Le Maire · Eendracht around Cape Horn to Jacatra

Eendracht (called Unitie in the English account) sailed with Hoorn from the Netherlands to Patagonia. After Hoorn burned at Puerto Deseado, Eendracht passed the strait between Tierra del Fuego and Isla de los Estados and rounded Cape Horn. The Pacific and East Indies stages end at Jacatra, where the VOC seized the ship. The return aboard Amsterdam is a separate itinerary.

Newport marks the circumnavigation’s return on 27 June 1898; the final 3 July Fairhaven homecoming is also shown. Captain Howard accompanied the local Montevideo–Buenos Aires excursion described in the book.
