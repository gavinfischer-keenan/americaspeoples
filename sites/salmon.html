A great house beside the San Juan River

Salmon Pueblo occupies a river-valley setting north of Chaco Canyon. Around 1090, builders created an E-shaped great house with multistory rooms and two prominent communal structures: the elevated Tower Kiva in the central block and the Great Kiva in the plaza. The present name comes from a later landholding family; descendant communities know the place through other names and histories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Its two broad archaeological phases organize the evidence: Chacoan, about 1090–1125, and San Juan, about 1125–1280. These labels help compare deposits and alterations. They should not be read as a vacant interval or as proof that all residents changed at one moment. Source 1 ↗

Two kivas with different histories

The Tower Kiva and Great Kiva are not interchangeable names. Unit 64W stood on a raised central platform; Unit 130W occupied the open plaza to its south. Their different forms, deposits, and rebuilding histories let the guide connect the visible plan with specific archaeological observations. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A date is only as direct as its evidence. The archive reports tree-ring support for the Tower Kiva’s first building episode. For the Great Kiva, the early date is inferred from context because no tree-ring date marks its initial construction. Both appear around 1090 on the clock, but their confidence explanations differ. Room 96W shows change within an existing place

Room 96W began as a square Chacoan chamber and was converted into a round kiva toward the end of that phase. Later additions included more of the features associated with San Juan kivas. The circular insertion created small spaces between its walls and those of the original square room. Source 1 ↗

This is a useful local example because it makes change tangible without inventing a migration event. A published synthesis places the conversion in the 1120s, while the archive explicitly reports that no tree-ring dates came from the room. Its mapped location therefore remains the great-house complex anchor, and the chronology retains its interpretive status. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A new Great Kiva roof in 1263

The Great Kiva supplies a particularly clear late construction observation: its roof was replaced in spring or summer 1263. The archive describes hundreds of beams cut over nearly twenty years. Timber age therefore records procurement as well as the moment those materials entered the rebuilt roof. Source 1 ↗

Later work also included refacing interior masonry and constructing a surrounding cobble berm. A broader research synthesis discusses flooding in the river-side settlement and interprets the berm as flood protection; the unit summary emphasizes structural support. These functions can be discussed together, but the precise date of every part of the berm is not established by the roof date. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Fire, migration, and a living ancestral place

An intentional fire ended the Tower Kiva’s final use in the late thirteenth century, according to SPARC. The burning belongs to the end of the San Juan occupation, but it does not establish warfare. Source 1 ↗

Many Tribes and Nations maintain ancestral connections to Salmon Pueblo. SPARC itself acknowledges that its present archaeological archive does not supply those communities’ interpretations and histories. It is a valuable record of excavation and research, not a substitute for descendant voices or a basis for assigning one uniform migration route to every household. Source 1 ↗

Use the Four Corners guide for the regional story, and compare Salmon with Aztec and Chaco as separate local studies. Their connections matter, but their different building tempos and patterns of reuse remain visible when each place keeps its own chronology. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Salmon Great House — major Chacoan construction

A large E-shaped great house was built around 1090 on the north side of the San Juan River, with multistory room blocks, an elevated central kiva, and a detached plaza Great Kiva.

Dates and evidence notes

Dating method: Published tree-ring, architectural, stratigraphic, and institutional chronology at the source’s stated precision.

1090 is an approximate major-construction anchor, not the date of every surviving wall. Proposed earlier preparatory work and astronomical design dates are not treated as established construction. Approximate great-house complex anchor, about 75 m scope. Room 96W and distributed remodeling reuse this point; individual room centers are not asserted.

Salmon Tower Kiva — initial construction

The central Tower Kiva, excavation unit 64W, was raised on a platform to the second-story level during Salmon’s first Chacoan building episode.

Dates and evidence notes

Salmon Great House — smaller rooms and inserted kivas

Later occupants subdivided large Chacoan rooms and installed numerous small kivas in rooms and the plaza. More than twenty had been added by the middle of the thirteenth century.

Dates and evidence notes

Salmon Great Kiva — roof replacement in 1263

The Great Kiva’s later occupants replaced its roof in spring or summer 1263, drawing on hundreds of beams cut over nearly twenty years. They also refaced the interior and built a surrounding cobble berm.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Roof replaced in spring or summer 1263 CE.
Dating method: Published tree-ring, architectural, stratigraphic, and institutional chronology at the source’s stated precision.

1263 is the archive’s dated roof-replacement observation. It does not date all berm construction, the first Great Kiva, or the end of use. Stored or reused beams demonstrate why timber cutting and building events must be separated. Approximate public named-structure anchor; allow about 30 m. No surveyed footprint asserted.

Salmon Tower Kiva — final use and burning in the 1280s

The Tower Kiva remained in use into the late thirteenth century and ended with an intentional fire. The archive places this ending with the close of the San Juan occupation; it does not establish a battle.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Tower Kiva used until the 1280s; final fire at end of San Juan occupation.
Dating method: Published tree-ring, architectural, stratigraphic, and institutional chronology at the source’s stated precision.

The 1280–1289 display window represents the stated decade, not nine years of continuous use or an exact fire year. Burning is not coded as warfare or a violent-death marker. Continuing descendant relationships are distinct from the end of residential use. Approximate central-core anchor; allow about 35 m. The point is the public mapped core containing the Tower Kiva, not a surveyed kiva center.
