Dravidian & Austroasiatic Languages Across South and Mainland SE Asia

Two deeply rooted Eurasian families anchor South and Southeast Asia’s earliest agricultural landscapes: Dravidian, whose Sangam Tamil poetry rivals Sanskrit antiquity while leaving Brahui spoken far off in Balochistan, and Austroasiatic, linking Munda hill farmers in eastern India with Mon, Khmer Angkor, and Vietnamese.

Connects southern Indian Tamil-Brahmi archaeology, Bayesian Dravidian phylogenetics, and mainland Southeast Asian Neolithic–Bronze excavations.

Deep south Asian roots and mainland river valleys

Bayesian phylogenetic analysis places Proto-Dravidian diversification in South Asia around 4,000–4,500 years BP, aligning with early southern Neolithic pulse-millet farmers whose geographic footprint later absorbed and traded vocabulary with arriving Indo-Aryan branches. [1] [4]

Eastward across the Bay of Bengal and Chota Nagpur hills, Austroasiatic speakers linked Munda rice communities of eastern India with Mon brick kingdoms, Khmer Angkor hydraulic cities, and Red River wet-rice farmers of northern Vietnam. [2] [3]

Sangam poetry, Brahui outlier puzzle, and script borrowing

Classic Old Tamil poetry composed across Early Historic Vaigai–Kaveri assemblies preserves indigenous Dravidian ecological genres (akam and puram), while the isolated presence of Brahui in Pakistani Balochistan underscores deep western subcontinental Dravidian reach prior to Indo-Aryan shifts. [1]

Epigraphic crossroads in Mainland Southeast Asia

From Old Mon inscriptions at Dvaravati and Bagan to Old Khmer vernacular tax records carved directly beside royal Sanskrit verses at Angkor, Austroasiatic languages carried hydraulic agrarian administration across Indochina centuries before modern national borders. [2] [3]
