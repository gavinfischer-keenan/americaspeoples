Hannibal: the campaign and the elephants

Hannibal’s war crossed cities, mountains and political alliances. Its elephants are famous; what can be established about their route, survival and material traces requires much closer reading.

Selected Second Punic War episodes, 219–202 BCE, with earlier Punic urban and elephant-bone context. No exact Alpine pass or Zama battlefield is asserted; no population is inferred from troop or casualty totals.

The whole area in view

Approximate evidence localities and broad campaign regions. Modern terrain is a geographic aid, not a reconstruction of historical coasts, roads or boundaries.

  1. Qart Hadast: the Punic fortified town

    229–209 BCE

    The surviving Punic wall gives material evidence for the fortified city from which the Barcid campaigns were supplied. Foundation in 229 BCE follows the site museum’s chronology; the interval is a selected Punic phase, ending with Scipio’s capture, not the entire life of Cartagena. [9]

    Map reference: 37.606°, -0.978°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  2. Saguntum: siege and the road to war

    219 BCE

    Hannibal’s siege of Saguntum preceded the invasion of Italy. The city anchor does not trace the siege works. Ancient narratives frame responsibility for war from partisan perspectives. [3]

    Map reference: 39.68°, -0.277°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  3. Rhône crossing: people, boats and elephants

    218 BCE

    Polybius describes a contested river passage and special preparations for transporting elephants. The exact crossing site is disputed. This lower-Rhône anchor and the animated line locate a broad episode, not a known ford. [3]

    Map reference: 44.12°, 4.68°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  4. Western Alps: the unresolved pass

    218 BCE

    The army crossed the Western Alps and entered northern Italy; the identification of the pass remains debated. The marker is deliberately regional. Neither the Traversette nor Clapier hypothesis is presented as established, and no elephant-bone identification secures the route. [3] [7]

    Map reference: 45.1°, 6.8°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  5. Taurini: force after the crossing

    218 BCE

    Polybius reports an attack on the Taurini after the descent into Italy. The Turin-area marker approximates an ancient community’s locality; it does not identify an excavated assault horizon. [3]

    Map reference: 45.07°, 7.68°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  6. Ticinus: the cavalry encounter

    218 BCE

    A cavalry action brought Hannibal into contact with the consul Publius Cornelius Scipio. The broader Ticino/Po landscape is shown; the exact battlefield is unresolved and the river system has changed. [3]

    Map reference: 45.2°, 8.85°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  7. Trebia: battle in the river plain

    218 BCE

    Hannibal defeated a Roman army near the Trebia; elephants belong to the ancient account of this campaign. The locality is a river-region reconstruction. Modern field boundaries are not ancient battle lines. [3]

    Map reference: 45°, 9.63°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  8. Etrurian marshes: animals and exhaustion

    217 BCE

    Polybius describes an exhausting marsh crossing and Hannibal riding the surviving elephant. The text’s animal count is a reported episode, not a fossil inventory. The wetland corridor is broad and is not a reconstructed 217 BCE shoreline. [3]

    Map reference: 43.75°, 10.9°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  9. Lake Trasimene: the ambush

    217 BCE

    A Roman army under Flaminius was ambushed beside Lake Trasimene. Battlefield proposals differ in detail. The regional point does not authenticate a particular modern memorial or every number in the ancient account. [3]

    Map reference: 43.18°, 12.08°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  10. Cannae: victory without capturing Rome

    216 BCE

    Cannae was a major Roman defeat, followed by changes in alliances across southern Italy. The site anchor is approximate. Ancient force and casualty figures vary and are not used here as population observations. [3] [5]

    Map reference: 41.3°, 16.13°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  11. Capua: alliance and Roman recapture

    216–211 BCE

    Capua joined Hannibal’s side after Cannae and was recaptured by Rome in 211 BCE. The marker is ancient Capua at modern Santa Maria Capua Vetere, not the later town of Capua. Alliance did not erase local institutions. [5] [6]

    Map reference: 41.08°, 14.25°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  12. Tarentum: city and citadel divided

    212–209 BCE

    Hannibal gained Tarentum while the Roman garrison held its citadel; Rome recovered the city in 209 BCE. A single city dot cannot express divided control. The surviving Roman citadel is a reason not to colour the whole urban area as uncontested Punic possession. [5] [6]

    Map reference: 40.47°, 17.23°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  13. Near Rome: the 211 BCE diversion

    211 BCE

    Hannibal moved near Rome while Capua was under siege. This was an approach and encampment, not capture of Rome. The Anio-area point is schematic. [6]

    Map reference: 41.95°, 12.54°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  14. Metaurus: Hasdrubal’s failed reinforcement

    207 BCE

    Hasdrubal’s separate army was defeated before it could join Hannibal. This is campaign context, not a battle Hannibal personally commanded. The precise battlefield remains disputed. [6]

    Map reference: 43.75°, 13.02°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  15. Croton and the Lacinian coast: departure for Africa

    203 BCE

    Hannibal’s remaining Italian base lay in the south before the army returned to Africa. The coast anchor does not reconstruct the fleet’s harbour movements or exact sea course. [4]

    Map reference: 39.02°, 17.2°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  16. Zama: the final battle of the war

    202 BCE

    Scipio and his allies defeated Hannibal’s army; elephants again feature in Polybius’ account. Regional Tunisian anchor only: the battlefield identification is disputed. These elephants are not presumed to be the animals that crossed the Alps sixteen years earlier. [4]

    Map reference: 36.1°, 9.45°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

  17. Córdoba: a real elephant bone, a limited attribution

    Fourth–third centuries BCE; likely Punic military context

    A carpal bone from Colina de los Quemados was radiocarbon-dated to the fourth–third centuries BCE; associated evidence suggests a Punic military context. The 2026 report does not identify an individual animal or tie it to Hannibal’s Alpine column. The broad dating range must remain distinct from the inferred Second Punic War context. [8]

    Map reference: 37.87°, -4.79°. Approximate unless the source establishes a surveyed position.

A Mediterranean war, not only an Alpine crossing

Hannibal’s expedition is often reduced to elephants in the snow. The campaign makes more sense when followed from the fortified Punic base at Qart Hadast, through the siege of Saguntum and the invasion of Italy, to shifting alliances in southern Italy and the eventual return to Africa. Victory at Cannae did not equal the capture of Rome. The war also involved commanders and armies elsewhere; Hasdrubal’s defeat at the Metaurus appears here as the loss of a separate reinforcing force, not a battle commanded by Hannibal. [3] [6]

The dated map distinguishes archaeological places from literary episodes. A surviving Punic wall can establish a fortified urban phase. A historian’s description can place an army in a river region. A modern polygon can summarise a broad political reconstruction. None of these provides an excavated track through every valley or a reliable headcount of everyone who lived within a coloured boundary. [9] [3] [1]

The written witnesses and their distance from events

Polybius wrote after the Second Punic War and sought to explain Rome’s rise. His narrative is more than a list of dates: it offers arguments about command, terrain, logistics and political decisions. That purpose makes it rich evidence, while also requiring scrutiny of speeches, numerical claims and explanations of success or failure. The surviving books and fragments are not contemporary field reports. [3] [4]

Livy and the ancient summaries of his books add another Roman narrative tradition. The Periochae are compressed summaries, and modern bracketed dates on a translation page are editorial aids. At Tarentum, their distinction between the captured city and a Roman-held citadel is crucial. At Rome, they describe an approach and encampment, not conquest. Reading those distinctions is more useful than colouring every named place as wholly controlled by one side. [5] [6]

What can be said about the elephants

Polybius reports thirty-seven elephants in the Rhône-crossing episode and describes the work required to move them through difficult Alpine ground. Elephants also appear in his account of the Trebia campaign and in the later marsh-crossing story, where Hannibal rides the remaining animal. These are ancient literary numbers and scenes. They do not justify a precise survival graph, a known species for every animal, or the familiar unsupported assertion that most died while actually crossing the Alps. [3]

At Zama, Polybius describes elephants again, with an outcome affected by frightened animals and Roman arrangements. That army belonged to a different phase of the war, sixteen years after the first crossing. The narrative does not make those animals survivors of the original Alpine column. The map keeps the 218 BCE and 202 BCE episodes separate. [4]

A bone from Córdoba: stronger material evidence, narrower conclusions

The 2026 Colina de los Quemados study reports a real elephant carpal bone from an archaeological context in Córdoba. Its author-university abstract places the radiocarbon result between the fourth and third centuries BCE. Associated artillery stones, coins and other context suggest a military episode linked to the Second Punic War. The bone is more direct evidence for the presence of an elephant than a literary scene, but it is not an individual identified as one of Hannibal’s elephants. [8]

The map preserves the broad dating range instead of assigning the bone to the year of the Alpine crossing. It also distinguishes the age evidence from the proposed association with a war. The retrieved institutional abstract supports that cautious interpretation; it does not establish which army owned the animal, whether it died in combat, or any passage through an Alpine valley. [8]

Why the Alpine pass remains unresolved

Modern proposals combine readings of ancient topography with route experiments, geology and environmental traces. These approaches can test whether a proposed path is possible; possibility is different from identification. Bainbridge and Cracolici’s recent study follows the long debate and the ways disciplinary claims have sometimes been made more decisive than the surviving evidence allows. The route question remains open. [7]

The map therefore uses a broad western-Alpine connector with no named pass asserted at its central vertex. Neither the Col du Clapier nor Col de la Traversette is designated as the correct answer. The same restraint applies to the Rhône crossing, the Ticinus and Metaurus battlefields, and the final Zama location. The points are regional anchors, with uncertainty stated where a reader encounters the evidence. [7] [3] [4]

Territory is not a measure of the army’s presence

Nine Carthage versions are selected from Cliopatria’s dated historical-polity reconstruction and clipped to this study’s 219–202 BCE window. They retain whole source components rather than filling a hull around battle points. Their irregular source intervals can combine changes within several years; the final interval may also combine battle and subsequent treaty adjustments. They are broad political context, not a claim that Hannibal continuously occupied every place inside them. [1] [2]

Allied communities retained local interests, citadels could remain in opposing hands, and armies moved beyond settled control. Tarentum’s divided city and citadel make the problem concrete. Routes are therefore displayed as qualified campaign connections and territories as a separate reconstruction. An active route does not manufacture a frontier, and an old battle point does not demonstrate ongoing occupation. [5] [1]

Population numbers that this map does not invent

The sources used here do not provide comparable dated resident totals for every mapped city. Ancient troop strengths, battlefield deaths, captives and Roman citizen registrations measure different groups. Even a genuine registration figure would not equal the population of the city of Rome. Numeric population observations are consequently omitted rather than assigning city totals from military counts or area. The absence of a number means that this draft has not established it. [6] [3]
