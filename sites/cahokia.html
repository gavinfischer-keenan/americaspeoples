Cahokia and the American Bottom

Cahokia stands in the American Bottom near present-day Collinsville, Illinois. Its monumental core belonged to a wider agricultural and settlement landscape. Earthwork dimensions and mound totals are architectural evidence, not direct resident counts.

A closer reading

A city sustained by work and gathering

Cahokia’s Grand Plaza was made on ground where people had already lived. Domestic deposits beneath later fills reveal an inhabited landscape before large-scale earthmoving reshaped the area. The open space was itself a building project, making the plaza as important to the city’s history as the mounds around it.

Evidence: doi.org [1] ↗

At the center’s height, especially around 1050–1150, earthworks served varied purposes. Some supported buildings, while others contained burials or marked significant places. Their different roles connected public gathering, residence and remembrance. The surviving protected site is only part of that larger urban landscape, and modern reconstruction has added another layer to what visitors see.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗ · cahokiamounds.org [2] ↗ · artsandculture.google.com [3] ↗ · cahokiamounds.org [4] ↗ · experts.illinois.edu [5] ↗

Cahokia’s monumental centre stood within a much larger inhabited landscape of houses, activity areas and outlying communities. Its peak expansion in the eleventh and twelfth centuries created a city of thousands of people. Earthen mounds gave some buildings elevated positions, while open spaces organized movement and assembly. Producing food and moving construction material were essential to maintaining this urban world as well as its spectacular public architecture.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗

At Mound 34, archaeological evidence connects monumental space with the working of copper. Excavators identified workshop areas, raw copper pieces and worked fragments. Such finds bring a different scale into the city’s history: individual skilled operations contributed objects that circulated within broader social and ceremonial relationships. The surrounding deposits also contained serving vessels, animal remains and marine shells, joining craft production to the activities of a busy public place.

Evidence: cahokiamounds.org [1] ↗

Mound 51 preserves evidence for a changing public landscape. Excavations revealed an earlier borrow pit that supplied earth for construction. People subsequently filled it with soil and refuse, leveled the area and raised the mound over part of the old pit. The mound itself was built in multiple stages. This sequence connects the digging, filling and rebuilding of a single location with the larger work of making Cahokia.

Evidence: cahokiamounds.org [1] ↗

A village beneath the monumental plaza

The Grand Plaza was built over an inhabited landscape. Excavated domestic deposits and ceramics precede fills that reshaped the terrain into a much larger open space. These are two observations at the same place: earlier habitation and later earthmoving. Their overlapping rounded windows reflect archaeological resolution, not a contradiction that must be removed by inventing a precise turnover year. Source 1 ↗

Different mounds preserve different histories

The smaller mounds cannot all be assigned Monks Mound’s model. Mound 48 has a tentative single-episode interpretation, while Mound 51 preserves at least two stages above a filled borrow pit. Fox Mound is visible in the paired southern group but lacks the excavation history needed for comparable phase dating. The map therefore shows different degrees of chronological confidence. Timber monuments and an enclosure rebuilt

Woodhenge and the stockade make vanished wooden architecture visible through post settings and reconstruction. The official brochure assigns the timber-circle sequence to 1100–1200 and the four enclosures to 1175–1275. Both are broad sequences. They show repeated work, but they do not supply an exact construction year for every post or prove an annual threat of attack. Source 1 ↗

After the medieval city, Indigenous histories continue

The River L’Abbe mission was established on Monks Mound’s first terrace in 1735 and operated until 1752. It belongs to Cahokia people of the Illinois Confederation and French mission history, centuries after the main urban phase. Regional evidence also rejects an equation between urban decline and an empty landscape. The Peoria Tribe continues Illinois community histories; the Osage Nation’s account connects ancestral heritage and continuing responsibilities to the mound landscapes around St. Louis. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Mound building linked diverse Mississippian communities across different towns and centuries. These archaeological traditions belong within longer Indigenous histories. Living tribal connections to ancestral landscapes continue beyond the periods when particular monumental centers flourished. Source 1 ↗

Cahokia — urban archaeological complex

Cahokia’s urban complex flourished in approximately 1050–1400 CE. ‘Mississippian’ names an archaeological tradition associated with this phase, rather than a race or a single verified ethnic identity.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: ca. AD 1050–1400
Dating method: Archaeological chronology synthesized with regional environmental evidence

The regional population persisted and later increased. Ending this marker must not imply an empty watershed or extinction.

Grand Plaza area — village before plaza construction

Domestic deposits and ceramics beneath plaza fill document a village landscape before the later open monumental space. The published assemblage includes Merrell and Edelhardt phase material.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 950–1050 CE
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

950–1050 is the ceramic-phase context of pre-plaza habitation, not a year-by-year occupancy register. The anchor represents the public plaza area rather than the exact excavation trench.

Grand Plaza — mid-eleventh-century earthmoving

Excavations identify a major engineered landform: construction filled and leveled parts of the terrain to create the Grand Plaza in the middle of the eleventh century.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Middle of the 11 th century CE; displayed as approximate 1025–1075
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

1025–1075 is a deliberately rounded display window for “middle of the 11 th century,” not a published statistical interval or fifty years of continuous construction. Habitation beneath fill belongs to an earlier phase.

Monks Mound — modeled start of main construction

Schilling’s Bayesian model places the start boundary for construction of the main body of Monks Mound in 1052–1136 CE at 95.4% probability.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1052–1136 cal CE,95.4% modeled boundary
Dating method: Published Bayesian stratigraphic model using radiocarbon and archaeomagnetic constraints; source-calibrated calendar ages retained

A probability interval for one modeled boundary, not the lifespan of the mound or continuous construction throughout the interval. Samples above and below construction constrain the modeled phase; no unambiguous construction samples were placed within it. The mound-scale anchor is shared across these phases.

Mound 48 — probable Lohmann-phase construction

Coring and test excavation led site researchers to interpret Mound 48 as probably built in one construction episode during the Lohmann phase.

Dates and evidence notes

Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

1050–1100 is the Lohmann phase window, not a measured start and finish for this mound. The site account says “probably”; the single-episode inference remains qualified.

Woodhenge — repeated timber-circle construction

The official site brochure places five partly excavated timber-circle constructions in 1100–1200 CE. The standing display represents a reconstruction of the third circle.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1100–1200 CE
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

This is the brochure’s broad sequence window, not exact dates for each circle. Older institutional texts use 900–1100 or 900–1200; those differ and are not silently combined. Modern posts do not survive from the medieval construction.

Central precinct — four stockade constructions

The site brochure describes four successive log enclosures around Cahokia’s central ceremonial precinct during 1175–1275 CE.

Mound 34 — platform and terrace sequence

At Mound 34 an initial platform was followed by a larger rectangular platform and adjoining terraces. A summit building with a hearth was recorded; its profile suggests burning.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Relative post-premound sequence; broad late urban context after about 1200, before approximately 1400
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

The broad 1200–1400 window locates this sequence after premound structures dated about 1200 and within Cahokia’s urban-period context. Neither 1400 nor 1200 is a direct construction date for this mound. Burning does not identify its cause or prove warfare.

Mound 51 — mound over a filled borrow pit

Excavation identified at least two building stages at Mound 51, with fire features on stage surfaces. The mound covered part of an earlier borrow pit that had been filled and leveled.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1050–1400 CE
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

1050–1400 is only the broad Cahokia urban context for this architectural sequence. The account does not independently date the two stages; the point is the Persimmon Mound southeast of Monks Mound, not an erroneously labeled western OSM node.

Mound 60 / Fox Mound — southern plaza architecture

Fox Mound is a rectangular platform paired spatially with Round Top Mound at the south end of the Grand Plaza. Their contrasting shapes organize this southern monumental group.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1050–1400 CE
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

1050–1400 is broad urban-period context, not a direct date for Fox Mound. The official account reports no excavation of this mound; proposed charnel-house functions remain analogies. A shared contour can reflect slopewash rather than an original connecting platform.

Grand Plaza margins — later domestic occupation

The plaza study identifies later Mississippian houses along the eastern, western and southern margins of the Grand Plaza, approximately 1200–1300 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1200–1300 CE
Dating method: Archaeological phase attribution and published site chronology

This is a ceramic-phase habitation observation at the plaza margins, not continuous residence everywhere in the central open area. The shared plaza-scale point does not locate an individual house. Absence of later material in these excavations is not proof that the entire city was empty after 1300.
