A closer reading

From streamside homes to a multistory town

Paquimé’s local settlement history begins before the multistory architecture visible today. INAH describes early pit houses near streams and springs, followed by a transition toward earthen houses organized around courtyards. Water and household routines place ordinary choices about dwelling at the beginning of the town’s later monumental history.

Evidence: lugares.inah.gob.mx [1] ↗

During the Medio period, large residential groups, plazas and specialized buildings changed the scale of settlement. The development need not be told as the simple arrival of a replacement population: recent maternal-line evidence points toward substantial continuity. Architecture grew more elaborate within a connected regional world, while the histories of particular households remain more difficult to recover.

Evidence: lugares.inah.gob.mx [1] ↗ · pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov [2] ↗

Settlements beside water

INAH's account begins with pit-house settlements near streams and springs around 600 CE, well before the surviving multistory town. Their setting puts access to water and everyday settlement choices at the beginning of the story. Around 900, the institutional sequence describes a change toward earthen courtyard houses. The early interval is a broad place-area phase, not a direct date for the later public buildings. Keeping it separate makes Paquimé's development visible without projecting its eventual monumental form backward over several centuries. Source 1 ↗

Illustrative frame within official early-settlement account, approximately 600–900 CE. Source 1 ↗

Courtyards and earthen walls

Around 900 CE, the official sequence describes a transition from pit houses to earthen-walled houses arranged around courtyards. Household and workshop spaces provide a view of architecture organized around recurring activities rather than isolated monuments. The approximate date marks a change in the settlement account; it is not evidence that every household rebuilt at once. Later mitochondrial research also challenges a simple population-replacement explanation for Paquimé's growth, although those maternal-line results cannot assign identities or origins to the occupants of any particular courtyard. Approximate change around 900 CE; not an exactly dated citywide rebuilding. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The Medio town

During the Medio period, large residential units, plazas, specialized buildings and public works transformed Paquimé's scale. Casa Amurallada is a named component that INAH assigns to this phase, while the existing town record supplies the conventional 1200–1450 CE frame. Some regional chronologies begin the phase closer to 1150, so the starting boundary remains approximate. Recent mitochondrial results suggest substantial maternal-line continuity across the town's rise. Architectural elaboration and increasing connections therefore need not be narrated as a new population simply replacing an older one. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Viewing year near conventional Medio onset; regional chronology can start around 1150 or 1200. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Water through the town

The water system connected collection, filtering, storage and distribution with life inside the town. INAH describes the Depósito de Agua, a principal reservoir and channels supplying residential spaces. These components help explain the maintenance and coordination needed to sustain dense earthen architecture. Irrigation also belongs to the wider agricultural account, but it should not be confused with a fully reconstructed street-by-street supply network. The viewing year falls within the town's broad phase; no independent narrow construction date was verified for each tank, filter or channel. Source 1 ↗

Illustrative view within the broad town phase; no independently dated construction year for each channel. Source 1 ↗

Raising macaws and moving knowledge

Scarlet macaws connect Paquimé's architecture with animal care, specialized labor and wider circulation. Casa de las Guacamayas provides a named setting for bird keeping, while isotope research on 29 specimens indicates that most were probably raised locally, with some possible nonlocal origins. The checked abstract does not establish that every sampled bird came from that house. Nor do strontium values trace a unique trade route. The scene therefore brings local husbandry into the exchange story rather than treating every exotic bird as a newly imported object. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Illustrative Medio-phase frame; isotope specimens are not independently assigned to 1320. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A dated act at the House of the Well

The House of the Well contains an activity dated more directly than many of the town's architectural phases: an interment associated with its central roof-support post. The radiocarbon result calibrates to 1301–1397 CE at 95.4% probability. Tree-ring measurements from the building are older and lack outer rings, preventing their simple use as exact construction dates. The source interprets the interment in relation to ritual and the building's erection. Those interpretations remain distinct from the measured dating result and from claims about the entire community. Source 1 ↗

Viewing frame within calibrated 1301–1397 CE, 95.4% probability. Source 1 ↗

Decline without an invented disappearance

Paquimé’s large-scale occupation declined during the fifteenth century, bringing its main town phase to an end around the conventional date of 1450. Its households, water systems, bird keeping, and ritual spaces had developed through different histories. The changing use of this built landscape transformed the regional center, although the available evidence does not establish one final departure date or a single cause of decline. Interpretations and remaining questions

Scholars place the beginning of the Medio phase around either 1150 or 1200. Its chronology therefore remains broader than a single agreed starting year.

Timbers from the House of the Well lack their outer rings, so their tree-ring dates do not identify the exact year of construction.

One individual's close parental relatedness cannot establish all Paquimé residents' marriage practices, status or identity.

Macaw isotope values constrain possible origins, not a unique source locality or drawn trade route.

Maternal mitochondrial continuity does not demonstrate absence of all migration, language continuity, or an ethnic identification.

No verified source here establishes one citywide massacre or the disappearance of everyone at 1450.

The construction dates of individual water-management features and their precise locations remain unresolved in the available documentation.

Verified descendant perspectives remain an important gap in the sources presented here.

Isotope measurements help investigate origins and diets. Their implications for elite ritual and political organization require additional interpretation.
