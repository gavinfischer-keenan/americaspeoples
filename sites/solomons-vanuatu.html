Solomon Islands and Vanuatu · PLACE / COMPLEX

After Lapita in the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu

At Te Ana Tavatava in the Duff Islands, coral gravel accumulated each time people renewed the floors of their village. At Mafilau on Epi, the shells, potsherds and broken tools that people cleared away formed a mound taller than a person. These ordinary acts left two unusually deep records of life on islands whose settlements were often rebuilt, buried or moved. The map follows the communities that lived here after the first Lapita settlements. At times the clearest traces are farming soils or pots used in a kitchen. At others they are a shrine, a burial or a tool carried over open water. Looking at each place and its dated deposits shows how long connections persisted and how differently neighbouring islands used them.

The first coastal settlements

Nenumbo once stood near a shallow lagoon on Ngaua in the Reef Islands. Postholes and concentrations of stone and pottery suggest that people worked in different parts of a small hamlet. Plant traces recover a different part of their lives. Soil samples from the excavations contain banana phytoliths, microscopic bodies formed by plants. They give evidence for plant use alongside the shellfish and other reef foods left in the deposits. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

In northern Vanuatu, plain and shell-impressed pottery at Matantas links the southeast shore of Big Bay to a late Lapita site at Port Olry. At Mafilau farther south, the people who built up the great mound on Epi made Early Erueti pots and worked shells into rings and drilled ornaments. Banana traces occur throughout the deposit. The mound grew by repeated dumping, and its contents combine cooking, coastal gathering, cultivation and craft. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Villages on shifting shores

Lakao has an uncommon record of prolonged activity in one place. Early users left fire pits and shellworking debris in the cave at Te Ana Tavatava. Later residents spread fine coral gravel on house floors in the open, building a thick layer through repeated resurfacing. The excavators identified village phases spanning roughly 400 BCE to 1000 CE. A cowrie-shell tool in their village deposits gives a clue to the processing of vegetable foods. Source 1 ↗

Across the sea, Malua Bay’s excavated surface appears to have been prepared by moving coral gravel onto beach sand. Charcoal and fire-cracked stones place people cooking near that sheltered landing place. Along the Santa Cruz coast on Nendö, excavations place the transition from using pottery to leaving pottery-free deposits around 100 BCE–100 CE. Some late settlements on Nendö had dance circles and craft-working evidence. Changes in pottery use document only one aspect of those histories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Island pottery histories diverge

People at Sinapupu on Tikopia used incised vessels with strong similarities to Mangaasi pottery from central Vanuatu. Excavations recovered some of the distinctive sherds alongside shell arm ornaments. The fit between the two chronologies supports contact. How the pots reached Tikopia, and where individual vessels were made, are harder to recover from the sherds. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At Melarip on east Efate, weathered sherds with raised decoration and incised lines belong to the island’s last major ceramic phase. On Lamen Island off Epi, pottery remained in use much later. The Lamen pots show some likenesses to Malakula forms, and ash from nearby volcanic activity separates parts of the excavated sequence. On Tongoa, where the fifteenth-century Tombuk eruption transformed the island, the ceramic sequence changed again. The chronology follows where vessels are found and how they were made. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Living with rivers and slopes

At Ivanaud on Aneityum, excavators found stone-lined drains beneath later layers of flood sediment and garden soil. The lower and more closely spaced drains may relate to growers’ attempts to manage a wetter valley floor. Elsewhere on the island, a large furrow-irrigated system at Aname was rebuilt across two phases. The island’s food production depended on cultivation and on the work of managing water around it. Source 1 ↗

On Tikopia, a different form of land management can be traced through measurements on rat and pig remains. The chemical signals changed during the last major phase of island history before regular overseas contact. The excavators and isotope researchers relate these changes to how people used trees, soils and gardens. The samples sit within the Tuakamali sequence, which also brought changes in tool styles and contacts with the Banks Islands and Western Polynesia. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

From inland shrines to the lagoon

Around 1200 CE, the Bao ridge above Munda acquired one of the earliest dated shrines in Roviana. People set a flat basalt slab above a paved area in front of a stone-faced platform. Roviana histories connect the inland ridge to the origins of communities that later held power along the lagoon. On Nusa Roviana, later shrine platforms with shell valuables and food offerings stood among settlements and within the fortified ridge. Lagoon traditions connect the ridge to named chiefs and to raiding. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

In northwest Malakula, the relationship of village and ceremonial space took a different form. At Chachara, low platforms called nasara sit by concentrations of pottery and cooking debris. Such platforms are known from ceremony and grade-taking in northern Vanuatu. The midden beside the northern platform contains pig bone, shellfish and a shell adze as well as large quantities of a distinct late pottery style. Source 1 ↗

What travelled between islands

The places where tools ended up and where their materials formed can be far apart. A small flake from a fourteenth-century deposit on Sikopo in the Arnavon Islands matches obsidian in West New Britain. At Kahula on Taumako, two adzes found in early village layers came from Tutuila. On Emae, volcanic glass from Vanua Lava turned up in a midden. Each piece preserves a link across open water. The artifact and its geological source are the two securely shared points in the journey. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

On Taumako, travel is also reflected in the Namu burial ground. Researchers compared strontium in tooth enamel, a record of where people lived when their teeth formed. They found patterns consistent with some women growing up elsewhere, including one with an especially distinct signature. Historic accounts of marriages between the Duff, Reef and Santa Cruz islands provide a context for interpreting the findings. Source 1 ↗

Roi Mata’s landscape

On the northwest coast of Efate, people laid stone walls around residential yards at Mangaas during its final settlement phase. In local tradition this was the residence of the last Roi Mata. Across the water on Lelepa, Fels Cave is associated with his death. The island of Artok holds the burial place where excavation uncovered a central grave with numerous accompanying burials. Archaeology and local accounts trace different aspects of that same landscape. Community and chiefly management have kept its customary restrictions in use. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

Nenumbo — bananas at a Lapita settlement

Banana phytoliths from excavated sediments at Nenumbo show that plants mattered at one of the earliest known settlements in the Reef Islands. The younger of the two sampled cultural layers had abundant banana phytoliths without the seed forms typical of wild plants.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 3185–2639 cal BP site age in Lentfer et al. Table 1
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum.

Banana plant parts are easier to identify than individual cultivars. The sampled deposits leave the balance of harvested reef foods and cultivated plants uncertain.

Matantas — pottery and nutshells on Big Bay

At Matantas, excavations in the southeast corner of Big Bay recovered plain and shell-impressed pots from a late Lapita context on the north coast of Espiritu Santo. Two nutshell samples provide the site's dated evidence.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Wk-20005 2634 ± 48 radiocarbon BP, 2790–2470 cal BP (95.4%); Wk-20006 2588 ± 48 radiocarbon BP, 2760–2450 (93.6%) and 2390–2360 cal BP (1.8%)
Dating method: Published calibrated ranges are displayed separately; the outer bounds organize the record without filling the intervening gap.

The plateau in the calibration curve widens the estimates. The sampled part of the site and its ceramics do not show how long people lived there before or after the dated deposits.

Mafilau — an Early Erueti midden mound

At Mafilau on Epi, people piled marine shell, broken pots, cooking stones and shellworking refuse into a mound. About 4.5 metres of dense deposits accumulated above an earlier beach. Banana phytoliths occurred throughout the mound.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Table 1 calibrated ranges: Wk-19650 2767–2541 cal BP; Wk-19649 2710–2407; Wk-18010 2724–2428; Wk-18009 2726–2432
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum. The outer limits are from the four Mafilau determinations tabulated by Henderson et al. 2025.

The mound holds discarded material gathered from elsewhere in the settlement. Similar shapes at other central Vanuatu sites do not establish where individual pots were made.

Te Ana Tavatava — the Lakao village

At Te Ana Tavatava, a village grew outside an earlier cave shelter. Repeated applications of kilikili, fine coral gravel spread over house floors, built up a deep deposit. A type of cowrie shell tool associated with peeling vegetables appears in the village layer.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Excavators’ approximate Lakao I phase 400 BCE–500 CE and Lakao II phase 500–1000 CE
Dating method: Published site occupation chronology, based on four charcoal ages at 2σ within a single sequence of repeated floor deposits.

The floor deposits were not subdivided finely enough to date individual households. Cowrie peelers show food processing without identifying the crops.

Sinapupu — incised pottery and shell ornaments

Deep deposits on Tikopia contained distinctively incised Sinapupu pottery and well-worked shell. At TK-1, imported-looking ceramics accompanied Trochus arm ornaments. Other excavation units yielded giant-clam-shell adzes.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Modeled beginning of Early Sinapupu: 117 BCE–310 CE (95% HPD); modeled end of Late Sinapupu: 1071–1084 and 1155–1207 CE (95% HPD)
Dating method: Published model of archaeological phases, based on AMS dates and a Bayesian reanalysis of earlier radiocarbon results. BCE limits use astronomical year numbering.

The Vanuatu connection is based on ceramic comparisons. The source island or workshop for a particular vessel remains uncertain.

Malua Bay — a prepared shoreline surface

At Malua Bay on northwest Malakula, people appear to have brought coral gravel onto beach sand to make a hard, dry surface. Burnt material and refuse in the layer attest to cooking and other activity near the shoreline.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: ANU-10524, 1900 ± 80 radiocarbon BP, 2000–1627 calibrated BP
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum.

The excavator infers that gravel was deliberately moved from the nature of the layer and similar local practices. The date pertains to charcoal in the layer.

Pangpang — Late Mangaasi pottery at Melarip

On a terrace near Forari Bay, a test pit at Melarip uncovered weathered potsherds and marine shell. The decorated fragments combine raised relief and incision, characteristic of the final phase of pottery making on Efate.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Regional Late Mangaasi ceramic phase c. 1600–1200 BP
Dating method: Approximate conventional calendar placement of the regional ceramic phase using the 1950 archaeological BP frame. The publication reports typological dating at this locality.

The layers had been disturbed. The finds locate activity on the terrace within a broad ceramic phase, without tracing a house plan or the length of its use.

Lamen Island — pottery in a layered coastal settlement

At Lamen Island, pottery lay in successive coastal deposits interbedded with volcanic ash. Some of its varied forms resembled vessels from farther north on Malakula. The pots did not follow the sequence recorded across the water at Tongoa.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Wk-22306, Turbo chysostomus shell, 1857 ± 30 radiocarbon BP, 1508–1243 cal BP (95.4%)
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum.

The shell determination dates one excavated level. The origin of the ash layers and the production places of individual pots are uncertain.

Ivanaud — drains at the edge of dryland gardens

Stone-lined drains exposed within buried garden horizons along the Anetcho River show how Aneityum farmers managed a wet valley floor. Spriggs linked the dense older drains at Ivanaud to early cultivation on newly accumulated soils.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1060–790 BP calibrated context for charcoal in Anetcho River AT188 Layer III.1, correlated with dryland garden horizons at AT130
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum. The range comes from Spriggs’s published calibration, via a nearby correlated river-bank exposure.

The dating depends on stratigraphic correlation across exposures. Charcoal could have moved within the river catchment.

Sinapupu — beginning of the Tuakamali phase

Tikopia’s early Tuakamali deposits contain shell adzes and volcanic glass from the Banks Islands. The wider phase also saw adze and fishing gear forms familiar from Western Polynesia. Ceramics disappeared from the early Tuakamali deposits at TK-1.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1158–1212 cal CE modeled 95% HPD onset of the Early Tuakamali Phase
Dating method: Published Bayesian phase boundary from a revised radiocarbon sequence, including AMS samples from Tikopia's excavations.

Artifacts can move with newcomers, through exchange or both. The mix of material from different areas leaves the timing and composition of individual arrivals uncertain.

Bao — the inland faced shrine

At Bao, in the hills behind Munda, a paved enclosure leads to a platform faced with stone. Excavated charcoal in the platform fill points to activity around 1200 CE. Bao is a place of origin in Roviana accounts of the communities that later held power along the lagoon.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: NZA-10855 830 ± 60 radiocarbon BP, 1164–1270 CE; NZA-10856 789 ± 70 radiocarbon BP, 1200–1285 CE (published 1σ)
Dating method: Outer limits of the two published 1σ calibrated charcoal ranges. Both samples came from Layer 2 in the platform fill.

Researchers found the shrine's fill and associated paving, but little evidence for dwellings at the site. The duration of occupation described in oral accounts is unknown.

Chachara — cooking pots beside a nasara

Inland from Malua Bay, low stone platforms called nasara stood beside concentrations of pottery and fire-cracked stones. A nearby midden mound contained pig bone, shellfish, a giant-clam-shell adze and distinctive late pottery.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: ANU-10525, 980 ± 60 radiocarbon BP, 654–489 calibrated BP
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum.

The single shell date applies to the refuse mound. The start of construction and full use span of the nearby platforms remain uncertain.

Kahula — imported adzes in early settlement layers

Two basalt adzes from stratified early settlement layers at Kahula on Taumako came from Tutuila in Samoa. The geochemistry links the adzes to a faraway source while the excavation puts them in a specific village history.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1296–1472 CE, published chronology associated with the first settlement deposits at Kahula
Dating method: Published dates for deposits in the artifact sourcing study, which draws on the original excavations. Geochemical provenance comes from elemental and isotopic comparisons.

Other sampled Taumako artifacts have looser archaeological context. The dated adzes establish material movement without showing the intermediate stops, travellers or exchange arrangements.

Sikopo — New Britain obsidian in a later deposit

A tiny glass flake from a post-Lapita deposit on Sikopo matches the Kutau/Bao chemical group from Talasea in West New Britain. Around it were remains of pottery, shell work, fauna and other stone tools from occupation on the low coral island.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: OZX442, nutshell from 30–40 cm, 631–516 cal BP (2σ)
Dating method: Published calibrated BP limits translated to astronomical years using the 1950 datum. A flake from the same excavated depth was sourced with portable X-ray fluorescence.

The result is one of a few geochemically identified obsidian finds from the main Solomon Islands chain. The transfer path and how often such transfers happened remain uncertain.

Mangaas — the last settlement phase in Roi Mata’s Domain

At Mangaas on the Efate coast, stone walls enclosed residential yards during the last phase of settlement. Local traditions identify it as the residence of the last chief to hold the title Roi Mata, associate his death with Fels Cave on Lelepa and place his burial on Artok.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1450–1600 CE, published phase estimate for the late occupation at Mangaas
Dating method: Vanuatu’s heritage nomination relates charcoal ranges, post-eruption layers, walls and oral history to the late occupation phase.

The ages do not pin down a precise year for Roi Mata's death or any episode of the conflict described in oral tradition.

Namu — childhood origins in the burial ground

Dental enamel from people buried at Namu suggests that some grew up away from Taumako. The study found a difference between men and women in strontium isotope ratios and one young woman with a particularly distinct childhood signature.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1510–1800 CE, Namu period as framed by Kramer et al.
Dating method: The study’s archaeological period provides the time window. Strontium isotope ratios in tooth enamel inform childhood residence, not age at death or date of travel.

The neighboring islands' geologies limit pinpointing of origins. The more distant source of the one outlying signature is unresolved.

Nusa Roviana — walls and shrines on the ridge

On Nusa Roviana a fortified ridge rose above settlements on the coastal flats. Stone-and-earth walls, a ditch and named shrines formed a complex where defence and ritual were closely associated.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Wk-6757, 720 ± 50 radiocarbon BP, 1523–1653 CE at 1σ with published marine ΔR = 0
Dating method: Published calibrated shell sample associated with the hillfort wall. Identical range reproduced in the 2004 report's Table 1.

The shell date constrains one construction context. Later oral and written histories describe raids based in the lagoon. Assigning those raids to the first wall or dating individual battles from the walls is uncertain.

Emae — Banks Islands glass in a midden

A broken flake from a midden on eastern Emae matches obsidian from Vanua Lava in the Banks Islands. A piece of volcanic glass travelled across several island groups before someone discarded it in the Shepherd Islands.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1565–1670 CE, archaeological layer yielding obsidian flake E-11-03
Dating method: Published context chronology and elemental comparison of the flake with obsidian outcrops.

The match establishes the geological source. It leaves open who carried the stone and whether it passed through several hands.

Tutuila adzes at Kahula, Taumako

Two adzes found in early settlement layers at Kahula on Taumako are made of Tutuila basalt. The geochemical study distinguishes quarry and workshop signatures in the Tatagamatau area of Tutuila from other Pacific sources.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1296–1472 CE for the stratified Kahula contexts of the two early adzes, Hermann et al. 2023.
Dating method: Published context chronology from archaeological deposits and geochemical comparison to Tutuila.

The basalt may have travelled with people or through exchange. The makers’ identities and the stops or transactions between Tutuila and Taumako remain unknown.

Read the history & sources ↗

Kutau/Bao obsidian at Sikopo

A flake of volcanic glass found on Sikopo in the Arnavon Islands matches the Kutau/Bao source at Talasea in West New Britain. The small piece was recovered from a deposit with pottery and animal remains.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: OZX442, 631–516 cal BP (2σ), from 30–40 cm in the deposit with the flake.
Dating method: Published calibrated nutshell range, converted using 1950. The excavation recovered a glass flake in the same depth band.

The artifact establishes movement between a geological source area and a receiving island. It does not identify a direct trip or establish how often this movement happened.

Vanua Lava obsidian on Emae

Obsidian from Vanua Lava reached Emae in the Shepherd Islands. One flake in a late midden matches the rare rhyolitic glass available on the Banks Island.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1565–1670 CE, associated layer containing obsidian flake E-11-03.
Dating method: Published context chronology with geochemical comparison to volcanic outcrops.

This occurrence identifies a source and find place for one transported object. It leaves the number of transfers and people involved undetermined.
