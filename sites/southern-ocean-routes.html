Three passages, three different problems

At the tip of South America, distance alone never decided the route. A ship could enter the Strait of Magellan, work through the waterways around Beagle Channel, or stay outside the islands and round Cape Horn. The first two choices exchange some exposure to ocean swell for confined water, intricate shores and difficult anchoring. The open route leaves room to manoeuvre but exposes a vessel to the Southern Ocean. The historical charts below show the coastlines and names available to nineteenth-century surveyors; the whole-ocean map supplies the modern geographical setting. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

This guide follows voyage history only through 1934. Its recent photographs, bathymetry and climate statistics are explicitly modern context. They help explain physical differences between routes; they do not reconstruct the weather on a sixteenth- or nineteenth-century voyage. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Waterways already inhabited and traveled

The European voyage accounts did not describe an empty coast. They record people traveling by canoe, visiting anchorages and negotiating encounters with crews. King’s survey and FitzRoy’s narrative are evidence of those encounters, but their judgments are those of outsiders operating within a colonial expedition. The channels were Indigenous homelands before they became lines on Admiralty charts. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

FitzRoy’s 1839 narrative also records the taking of Fuegians to Britain during the first survey and the return of three survivors in 1833. This coercive history belongs beside the hydrographic achievements. European charting and naming should not be mistaken for the beginning of human geographical knowledge. The source’s labels and prejudices are not neutral descriptions of the people it encountered. Source 1 ↗

The atlas links individual documented visits, not continuous daily tracks. A dated entrance and exit establish two anchors; they do not reveal every tack, anchorage or delay between them. The voyage accounts make it possible to compare decisions without drawing a precision they do not support. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

No unsupported percentage decline in Cape Horn traffic is inferred here. The guide’s historical window ends in 1934.

Weather through the year: a shore station beside the strait

These are modern 1991–2020 monthly normals at Carlos Ibáñez airport, Punta Arenas, station 520006, published by Chile’s meteorological service. Temperature is the monthly mean air temperature; precipitation is the mean monthly accumulated amount in millimetres. Punta Arenas is a particular station near the strait, not a representative rain gauge for every western fjord or for Cape Horn. Source 1 ↗

The annual cycle is clear without treating summer as warm: the station’s January mean is 10.7 °C and July mean 1.7 °C. Precipitation occurs in every month. These long-term means do not describe the extremes, the number of wet days, or whether precipitation falls as rain or snow. Source 1 ↗

Wind, shelter and tides do not share one seasonal pattern

The regional wind map gives another scale of evidence: four three-month means from hourly ERA5 reanalysis, 1970–2024, on a 0.25° grid. Its panels are JFM, AMJ, JAS and OND. The study’s station observations show prevailing westerlies in each season and stronger winds at Punta Arenas and Porvenir during spring and summer. This is a reason to keep local wind seasonality separate from the broad ocean-wave cycle. Source 1 ↗

Tides vary sharply within the region. The same study’s January–June 2024 gauges recorded an M2 tidal constituent amplitude of 132.7 cm at Gregorio and 48.1 cm at Punta Arenas; Puerto Williams and Ushuaia were about 55 cm. Those are amplitudes of one tidal constituent, not total tidal ranges and not current speeds. Their contrast helps explain why a regional average cannot substitute for the conditions at a particular narrows. Source 1 ↗

FitzRoy describes severe gusts reaching an anchorage even where the sea surface was comparatively sheltered. That nineteenth-century observation belongs to a different evidence class from a modern gridded wind map. Neither supplies a reliable local monthly wave height for the Beagle Channel. Source 1 ↗

How high are the waves?

Significant wave height (Hs) describes a sea state: approximately the average height of its highest third of waves, or its spectral equivalent. It is not the height of the largest individual wave. A monthly mean Hs is an average of sea states; a 90th-percentile Hs is a level exceeded by about one tenth of the sampled sea states. Keeping those statistics separate makes the numbers useful. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Young and colleagues’ 2020 study combines 33 years of satellite altimetry with buoy and model evidence. Its January/July comparison describes the broad high-wave Southern Ocean belt, with mean Hs around 4 m in summer and 5 m in winter, and corresponding 90th percentiles around 5.5 and 7 m. These are approximate regional values, not measurements assigned to Cape Horn, Punta Arenas or a channel. The study also identifies lower wave energy east of South America. Source 1 ↗

Historical weather: observations, not a climate table

Logs tell us what a crew experienced and how it responded. They do not provide the evenly sampled observations needed for a monthly climatology. A frightening passage and an unusually easy one can both be accurate accounts. The following examples retain the event dates and the sailors’ decisions without converting descriptions into invented metre values. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

VoyageObserved periodWhat the account recordsWhat it cannot establish
Schouten and Le Maire south of Tierra del FuegoJanuary–February 1616The account records westerly billows, shifting winds, cold rain, hail and difficulty making westward progress. A favorable wind on one day did not settle the passage.Its large-wave descriptions are qualitative, and its position estimates need geographical checking. No numeric wave height is assigned.

Source 1 ↗

Beagle off the southern islandsJanuary 1833; especially FitzRoy’s account of 13 JanuaryA succession of large seas forced sail reductions; one wave put the lee bulwark under water and a quarter-boat was torn away. The ship later sought shelter.This was austral summer. It shows that summer offered no immunity from severe storms; it does not establish their annual frequency.

Source 1 ↗

Spray entering Magellan11–14 February 1896Slocum describes rough water and tide races at the entrance, squalls after entry, and the eventual anchorage at Sandy Point.The account distinguishes exposed approach, current and shelter. Modern broad-ocean Hs averages should not be attached to these individual episodes.

Read the maps from ocean scale to anchorage scale

Begin with IBCSO’s complete polar sheet: South America, Drake Passage, the Scotia Sea and Antarctica occupy part of one connected ocean. The chart extends from 50°S to the pole. Its modern 500 m grid and printed undersea names explain the setting; they do not show the seabed knowledge available to earlier mariners or a vessel’s track. Source 1 ↗

Then compare the Admiralty Strait of Magellan survey with the Beagle chart of Tierra del Fuego. The first follows the Atlantic–Pacific strait; the second resolves the southern channels, islands and headlands. Keep their dates, soundings and lettering attached to their original sheets. Blank areas and simplified shores are part of what the surveyors knew, not invitations to invent a precise route. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Finally use the seasonal wind plate and summer wave plate to compare spatial patterns. Their arrows, colour bars and percentile labels are essential. The NASA photograph and expedition sea-state photographs show modern geography and physical conditions only. They are not illustrations of the weather encountered by Magellan, Drake or Beagle.
