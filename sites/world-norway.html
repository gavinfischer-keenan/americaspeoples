THE SPREAD OF PEOPLES · DEEP DIVE

Norway from postglacial camps to 1900

The people who cut flint at Pauler, prepared stockfish for Bergen merchants and ran spinning machines beside Akerselva all worked with water, but in different ways. Retreating ice opened coastlines. Herring, cod and shipping connected ports. Fast rivers powered mills and carried materials toward towns. These histories come from deposits in the ground, surviving buildings and records that preserve different kinds of detail.

The regional map covers mainland Norway and nearby coastal islands. The Sápmi and Svalbard guides give their overlapping and distinct histories. Dates identify selected evidence through 1900. The outline and relief provide present-day geographic orientation.

How the evidence changes over time

The coast gave people a series of temporary opportunities as glaciers retreated, shorelines shifted and local habitats developed. Early site complexes can consist of separate scatters, and one radiocarbon result may date a particular feature. In later centuries, inscriptions or institutions can establish a named place or dated decision with far greater precision. The date labels preserve this difference. [1] [2] [15]

Coastal activity and the long memory of rock

At Pauler, concentrations of stone-working debris help locate activities that left few other remains. Southwestern sites such as Galta come from an equally mobile setting. The old landforms often stand above the present water, so today’s map is a reference for finding sites and imagining an earlier shoreline. Drowned or eroded sites also make the surviving archaeological record selective. Hummervikholmen preserves a different line of evidence in human remains from the southwest shore. [1] [2] [3]

Around Alta, repeatedly visited surfaces preserve images distributed over millennia. Changing water levels, panel position and differences between carving traditions help order them. Solbakk’s boat carvings give another regional comparison. A carved boat tells us that the form mattered, while its meaning and the circumstances of the carving need to be studied panel by panel. [4] [18]

Burial ships, market plots and coastal household work

The Flagghaugen grave at Avaldsnes gives evidence of contacts long before the Viking Age. Imported and locally valued objects accumulated in only a small portion of the population’s graves. Kaupang instead offers workshop and settlement evidence, including small artifacts lost or discarded in ordinary work. The Oseberg, Gokstad and Tune burials preserve exceptional vessels and burial labor. Taken together, these sites show that exchange and boatbuilding drew on many people beyond the people commemorated in an elite grave. [5] [6] [7] [8] [23]

Farther north at Vega, households combined fishing, farming and the tending of eider nesting places. The archipelago’s buildings and cultural landscape preserve the practical demands of sheltering birds, working seasonal fisheries and maintaining small holdings. One extensive heritage landscape can contain household traditions of quite different ages. The later continuity of the practices does not establish identical use on each island through the entire medieval and modern period. [9]

Church builders and merchants

Nidaros Cathedral grew in stages around a shrine and pilgrimage center. At Urnes, builders integrated carved material from an earlier building into a timber church, giving the surviving structure more than one date. The royal church at Avaldsnes and the two sites farther north show how religious sites occupied different positions along travel and political networks. Deciding when a community changed religious practices requires evidence beyond the dedication of any one building. [10] [11] [24]

Bryggen situates a medieval economy at the scale of warehouses, passages and ships. Stockfish brought preserved catches into long-distance trade. Merchant privileges and building layouts make part of that network visible, while the dispersed work of preparing the fish remains a different kind of evidence. Bergen’s surviving wooden buildings include rebuilding; a named wharf and its current building fabric have distinct histories. [12] [9]

Mining, roads and the political life of the nineteenth century

Silver at Kongsberg and copper at Røros brought demand for fuel, pack transport, provisions and skilled work. Røros’s wider mining district mattered as much as the central streets. Fires, war and rebuilding changed the structures that preserve that labor. In the late eighteenth century the Kongevegen over Filefjell made a planned connection across severe terrain and gathered a longer history of road building into an identifiable project. [25] [13] [14]

In 1814 the Treaty of Kiel, the Eidsvoll assembly, conflict and a revised constitution produced several distinct changes. Norway retained domestic institutions in its union with Sweden. Political participation also changed within that system as people organized across farms, workshops and towns. The Lofthus movement and the later Thrane associations document public action outside parliamentary office. The reforms of 1884 and 1898 tell a later story about the cabinet and electorate. [15] [15] [19] [21] [16]

Factory workers along Akerselva, including many women, built wage economies around the city’s falling water. The 1854 railway between Christiania and Eidsvoll added timetabled transport to a growing productive region. The Stavanger departure of the Restauration in 1825 offers a different scale of mobility: households used one small vessel to reach a transatlantic migrant network. These dated records show the infrastructure people could use at particular moments. [17] [22] [20]
