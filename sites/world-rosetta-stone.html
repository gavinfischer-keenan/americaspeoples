The Rosetta Stone

Near the mouth of the Nile in 1799, French troops found a slab that had been reused as building stone. More than two thousand years earlier, Egyptian priests had ordered copies of their decree set up in temples. Three versions survive on the fragment. Comparing them helped scholars recover how hieroglyphs recorded the sounds of Egyptian.

From Ptolemaic Egypt in 196 BCE to nineteenth-century decipherment and the debate over the stone’s custody.

The priests’ decree at Memphis

The inscription records a decision by a council of priests in 196 BCE at Memphis, where the young Ptolemy V Epiphanes celebrated his coronation. The decree describes royal benefits such as temple support, remission of certain dues and relief for prisoners. In return the priests prescribed new honors for the king, including cult images and recurring observances. It shows priests publicly affirming a dynasty that was dealing with revolts and political strain. [7] [1] [8]

The closing instructions called for copies in temples. Other fragments and versions of the Memphis Decree help scholars compare readings. Memphis marks the council that issued the decree, and Rashid marks where this particular fragment was recovered. The complete route from its original temple setting to the later fort is uncertain. [7] [2] [8]

Three scripts and two languages

The upper register is Egyptian in hieroglyphs, the middle is Egyptian in Demotic and the lower register is Ancient Greek. All three communicate the decree, with differences in formulation. The stone is a fragment of a much larger granodiorite stela. The hieroglyphic register has lost much of its text, so the surviving Greek and Demotic carry more of the decree. [6] [1] [9]

What the stone says about itself

The Greek version ends with instructions to write the decree on hard stone in the three scripts visible on the slab. The copies were to stand near images of the king in temples. The decree sets out both the honors the priests had agreed to observe and a way to display that decision in the different registers of Ptolemaic government and ritual. [8] [9] [7]

The fragment at Rashid

In 1799, during the French occupation, troops rebuilding a fort near Rashid uncovered the inscribed fragment in reused building material. French officer Pierre-François Bouchard is associated with recognizing the find. The fort is now called Qaitbay Fort and is also known as Fort Julien. The mapped point indicates the vicinity of the fort. Copies of the inscriptions circulated, allowing scholars to work from impressions and drawings far from the original. [1] [3] [2] [4]

How the decipherment worked

Silvestre de Sacy and Johan David Åkerblad used the Greek text to identify names and some sound values in Demotic. Thomas Young developed correspondences between Demotic and hieroglyphic signs and worked out sound values in the hieroglyphic name of Ptolemy. A royal name inside an oval enclosure, called a cartouche, gave scholars a manageable group to align with a name in Greek. [7] [1]

Jean-François Champollion compared names from multiple monuments and drew on his knowledge of Coptic, a later form of Egyptian. Reading older Egyptian royal names as well as Greek and Roman ones helped establish that sound signs were fundamental to the system. His 1822 letter to Bon-Joseph Dacier announced the breakthrough. A wider understanding of how sound signs worked alongside word signs and classifiers grew through further study of Egyptian texts. [7] [1] [11] [12] [13]

From Alexandria to London

British and Ottoman forces defeated the French in 1801. With the capitulation at Alexandria, British officials took possession of the stone. It entered the British Museum in 1802, where it is catalogued as EA24. The military handover, the modern collection and the stone’s possible ancient display site are separate parts of its history. [4] [5] [6] [7]

Egyptian archaeologist and former antiquities minister Zahi Hawass has called for the stone’s return, citing its removal by occupying powers. Repatriate Rashid frames its case around Egyptian stewardship and the significance of Rashid itself. The British Museum recounts the transfer under the terms of the military surrender and displays the original in London. The debate concerns who should decide where an Egyptian cultural object is kept and displayed. [14] [15] [1] [6]
