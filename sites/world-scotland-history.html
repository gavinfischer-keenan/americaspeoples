Scotland and its islands through 1900

From struck flint in South Lanarkshire to the steel spans of the Forth Bridge, surviving places record very different ways of moving, living and governing. Scotland’s mainland and islands connect to each other and to Ireland, Scandinavia and wider Atlantic routes. Those connections have their own timelines.

Mainland Scotland, the Hebrides, Orkney and Shetland through 1900. Separate landscape guides cover selected island and mainland monuments in greater depth.

After the ice

South Lanarkshire’s late-glacial flint scatter resembles tools found elsewhere in northern Europe, giving archaeologists a basis for assessing its age. Mesolithic hearths and hazelnut shells at Cramond can be placed more closely in time. Offshore and coastal sediments preserve changes in sea level and the Storegga tsunami, helping explain why some early coastal settings are difficult to find or recognise today. [1] [2] [27]

Island houses and monument landscapes

Stone houses in Orkney preserve hearths, rooms and furniture. Uprights at Calanais in Lewis and the surviving tower at Mousa in Shetland belong to other landscapes and activities separated by long periods of time. The linked island guides show how these buildings fit among smaller remains, farms and routes by sea. [3] [4] [5] [6]

Military corridors and maritime communities

Roman military movement left roads and fortifications, including the Antonine line and the corridor near Trimontium. Later, Dunadd organised craft, exchange and royal authority in Argyll, while religious communities at Iona and Portmahomack developed different ties around the Irish Sea and North Sea. These networks can be traced through excavation, objects and historical records. [7] [28] [8] [9] [10] [11]

Norse settlements, Scottish kingdoms and unions

Norse households at Jarlshof and the cathedral at Kirkwall are evidence for northern island histories. The pledges and annexation of Orkney and Shetland between 1468 and 1472 changed their relationship to the Scottish Crown. Mainland struggles over sovereignty produced events and documents such as Bannockburn and the Arbroath letter. Parliamentary union with England followed in 1707, a century after the union of crowns. [12] [13] [14] [18] [16] [17] [20]

Land, work and movement

Culloden and its aftermath changed some patterns of authority in the Highlands. Later estate decisions brought displacement in places such as Sutherland. Crofters drew on local organisation to press for protections. In the industrial regions the mills at New Lanark, the growing population of Glasgow and the Forth railway crossing illustrate different effects of demands for labour and transport.
