Reading the building through time

Earlier structures probably belong to the early ninth century; later masonry includes work around 850–925. A gap in available construction dates around 975–1040 allows several interpretations, including reduced occupation or residence without newly dated building work. Renewed construction and later reuse make this a changing place. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Room 33: what the dates and differences can show

Plog and Heitman argue that exceptional burials support early inequality connected with ancestors and authority. The checked table reports combined calibrated two-sigma ranges of 691–877 for Burial 13 and 690–873 for Burial 14. These ranges and their medians are probability summaries, not exact burial years, and they do not date construction of the room. Source 1 ↗

Exceptional treatment in Room 33’s burials supports Plog and Heitman’s argument that ancestors and authority contributed to early inequality. These particular individuals illuminate differences within the community without establishing the status of every household or identifying a named ruler. Source 1 ↗

Production, wealth and competing explanations

Evidence of ornament making at Bonito complicates a strict division between small-house producers and great-house consumers. Mattson’s analysis still supports social differences while comparing arrangements involving household contributions, pilgrimage and production within great houses. Bonito’s distinctive assemblage cannot automatically represent every great house. Source 1 ↗

The Chaco guide adds the road map and comparisons with small houses and other great houses. Roads can establish material connections without settling whether every connection served transport, ceremony or political control. Source 1 ↗

After the building peak

Later use changes a simple abandonment story. The thirteenth-century evidence and possible connections to northern arrivals remain local interpretations, while modern Pueblo relationships to Chaco draw on multiple ancestral histories. The regional guide follows those changes without treating a modern community as a static copy of this house. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Pueblo Bonito — habitation evidence through successive rebuilding

Pueblo Bonito preserves habitation evidence from the ninth century into at least the early twelfth, through repeated rebuilding and changes in use.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Inhabited from the mid-850s until at least the early twelfth century.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; calendar CE, not radiocarbon BP.

The rounded 850–1125 envelope locates a long habitation history, not continuous use of every room. Crown and Wills identify a probable temporary late-tenth-century hiatus; the broad envelope must not erase such internal gaps. Public mapped archaeological place, rounded to six decimals. Site point, not a surveyed footprint or room plan.

Pueblo Bonito — later twelfth-century reoccupation

Some parts of Pueblo Bonito were reoccupied in the later twelfth century. Activity at the great house therefore did not end automatically when Chaco’s regional prominence changed.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Some reoccupation in the late twelfth century.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; calendar CE, not radiocarbon BP.

1150–1199 is a broad latter-half-century envelope for a source that says late twelfth century. It does not establish the first return year, extent of reuse, or continuous occupation throughout the window. Public mapped archaeological place, rounded to six decimals. Site point, not a surveyed footprint or room plan.

Pueblo Bonito — thirteenth-century use

Ceramics and architectural changes indicate use of parts of Pueblo Bonito into the thirteenth century, after its great-house building peak.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Ceramics suggest continued occupation into the A.D. 1200s.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; calendar CE, not radiocarbon BP.

The century is an evidence envelope, not an exact departure year. A possible connection with migrants from the Mesa Verde area is the authors’ tentative interpretation, not a demonstrated route or assignment of every resident. Public mapped archaeological place, rounded to six decimals. Site point, not a surveyed footprint or room plan.

Pueblo Bonito — documented building

The earliest building at Pueblo Bonito dates to about 850 CE. Successive additions continued through about 1150; this marker locates the great house rather than the entire Chaco region.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 850–850 CE; see claim for phase scope.
Dating method: Published archaeological chronology; approximate bounds retain the source’s chronological resolution.

Construction evidence, not a census or a claim of residence through the present. The established place remains visible as history after this phase. Public mapped archaeological place, rounded to six decimals. Site point, not a surveyed footprint or room plan.
