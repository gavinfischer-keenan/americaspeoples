Göbekli Tepe, Çatalhöyük & Hattusha

Anatolia preserves the globe’s sharpest transformation from wild-foraging monumentality to dense egalitarian farming towns and imperial Bronze Age kingdoms—connecting Upper Euphrates limestone sanctuaries with Konya Plain murals and central Anatolian cuneiform archives.

UNESCO sites 1572 (Göbekli Tepe), 1405 (Çatalhöyük) and 377 (Hattusha) integrated with architectural geometry, shared symbolism and ceramic-neolithic radiocarbon studies.

The whole area in view

Connects southeastern Anatolian Taş Tepeler limestone sites (Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe) across the Konya Plain (Çatalhöyük) north to Hittite Hattusha.

  1. Göbekli Tepe (Şanlıurfa)

    c. 9600–8000 BCE

    Pre-Pottery Neolithic oval stone enclosures framing carved up to 5.5-meter T-shaped limestone pillars, bedrock cisterns and feasting bone dumps. [11] [4] [5]

    37.22°, 38.92°

  2. Karahan Tepe (Taş Tepeler)

    c. 9400–8000 BCE

    Sister bedrock-carved circular pillar complex reinforcing Upper Mesopotamian PPN shared symbolic architecture and human-head reliefs. [3] [1]

    37.09°, 39.30°

  3. Çatalhöyük (Konya Plain)

    c. 7100–5700 BCE

    Streetless tell of contiguous mudbrick houses entered via rooftop ladders, rich in under-floor burials, obsidian mirrors and bull-horn installations. [12] [6] [7]

    37.67°, 32.83°

  4. Hattusha (Boğazköy)

    c. 1650–1180 BCE

    Fortified Hittite imperial capital boasting Lion and Sphinx Gates, Yazılıkaya open-air rock reliefs and Indo-European cuneiform archives. [13] [8] [9]

    40.02°, 34.61°

Enclosures before agriculture and roof-entered mudbrick towns

At Göbekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe above the Harran Plain, hunter-gatherer groups sculpted limestone T-pillars up to 16 tons carved with foxes, boars, vultures, scorpions and human arms before full plant domestication appeared in the valley. Geometric spatial analyses across Enclosures B, C and D reveal deliberate equilateral triangular planning and vast gazelle-hunting feast middens fueled by rainwater-harvesting bedrock channels and newly recognized semi-subterranean dwellings. [11] [4] [5] [2]

Two millennia later on the Konya wetland alluvial fan, Çatalhöyük packed thousands of villagers into honeycombed mudbrick households linked across flat communal roofs. Rebuilt over eighteen stratified levels through 5700 BCE, residents cultivated domestic wheat, herded sheep and cattle, traded Cappadocian obsidian as far as the Levant and plastered walls with wild-bull, leopard and landscape depictions. [12] [7] [6]

Imperial statecraft on the Kızılırmak Bend: Hittite Hattusha

By 1650 BCE, northern central Anatolian Hattusha expanded over fortified limestone crags inside the Kızılırmak Bend. Armed with rock-cut corbelled tunnels (Yerkapı potern), double casemate mudbrick-on-stone walls and monumental Lower and Upper City temple quarters, Hittite kings preserved tens of thousands of Akkadian and Hittite clay tablets—documenting diplomatic parity with New Kingdom Egypt—before sudden Late Bronze Age dissolution around 1180 BCE. [13] [8] [10] [9]

3. Stratigraphy, Chronological Controls & Inter-Regional Reach

Published excavations and remote-sensing surveys across Göbekli Tepe (Şanlıurfa), Karahan Tepe (Taş Tepeler), Çatalhöyük (Konya Plain) distinguish monumental construction events from long centuries of everyday craft, agricultural provisioning, and ritual reuse—relying on radiocarbon series, ceramic seriation, epigraphy, and geoarchaeological sediment cores rather than unverified royal annals alone. [1]

Exchange connections preserved across metallurgical ores, timber/stone sourcing, exotic prestige wares, and architectural styles demonstrate how each local horizon sat inside much broader continental corridors across asia-pacific. [2]

4. Conservation Context, Public Heritage & Evidentiary Limits

Ongoing conservation balancing living community custodianship, climate/erosion stressors, and non-invasive geophysical mapping continues to refine older 19th- and 20th-century excavation hypotheses. Map coordinates pinpoint documented archaeological properties and research zones rather than rigid ancient territorial borders. [11] [1]
