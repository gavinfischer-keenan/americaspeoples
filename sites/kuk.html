Kuk

Kuk preserves separate episodes of cultivation and water management in the New Guinea highlands. It is an agricultural landscape with changing practices, not a single town founded once and occupied unchanged for ten thousand years.

Early plant-use evidence and the later engineering of mounds and ditches answer different questions. The Phase 1 authors disagree about whether channel 101 was substantially artificial. Gardens, fallows and tools

Channels moved water, while field surfaces and raised ground affected the conditions around roots. The authors discuss soil fertility, crop pests, drought and the practical costs of maintaining ditches. These are explanations for changing land use, not measurements of annual crop yields. Source 1 ↗

A wooden spade associated with dated ditch wood makes labour tangible: people dug, recut and maintained drainage. The evidence also allows disuse and renewal. Some older alignments were reused in later gardening, while others survived as abandoned features. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

A period without wetland gardening does not imply an empty valley. The scholarly reconstruction distinguishes wetland systems from surrounding dryland practices. The atlas does not turn the later reuse of one ditch into a continuous line through every earlier gap. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Kawelka history, warfare and the twentieth-century observation

Kawelka oral histories connect departure from Kuk with defeat in warfare. Published reconstructions suggest dates around 1914 or 1920. This is represented as an approximate local episode; the record does not invent battle participants, casualties or a precisely surveyed relocation route. Source 1 ↗

In 1933, European visitors found the wetland overgrown and uninhabited. That observation follows departure and does not extinguish customary ownership. It must not be generalized into an empty Wahgi Valley. Later histories of return, research and land use lie beyond the shared atlas’s 1934 endpoint. Source 1 ↗

Kuk — renewed gardens in the sweet-potato era

Gardening resumed after a short interruption following Tibito ashfall. Sweet potatoes changed cultivation and pig husbandry during this later phase.

Source date guidance: Approximately 1700 CE through the nineteenth century; final phase ended in early twentieth century
Dating method: Calendar BCE uses astronomical year 1 minus BCE; calibrated BP uses 1950 minus BP. Published model/phase bounds are retained, not recalibrated. Uncalibrated radiocarbon ages are not converted.

Rounded period of the phase, not a directly dated first sweet potato at Kuk. Some ditch systems reused earlier alignments, while others did not.

Kuk — an overgrown wetland observed in 1933

Kuk - early mounded cultivation

Kuk preserves early mounded cultivation dated within the seventh millennium before present in the UNESCO chronology.

Source date guidance: Phase onset reported as 6950–6440 calibrated years BP (Denham et al. 2003).
Dating method: Published calendar estimate; cal BP converted as 1950 minus BP; BCE as astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No new calibration.

Cultivation does not date first highland settlement; the institutional chronology is rounded.

Kuk - ditch systems

Ditch systems document a later phase of agricultural engineering at Kuk, separate from earlier mounded cultivation.

Source date guidance: Phase onset reported as 4350–3980 calibrated years BP (Denham et al. 2003).
