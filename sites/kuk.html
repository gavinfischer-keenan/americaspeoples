Kuk

Kuk preserves separate episodes of cultivation and water management in the New Guinea highlands. It is an agricultural landscape with changing practices, not a single town founded once and occupied unchanged for ten thousand years.

A closer reading

Generations of drainage and cultivation

At Kuk, people changed growing conditions by shaping ground and moving water. Mounds, channels and later field systems record practical work at the wetland edge. A wooden spade associated with dated ditch wood makes that labor unusually tangible: drainage depended on digging, maintenance and choices about which older features to reuse.

Evidence: press-files.anu.edu.au [1] ↗ · press-files.anu.edu.au [2] ↗

The locality also belongs to Kawelka history. Oral accounts connect a departure in the early twentieth century with warfare, while later observations describe an overgrown wetland. Customary ownership and wider community history continued through those changes. A field system falling out of use is one event in a lived landscape, rather than a final end to its human relationships.

Evidence: press-files.anu.edu.au [1] ↗

During Kuk’s fourth drainage phase, roughly two thousand to eleven hundred years ago, cultivators cut major channels and smaller ditches into the swamp. This drainage changed where crops could grow and how water left cultivated ground. The wider highland economy included long-established starch crops such as bananas, taro and yams. Maintaining usable fields required repeated attention to water as well as planting and harvest.

Evidence: press-files.anu.edu.au [1] ↗

Later farmers returned to wetland cultivation after an interval of disuse. Broad, flatter-bottomed ditches and raised fields characterize the fifth phase. Recut ditches preserve successive episodes of maintenance, while a wooden spade connects the large pattern of field engineering to a tool held by an individual worker. Volcanic ash layers help separate the episodes, showing how new cultivation reused and altered an already worked landscape.

Evidence: press-files.anu.edu.au [1] ↗

Around the beginning of the eighteenth century, renewed drainage coincided broadly with the spread of sweet potato through highland economies. The crop could support people and pigs on a wide range of soils. Its effects on production and exchange developed over time rather than appearing everywhere at once. At Kuk, some earlier channels were reopened, linking new agricultural circumstances with a much older local tradition of managing wet ground.

Gardens, fallows and tools

Channels moved water, while field surfaces and raised ground affected the conditions around roots. The authors discuss soil fertility, crop pests, drought and the practical costs of maintaining ditches. These are explanations for changing land use, not measurements of annual crop yields. Source 1 ↗

A wooden spade associated with dated ditch wood makes labour tangible: people dug, recut and maintained drainage. The evidence also allows disuse and renewal. Some older alignments were reused in later gardening, while others survived as abandoned features. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Wetland gardening and cultivation on surrounding dry land had different histories at Kuk. Ditches could fall out of use and later be reused while farming continued elsewhere in the valley. Gaps in one agricultural system therefore need not mean that people left the wider landscape. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Kawelka history, warfare and the twentieth-century observation

Kawelka oral histories connect departure from Kuk with defeat in warfare. Published reconstructions suggest dates around 1914 or 1920. This is represented as an approximate local episode; the record does not invent battle participants, casualties or a precisely surveyed relocation route. Source 1 ↗

European visitors found Kuk’s wetland overgrown and uninhabited in 1933, following Kawelka departure from the locality. Customary ownership continued, and the observation did not describe the whole Wahgi Valley. Later return, research, and land use added further chapters to this history. Source 1 ↗
