A new hilltop city

Around 500 BCE, Monte Albán's founders established a hilltop city whose residential terraces transformed a demanding setting. Households shared retaining walls and drainage arrangements, while farming and small-scale irrigation tied the settlement to surrounding land. Nicholas and Feinman use these material relationships to argue that coordinated production and comparatively collective institutions helped the city grow. Their argument redirects attention from a single ruler to the work needed to sustain neighborhoods. The approximate foundation date introduces this process rather than dating every terrace or irrigation feature. Source 1 ↗

Images made and remade

The Danzantes carvings were not fixed forever in their first architectural setting. Winter argues that the original wall was dismantled after a relatively short period and that its stones entered later buildings. This reuse made earlier images available for new acts of remembrance. The figures themselves remain contested: a reading as remembered founding participants differs sharply from interpretations emphasizing captives and violence. The city-phase record anchors the viewing frame, while the wall's relative sequence provides a discussion of memory without inventing an exact demolition year. Source 1 ↗

Early urban era; relative sequence, not a dated demolition. The viewing year anchors the existing city phase, not the relative wall-reuse event. Source 1 ↗

Structure J and uncertain place names

Structure J incorporates carved monuments whose texts and images have often been read as a register of conquered places. Carter reassesses the proposed readings and the certainty of those identifications. The carvings are attributed to the Pe phase, approximately 300–100 BCE, but that estimate is not automatically the construction date of the entire building. Visitors can examine a specific material source for political claims while seeing why uncertain place names cannot safely become an imperial territory drawn on the map. Source 1 ↗

Pe-phase carving estimate 300–100 BCE; not automatically Structure J construction. Source 1 ↗

A ballcourt and public space

The ballcourt associated with the North Platform adds a named public structure to Monte Albán's monumental and residential landscape. INAH places its construction around 100 BCE. Its presence helps shift the narrative from the founding of a settlement to the repeated addition of spaces for public activity. The broad city record and the more specific ballcourt occurrence perform different jobs: one keeps the settlement in view, while the other marks a particular architectural intervention. Neither establishes a complete schedule for all surrounding plaza buildings. Source 1 ↗

Approximate construction 100 BCE. Source 1 ↗

Homes, ancestors and intercity contacts

The residence associated with Tomb 105 gives the Classic city a household and ancestral scale. Its existing construction record, approximately 500–800 CE, can be reused without creating a second version of the same claim. Mortuary space, residence and the wider terrace landscape belong in the same account of urban life. Connections with Teotihuacan are part of an earlier intercity history described by INAH, not evidence that all local political changes were imposed from outside. The scene places elite architecture beside the labor and food production sustaining the city. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Residence construction window 500–800; earlier contacts 200–500 are retrospective context. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Political decentralization

Between roughly 800 and 900 CE, Monte Albán lost population and much of its earlier monumental prominence as the Oaxaca Valley's political geography changed. Feinman and Nicholas challenge the older picture of a simple Mixtec invasion replacing Zapotec society. A declining urban center and a reorganizing regional population are different historical processes. The existing city-phase record remains current here, while the guide explains decentralization as a transition in where people and institutions concentrated, without treating the end of major construction as the disappearance of all communities. Source 1 ↗

Approximate demographic/monumental change 800–900. Source 1 ↗

Reusing an ancestral tomb

Tomb 7 combines an older burial structure with later Postclassic objects and activity. The broad 900–1520 CE interval describes that later assemblage and reuse, not the original construction of the tomb or a precisely dated single burial. This layered setting gives the final scene a different purpose from the earlier story of city growth. Localized Mixtec presence, particularly in later centuries, must be considered alongside continuing Zapotec communities. The site's ancestral significance could persist even as its former urban and political role changed. Source 1 ↗
