A closer reading

Making a hilltop city

Around 500 BCE, Monte Albán’s early inhabitants transformed a demanding hilltop setting with residential terraces. Retaining walls and drainage tied neighboring households to shared practical problems. Farming and small-scale irrigation linked the city to surrounding land. These material relationships make collective work an important part of explaining how the settlement grew.

Evidence: frontiersin.org [1] ↗

The city also remade its own images. Danzantes carvings were dismantled from an earlier setting and reused in later buildings. Their meanings remain disputed, but their movement provides a tangible history of how older monuments acquired new contexts. Stone images carried into a new building could connect a changing city with remembered versions of its past.

Evidence: cambridge.org [1] ↗

A new hilltop city

Around 500 BCE, Monte Albán's founders established a hilltop city whose residential terraces transformed a demanding setting. Households shared retaining walls and drainage arrangements, while farming and small-scale irrigation tied the settlement to surrounding land. Nicholas and Feinman use these material relationships to argue that coordinated production and comparatively collective institutions helped the city grow. Their argument redirects attention from a single ruler to the work needed to sustain neighborhoods. The approximate foundation date introduces this process rather than dating every terrace or irrigation feature. Source 1 ↗

Approximate foundation 500 BCE, encoded 1−500=-499. Source 1 ↗

Images made and remade

Monte Albán’s Danzantes carvings were moved and reused in later buildings. Winter argues that their original wall was dismantled after a relatively short period, giving the earlier images new settings for remembrance. The figures’ meanings remain disputed: Winter’s interpretation as remembered founding participants differs from readings that emphasize captives and violence. The sequence of dismantling and reuse is better established than an exact demolition year. Source 1 ↗

Early urban era; relative sequence, not a dated demolition. The viewing year anchors the existing city phase, not the relative wall-reuse event. Source 1 ↗

Structure J and uncertain place names

Structure J incorporates carved monuments whose texts and images have often been read as a register of conquered places. Carter reassesses the proposed readings and the certainty of those identifications. The carvings are attributed to the Pe phase, approximately 300–100 BCE, but that estimate is not automatically the construction date of the entire building. Visitors can examine a specific material source for political claims while seeing why uncertain place names cannot safely become an imperial territory drawn on the map. Source 1 ↗

Pe-phase carving estimate 300–100 BCE; not automatically Structure J construction. Source 1 ↗

A ballcourt and public space

Builders added a ballcourt beside Monte Albán’s North Platform around 100 BCE, according to INAH. The court joined the city’s expanding landscape of public structures, residential terraces, and plaza buildings. Its construction records one particular addition to a city that had already been inhabited for centuries. Source 1 ↗

Approximate construction 100 BCE. Source 1 ↗

Homes, ancestors and intercity contacts

The residence associated with Tomb 105 connected elite domestic life with the commemoration of ancestors at Monte Albán. Its construction is broadly dated to 500–800 CE. Residence, burial space, and the surrounding terrace landscape formed parts of the same urban community, sustained by household work and food production. INAH also describes earlier connections with Teotihuacan, although those relationships do not establish that outside powers directed every local political change. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Residence construction window 500–800; earlier contacts 200–500 are retrospective context. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Political decentralization

Between roughly 800 and 900 CE, Monte Albán lost population and much of its monumental prominence as people and institutions became concentrated elsewhere in the Oaxaca Valley. Feinman and Nicholas challenge the older explanation of a simple Mixtec invasion replacing Zapotec society. They instead emphasize changes in regional settlement and political organization. The city’s decline formed part of this wider reorganization of inhabited communities. Source 1 ↗

Approximate demographic/monumental change 800–900. Source 1 ↗

Reusing an ancestral tomb

Tomb 7 combines an older burial structure with later Postclassic objects and activity. The broad 900–1520 CE interval describes that later assemblage and reuse, not the original construction of the tomb or a precisely dated single burial. This layered setting gives the final scene a different purpose from the earlier story of city growth. Localized Mixtec presence, particularly in later centuries, must be considered alongside continuing Zapotec communities. The site's ancestral significance could persist even as its former urban and political role changed. Source 1 ↗

Viewing year within broad Postclassic assemblage 900–1520; not precise burial date. Source 1 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Governance models range from coercive central control to collective institutions; present Nicholas/Feinman's argument as interpretation.

Danzantes identities and Structure J conquest readings remain disputed.

INAH describes a Teotihuacan invasion, but the evidence reviewed here does not independently establish that conflict.

The Danzantes stones were reused, but the timing of that reuse lacks a securely established calendar range.

Tomb 7’s later objects and reuse are broadly assigned to the Postclassic period. Their more precise deposition dates remain unresolved here.

The locations of particular buildings and ancient fields are not precise enough here to reconstruct territorial boundaries.
