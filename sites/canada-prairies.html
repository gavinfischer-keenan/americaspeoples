Hunting and processing in deep time

At Wally’s Beach in southern Alberta, cut marks on horse and camel bones accompany artifacts near an ancient river crossing. New treatment and radiocarbon measurement of the bones revised the hunting and butchering evidence to about 13,300 calibrated years before 1950. Several carcasses occur in a small area, but their proximity does not prove that they all belonged to one hunt. The findings show people dealing with animals no longer present on the plains, while leaving open the larger balance of causes behind their extinction. Source 1 ↗

The Atkinson site near Lauder, Manitoba, offers a different kind of evidence. A hearth, tools and bison remains belong to a published fifth-millennium BCE occupation envelope. The animal parts are interpreted as material brought to a processing place rather than proof that bison were killed exactly there. This distinction reveals the work between a hunt and a meal: selecting portions, carrying them, extracting useful food and using a campsite. Farther north, the Quarry of the Ancestors adds an Alberta stone-resource landscape to this wider picture. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Gardens beside rivers and prairie edges

Fishing and cultivation met at Lockport on the Red River. Excavators found charred maize kernels, substantial storage pits and hoes made from bison shoulder blades. A recent research synthesis places the farmers approximately 1250–1450 CE; an older government field guide highlights evidence around 1400. These materials demonstrate more than the presence of an imported food. Tools and storage facilities make cultivation part of the local economy. The rapids also offered productive fishing, so the site resists a simple story in which agriculture replaced every older food practice. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Southwestern Manitoba adds another farming landscape. At the Olson site south of Melita, bison scapula hoes helped researchers move beyond earlier hints of cultivation. Associated material dates to the late 1400s or early 1500s. Similarities with other farming tools and pottery invite comparisons across the northeastern plains and adjacent woodlands, but they do not establish a named people’s movement on their own. The tools make cultivation visible at a particular place, adding a practical history of planting and working soil to the prairie narrative alongside the better-known evidence for hunting. Source 1 ↗

Métis commerce, settlement and collective rights

The Red River Métis emerge in this selection as traders, political actors and makers of settlements. On 19 June 1816, Cuthbert Grant’s party clashed with Governor Robert Semple’s party at Frog Plain, also known as Seven Oaks, during escalating disputes over pemmican and the fur trade. The Manitoba Métis Federation remembers the victory as an assertion of rights to trade and travel in the homeland. This political significance need not turn the day into a literal beginning of Métis peoplehood; communities and relationships already made that assertion possible. Source 1 ↗

In 1872, Xavier Letendre established a ferry and commercial enterprise where the Winnipeg–Fort Carlton trail crossed the South Saskatchewan River. Batoche developed with stores, warehouses and family river lots. Its history therefore begins in this guide with work, transport and home-making rather than battle. The later 1885 confrontation between the Métis provisional government and Canadian forces grew amid disputes over land and authority. Canadian victory after four days of fighting did not erase the community: residents rebuilt lives there, even as subsequent economic changes and dispossession placed further pressures on them. Treaties, schools and the expanding Canadian state

Treaty gatherings were local events within much larger political relationships. Treaty 1 was concluded at Lower Fort Garry on 3 August 1871 between Crown representatives and Anishinaabe and Swampy Cree signatories. Treaty 7 followed at Blackfoot Crossing on 22 September 1877, involving the Blackfoot nations, Tsuut’ina and Stoney Nakoda. Blackfoot Crossing was already a gathering place, selected by Crowfoot for these negotiations. Marking the meetings makes the participants and geography visible; a treaty point cannot by itself explain every promise, later breach or differing understanding of what had been agreed. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The residential school at Lebret, also known as Qu’Appelle, is documented by NCTR from 1884 under Catholic administration. Placing it beside treaties and Métis settlements shows how colonial power entered communities through multiple institutions. Land negotiation, military intervention and the schooling of children were connected historical pressures, but each requires its own evidence and chronology. The school’s operation continued long after 1934. Ending this regional view at that year leaves those later experiences to further study; it does not imply that colonial harms, prairie communities or Indigenous political life stopped then. Places, finds and events on the map

Wally’s Beach — horse and camel butchering

Cut-marked horse and camel bones and associated artifacts at Wally’s Beach support hunting and butchering near an ancient river crossing. Revised radiocarbon work places these activities about 13,300 cal BP.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 13,300 cal BP
Dating method: 1950−13300=−11350; rounded published calibrated chronology, not new calibration.

Rounded calibrated-age anchor. Several carcasses need not represent one hunt; the evidence does not determine a migration route or prove that hunting alone caused megafaunal extinction.

Atkinson — bison processing near the Souris River

The Atkinson site near Lauder preserves a hearth, tools and bison remains interpreted as processing of transported animal portions. Published dates place the occupation in the fifth millennium BCE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4445–4225 calBC, published occupation envelope
Dating method: Astronomical year=1−BCE, giving−4444 to−4224. No raw radiocarbon ages newly calibrated.

The printed calibrated endpoints are retained; this is an occupation-evidence envelope, not a 220-year continuous camp. Processing remains are not automatically an on-site kill.

Lockport — maize, storage pits and bison-bone hoes

At Lockport, charred maize, underground storage pits and hoes made from bison shoulder blades document Indigenous horticulture beside a productive fishing place.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: c.1250–1450 CE; c.AD 1400 in older government field guide
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

The 1250–1450 synthesis is approximate. The older field guide emphasizes c.1400; neither dates every pit, identifies all farmers’ languages, or establishes uninterrupted cultivation.

South of Melita — hoes and pre-contact cultivation

Bison scapula hoes recovered at the Olson site provide evidence for local cultivation south of Melita. The research team dates material associated with the farmers to the late 1400s or early 1500s.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Late 1400s or early 1500s; approximate published wording
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

1475–1525 is an explicitly approximate quarter-century rendering of the source wording, not a radiocarbon confidence interval. Similar tools do not by themselves establish a specific ethnic identity or migration.

Batoche — ferry, store and river-lot community

Xavier Letendre opened a ferry crossing and commercial establishment at Batoche in 1872. The crossing connected a Métis settlement and river-lot farms with the Winnipeg–Fort Carlton trail.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1872
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

A documented commercial milestone, not first human presence. Later family-lot counts refer to the 1880s and are not assigned to 1872.

Blackfoot Crossing — Treaty 7 gathering

Representatives of the Blackfoot nations, Tsuut’ina, Stoney Nakoda and Crown concluded Treaty 7 at Blackfoot Crossing, a long-established gathering place selected by Crowfoot.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 22 September 1877
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

Lebret — Qu’Appelle residential school documented

The NCTR register dates the Catholic residential school at Lebret, also known as Qu’Appelle, from 1884. The institution became part of the prairie residential-school system.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1884, beginning of operation span in NCTR register
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

This dates documented institutional operation, not every building or child’s attendance. The institution continued beyond 1934; no closure is implied by the historical map cutoff.

Batoche — battle during the North-West Resistance

Canadian forces defeated the Métis defending Batoche after four days of fighting in May 1885. The battle was followed by prosecutions, but did not end the Métis community’s presence at Batoche.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 9–12 May 1885
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

Battle window only; the community was not destroyed or extinguished in 1885. Later economic decline and relocation have their own chronologies.

Quarry of the Ancestors — early archaeological context

At Quarry of the Ancestors (HhOv-319), artifacts in a periglacial sediment sequence support early human activity near the Pleistocene–Holocene transition. Associated upper aeolian sand yielded an IRSL age of 11.3 ± 0.8 ka.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 11.3 ±0.8 ka; Table 1 datum 2010 CE; 1 sigma
Dating method: 2010 minus 12,100 to 10,500 years; IRSL, not calibrated radiocarbon BP

Sediment age and archaeological stratigraphy constrain activity indirectly. This is neither an exact human-arrival date nor the date of Lake Agassiz flooding; Table 1 uses 2010 CE as datum.
