A closer reading

Water, households and a changing enclave

In the Moquegua valley, Wari-associated settlements depended on an agricultural landscape made possible by irrigation. A long canal served land around Cerro Baúl and Cerro Mejía, connecting hilltop architecture to fields and water management. The settlements and their supporting works grew through their own sequences rather than appearing together in one construction campaign.

Evidence: mdpi.com [1] ↗

Household evidence gives that landscape a social dimension. Architecture, foods and ways of preparing meals differed among residents. These contrasts support interpretation of a community with varied backgrounds, although they do not identify every person’s birthplace or language. Cooking, storage and access to water reveal everyday differences within the larger political setting of the enclave.

Making an irrigated colonial landscape

People in the Wari-associated Moquegua enclave built and maintained an agricultural landscape around their hilltop complexes. Nash describes a canal longer than 20 kilometers that brought at least 700 hectares into cultivation, with part operating by around AD 650. Cerro Baúl and nearby Cerro Mejía were settled before 700. Water management, farming, and household work sustained these neighboring settlements. The canal and buildings developed through their own sequences, and their broad dates do not establish a single founding campaign. Source 1 ↗

650 is an approximate by-then operating statement for part of the irrigation system. Source 1 ↗

Households with different ways of living

Households in the Moquegua enclave differed in their architecture, diets, and ways of preparing food. Cooking, storage, work, and access to water were central to everyday life at Cerro Baúl and Cerro Mejía. Nash interprets this variety as evidence of a polyethnic colonial community: people with different backgrounds living within the enclave. The household comparison concerns a broad archaeological phase around the period of irrigation development. It does not identify the birthplace, language, or reasons for moving of each resident. Source 1 ↗

Shares the approximately 650 irrigation orientation. Household diversity summarizes the wider enclave phase; it is not a census or a claim that each household existed in 650. Source 1 ↗

Rebuilding Cerro Baúl’s summit

Moseley and colleagues describe major reorganization around AD 800, when earlier Cerro Baúl summit structures were leveled or replaced. The later complex brought ceremonial buildings, residences and productive areas into a changed arrangement. D-shaped temples and a pottery workshop with raw materials and tools document different activities. A Nazca-style drum in a ceremonial context provides an object connection, without establishing the language or ethnicity of everyone present. This was rebuilding of an existing colony, rather than evidence of a single master plan completed in one year. The approximate phase does not directly date every wall or demonstrate that nearby settlements followed the same schedule. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Approximate contextual rebuilding phase; no precise common foundation for every component. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Work in the brewery and later hillside homes

Milling, boiling and fermentation took place in distinct areas of Cerro Baúl’s brewery. Seven boiling firepits and molle remains support a specific account of preparation, while serving vessels and feasting evidence connect that work to gatherings. Nearby Cerro Mejía has later household evidence than the older early-only model allowed. A burnt-seed sample from a late Unit 17 context has a reported calibrated component at AD 882–995, representing 90.2 percent probability. These are different observations: the summit brewery’s contextual phase is not the same measurement as the hillside household’s seed sample. Together they reveal varied productive and domestic activity across the enclave. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

900 is a shared orientation point; brewery phase and Mejía calibrated seed range have distinct semantics. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Closing a brewery, not erasing a region

Burning in the brewery and the breaking of serving vessels underpin an interpretation of ceremonial closure around AD 1000. The sequence does not establish a battle, identify an attacking army or show that every Wari-associated household left Moquegua simultaneously. The approximate date also comes from an older chronological synthesis. Later research emphasizes varied local histories, including later Cerro Mejía household samples and an unfinished project at Cerro Petroglifo. The brewery’s termination is therefore one building’s history within a wider changing region. Other communities and activities cannot be assigned the same ending simply because they participated in the Wari-associated enclave. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Approximate closure interpretation in older synthesis, not an exact radiocarbon year. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Interpretations and remaining questions

Cerro Baúl and Cerro Mejía preserve a local Moquegua sequence within the wider Wari-associated world. Huari and Pikillacta offer other settings whose histories should be compared without assuming that every center developed simultaneously.

Nash’s later household dates extend Cerro Mejía’s history beyond the earlier interpretation that occupation ended before 800. Old wood and reused beams can be older than the buildings in which they were placed.

The published ranges 882–995 and 887–995 contain 90.2% and 88.5% of their samples’ dating probability. They are parts of broader date distributions, rather than complete 95.4% ranges.

An offering at Cerro Petroglifo dates activity at an unfinished project rather than occupation of completed houses. Its possible dates fall in separated intervals within the broad 1045–1223 span.

Precise locations for particular houses, brewery spaces, and canal segments are not established here. Cerro Mejía and Cerro Petroglifo have archaeological evidence but lack verified public coordinates in this study.

Polyethnic is an archaeological interpretation; forced versus voluntary migration, languages and exact origins remain unresolved.

The earthquake chronology proposed for Pikillacta remains unverified in the sources used here.
