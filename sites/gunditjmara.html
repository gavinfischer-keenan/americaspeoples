A closer reading

Country, resistance and the return of land

Gunditjmara institutions describe continuing responsibilities to Country. Channels, barriers, houses and objects provide archaeological evidence of particular activities within that living history. They are material traces of relationships and work, rather than a complete measure of how long a people has belonged to a place.

Evidence: gunditjmirring.com [1] ↗ · whc.unesco.org [2] ↗

The history of Lake Condah Mission followed resistance to removal from Country. Colonial supervision and exclusionary policy constrained residence and family choices, while community connections to the site persisted. Reading those experiences alongside the earlier engineered landscape keeps both coercion and continuity visible. Neither mission records nor archaeological phases alone can contain the full history of Gunditjmara life.

Evidence: budjbim.com.au [1] ↗ · digital-classroom.nma.gov.au [2] ↗

Gunditjmara life around Budj Bim brought knowledge of water, stone, animals and seasons into a productive cultural landscape. Families managed kooyang, or short-finned eels, through systems that trapped, held and harvested them. Oral knowledge and practical skills passed between generations alongside the maintenance of channels and barriers. Basket weaving, storytelling and dance form part of the continuing cultural traditions associated with this Country. The engineering history is therefore also a history of people teaching, cooperating and maintaining relationships with places through repeated use.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗

European settlement in the 1830s brought conflict over Gunditjmara land. The community account describes more than twenty years of resistance in the Eumerella wars. As colonial authorities established reserves, many people refused to leave ancestral Country. The mission opened at Lake Condah in 1867, close to eel-trapping places and within sight of Budj Bim. Its location followed that resistance to removal. Although the mission imposed a colonial institution on community life, remaining in this landscape helped families sustain connections with places that mattered to them.

Evidence: budjbim.com.au [1] ↗

When the mission formally closed in 1918, Gunditjmara people protested and some continued living in its buildings. Much of the reserve was transferred to soldier settlement in the 1940s, and the government destroyed mission structures in the following decade. Families nevertheless remained in the district and protected their heritage. The return of mission land in 1987 marked a major change in control. Today the site carries family memories and the history of political struggle as well as the surviving remains of its buildings.

Evidence: budjbim.com.au [1] ↗

Traditional Owners put forward Budj Bim for World Heritage recognition in 1989. Three decades later, in July 2019, the cultural landscape was inscribed for its Aboriginal cultural significance. This public recognition followed generations of work to remain on Country and recover land. It placed an ancient aquaculture landscape and continuing Gunditjmara knowledge before an international audience. The sequence from dispossession through land return to heritage recognition adds a recent political history to the much longer story told by the channels, wetlands and house sites.

Work, knowledge and material evidence

The Muldoons sequence documents local engineering changes, while Kurtonitj documents a household context centuries later. Neither assemblage stands for every Gunditjmara person. Their different dates and methods are preserved so that community history is not compressed into one material style. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Mission life, coercion and choices

Lake Condah Mission followed resistance to removal from Country. Colonial supervision and the 1886 exclusionary policy then constrained residence.
