A living people, not an extinct archaeological category

Gunditjmara institutions describe continuing responsibilities to Country. Dated channels, barriers, houses, and objects record particular activities within that living history, whose duration extends beyond the archaeological sequence. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Work, knowledge and material evidence

The Muldoons sequence documents local engineering changes, while Kurtonitj documents a household context centuries later. Neither assemblage stands for every Gunditjmara person. Their different dates and methods are preserved so that community history is not compressed into one material style. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Mission life, coercion and choices

Lake Condah Mission followed resistance to removal from Country. Colonial supervision and the 1886 exclusionary policy then constrained residence. Describing those constraints also requires retaining community choices, family connections and the continuing importance of the site. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

How this study connects to Budj Bim

The Budj Bim study follows aquaculture engineering and the changing water landscape. This Gunditjmara study follows household life, resistance and mission history. Links between them connect these perspectives on Country and community life. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Lake Condah Mission: institution opened on Gunditjmara Country

Lake Condah Mission opened after Gunditjmara resistance to removal from their own Country; its location became an enduring focus of families and community organization.

Lake Condah Mission: formal closure and community persistence

The mission closed formally in 1918 and people were pressured or forced to leave, while continued residence, visits and community ties outlasted the administrative closure.
