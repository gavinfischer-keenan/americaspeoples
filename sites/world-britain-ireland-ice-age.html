Early people and changing landscapes of Britain and Ireland

One coastal find records footsteps in ancient mud. Another is a set of teeth. Others are marks on bones, hearth debris and worked stone. The map brings these discoveries together to show where and when archaeologists can place early activity in Britain and Ireland, across a record with long breaks.

Selected early hominin and postglacial evidence across Britain and Ireland. Dates before written history come from geological dating, radiocarbon measurements and comparison of objects. Rounded ages retain their uncertainty on the timeline.

Footprints, flakes and fossils

Footprints at Happisburgh carry a geological age range of about one million to 780,000 years ago. Pakefield and Boxgrove preserve evidence of other periods. At Boxgrove, bone and teeth give researchers a different way to ask about human ancestry than flint alone. The Welsh teeth at Pontnewydd document an early Neanderthal presence substantially later in the Middle Pleistocene. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

Burials and the reach of sparse finds

At Paviland the age of the ochre burial comes from the human remains themselves. In Ireland, the Castlepook claim depends on interpretation of a mark on a reindeer bone from a historic collection. Bone from an Irish bear gives stronger late-glacial evidence of human activity through microscopic study and radiocarbon dating. Neither Irish cave by itself records the length of anyone’s stay. [6] [7] [12]

Different paths back as climate shifted

Human remains at Gough’s Cave and Kendrick’s Cave have yielded genetic evidence for people with different ancestry affinities, within a small and unevenly preserved sample. At Howburn, similarities in the shape and working of stone tools matter more to dating than a layer sealed by later deposits. Flowstone above a carving at Creswell confines the age of the image from the other direction, giving a minimum age for the engraving. [8] [9] [10] [11]

Water, camps and burial after deglaciation

Wet ground preserved timber around Star Carr, while charred hazelnut shells helped date activity at Cramond. Pits and postholes at Mount Sandel anchor an early chronological sequence in the north of Ireland. The cremation at Hermitage reveals evidence of funerary practice. Sediments from the Storegga tsunami record part of the changing coastal geography, with the effects on individual settlements difficult to establish. [13] [14] [15] [16] [17]
