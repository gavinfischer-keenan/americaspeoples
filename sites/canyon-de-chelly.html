Canyon de Chelly / Tséyi’: a living homeland

Tséyi’, or Canyon de Chelly, holds Pueblo villages, Diné farms and art, and histories of military violence, forced removal, and return. Its 1931 designation as a national monument took place within Navajo land and a continuing Diné homeland.

Tséyi’: a lived landscape

Canyon de Chelly, or Tséyi’, brings together the main canyon, Canyon del Muerto, tributary canyons and surrounding uplands within the Navajo Nation. Water, cultivable ground, alcoves and pathways supported many forms of dwelling and farming. The surviving Pueblo buildings are one part of that history. Diné families continue to live and cultivate here, while Pueblo descendants maintain connections to ancestral places. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Dates in this study describe particular buildings, rebuilding episodes and historical events. Antelope House, White House and Mummy Cave did not all begin or cease use together. The current NPS history places a broader Puebloan departure in the mid-1300s; an older institutional account uses about 1300. Those broad summaries remain distinct from the excavator’s proposed earlier end to Antelope House’s main village around 1270. Neither account dates the disappearance of a people. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Antelope House: successive communities at one place

Excavation revealed earlier buildings beneath the masonry visible today. Structure 67 has a cluster of structural cutting dates in 691–693. A much larger Pueblo I village, with wood-and-adobe walls, was built around 825–850. Evidence becomes sparse during much of approximately 950–1100, although structures remained in the south. The later village therefore should not be displayed as seven centuries of unchanged, continuous occupation. Source 1 ↗

The later masonry settlement grew through rooms added around kivas, increased building heights, new floors and changes in storage space. The circular Central Plaza was a late addition above Kiva C, followed by further remodeling. Morris’s excavation report places this construction after 1200; the 2013 visitor brochure’s 1300s date is incompatible with that sequence. The report’s late dates are less secure than its early structural dates, but its reasoning can be examined directly. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

White House and Mummy Cave: reading different kinds of dates

White House combines a lower multistory pueblo with an upper alcove dwelling, whose white plaster gave the complex its familiar name. NPS summarizes roof-timber evidence as building activity beginning around 1060 with additions as late as 1275. Breternitz’s analysis also records an earlier timber, a cluster in the 1070s and a late occupation associated with Mesa Verde pottery. A timber’s last ring, a building addition and a village’s final use are different observations. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

At Mummy Cave, a more specific claim is possible: Breternitz dates the tower’s second and third floors to 1284. This does not date every room in its east and west alcoves. NPS connects the central tower complex with movement from Mesa Verde in the 1280s, an interpretation that belongs beside the architecture and tree-ring evidence. It cannot establish the birthplace of every builder or a traced migration route. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Diné art, farming and memory

Antelope House’s familiar name also points to a later history. The NPS North Rim guide records local Navajo attribution of antelope paintings beside the village to Dibe Yazhi, or Little Sheep, who lived here in the 1830s. The later brochure uses the broader early 1800s. The map preserves this attribution without turning it into a scientifically measured pigment date or assigning all nearby images to one artist. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

In her contribution to the NPS brochure, Diné writer Ailema Benally explains relationships among landscape, language, families and responsibility passed between generations. This perspective helps readers understand why Tséyi’ is more than a collection of old buildings. Morris’s excavation report also records Dan Anagal’s account of family use of remodeled Antelope House rooms before the Second World War. These are attributed statements, rather than a claim to speak for all Diné people. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Military violence, captivity and return

In January 1805, a Spanish force commanded by Antonio Narbona attacked a Navajo refuge at the place now called Massacre Cave. Bradley’s handbook reproduces part of the commander’s letter and separately describes a Navajo account centered on people taking shelter. Narbona reported 115 killed and 33 captives; those figures and his classification of the dead remain attributed to him. They are not treated as an independent count, and the story of the refuge is not reduced to the commander’s categories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

U.S. troops entered Canyon de Chelly in 1864 and destroyed homes, orchards, and livestock to force Diné surrender. The campaign formed part of the Long Walk and confinement at Bosque Redondo. Families experienced different departures and routes, while some people remained in the homeland. Return followed in 1868. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The 1868 treaty enabled Diné people to return to a limited reservation within their homeland. Rebuilding followed severe losses, and return did not restore every earlier land right. Living communities continued the history of residence, work, and responsibility in the canyon. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

1931: protecting archaeology on Navajo land

The national monument’s creation involved negotiations and local concern about land use. Navajo council approval did not eliminate questions from canyon residents: a 1930 petition objected to boundaries that might restrict grazing and collecting wood. A later petition accepted assurances that existing rights would remain. These discussions show residents as participants with specific concerns, rather than an empty landscape being set aside for visitors. Source 1 ↗

Congress authorized the monument on 14 February 1931, and President Hoover proclaimed it on 1 April. The enabling act reserved Navajo land interests and agricultural and grazing uses while assigning preservation and related infrastructure responsibilities to NPS. The monument’s creation did not begin Diné residence or transfer away all Navajo interests. Contemporary NPS history continues to describe a living community and a partnership with the Navajo Nation. How to read this local map

White House, Antelope House and Mummy Cave use the public NPS archaeological feature points, checked against the official visitor map. Overlooks have different coordinates. Buried Structure 67, the Central Plaza and the Mummy Cave tower are described at their parent complex because this study has not surveyed their individual footprints. The 1805 episode uses a broad northern Canyon del Muerto anchor, and the 1864 and 1931 observations use a broad canyon-system anchor. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Read this study with the Four Corners regional guide for wider settlement change, and with Mesa Verde and Chaco for comparisons in architecture and movement. A stylistic resemblance, a dated beam and a claim about migration are related kinds of evidence, but they do not have identical precision. In particular, Tséyi’ does not supply one uniform departure date for all Pueblo communities or one fixed arrival date for all Diné histories. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

Antelope House, Structure 67 — late seventh-century construction

Structure 67 beneath Antelope House was built in the late seventh century; its structural cutting dates cluster in 691–693 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Morris 1986, p.42: 29 charred datable wall/roof beams from Structure 67 have cutting dates clustered AD 691–693.
Dating method: Dendrochronological cutting dates in collapsed structural members, interpreted with excavation stratigraphy.

Structure 67 is not individually surveyed by this proposal; the point is Antelope House. Tests stopped at groundwater before reaching sterile soil, so earlier occupation cannot be excluded. Public NPS archaeological feature point; allow roughly 100 m for the named complex. This is not a surveyed room, wall, painting, tower, or excavated feature footprint. The visitor overlook is a separate point.

Antelope House — later masonry village, approximately 1050–1270

Antelope House developed a later masonry village with south and north room blocks, kivas, storage rooms and successive upper floors, broadly during about 1050–1270 CE.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Morris 1986, pp.45–51: later room-block development and a proposed 1270 abandonment after about 220 years of occupation.
Dating method: Excavation synthesis of stratigraphy, wall bonds, ceramics, tree rings and archaeomagnetism; not a direct date for every room.

The phase boundaries are rounded reconstructions. The record does not count residents, extrapolate an exact abandonment day, or assign every standing wall to 1050. Public NPS archaeological feature point; allow roughly 100 m for the named complex. This is not a surveyed room, wall, painting, tower, or excavated feature footprint. Antelope House — central circular plaza and later infilling

The excavated sequence places Antelope House’s circular Central Plaza after 1200, followed by changed floors and rooms that partly replaced the plaza wall.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Morris 1986 dates the Central Plaza after AD 1200; Kiva C beneath it yielded a 1202 tree-ring date. Main-village end proposed around 1270.
Dating method: Relative construction sequence anchored by dated fill and the excavator’s site chronology. Lower and upper limits are phase brackets, not a direct construction-date confidence interval.

The point is the public Antelope House complex, not a digitized circular-plaza boundary. The report warns that the later dates are less secure than the earlier structural dates. Public NPS archaeological feature point; allow roughly 100 m for the named complex. This is not a surveyed room, wall, painting, tower, or excavated feature footprint. White House — documented building sequence, about 1060–1275

Tree-ring evidence summarized by NPS places White House building activity from around 1060, with additions as late as 1275.

Mummy Cave — tower upper floors built in 1284

Mummy Cave’s tower second and third floors are dated to 1284 in Breternitz’s contextual analysis of the tree-ring collections.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Breternitz 1966, p.42, entry 98a(7): 36 dates span 1251–1284; the second and third floors were built in 1284.
Dating method: Published dendroarchaeological synthesis of dated structural timbers and their tower provenience.

The public point covers Mummy Cave as a whole. No separate surveyed tower coordinate is asserted. Early dates from unrelated contexts do not prove continuous use from the fourth century to 1284. Antelope House — antelope paintings attributed to Dibe Yazhi

NPS records local Navajo attribution of the antelope paintings beside Antelope House to the Diné artist Dibe Yazhi, or Little Sheep.

Canyon del Muerto — Spanish attack and killing at Massacre Cave, 1805

A Spanish expedition commanded by Antonio Narbona attacked Navajo people taking refuge at the place now called Massacre Cave in January 1805.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Bradley 1973, p.30, reproduces part of Narbona’s letter dated 25 January 1805, describing attack on 17 January and taking the refuge the following morning.
Dating method: Documentary event chronology from an attributed English extract of the commander’s report, compared with NPS historical interpretation and a separately mediated Navajo account.

Published accounts differ in framing and details. The original Spanish letter and the underlying oral-history record were not consulted. This proposal maps no grave, firing trajectory, or exact attack footprint. Editorial vicinity anchor rounded to 0.01 degree with about 2 km tolerance. The map locates the public cave north of Mummy Cave; this point is deliberately not a surveyed cave, ledge, grave, firing position, or Massacre Cave Overlook coordinate.

Tséyi’ — U.S. military destruction of homes and subsistence resources, 1864

In 1864 U.S. troops under Kit Carson entered Canyon de Chelly and destroyed homes, orchards and livestock as part of the campaign to force Diné surrender.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: NPS brochure 2013, The Long Walk: winter 1864 entry into the canyon and a spring return that destroyed hogans and orchards and killed sheep.
Dating method: Institutional local campaign chronology, read alongside Smithsonian treatment of the wider U.S. scorched-earth campaign and Diné testimony.

The point represents the canyon system, not one destroyed household or the path of troops. No numerical casualty total or exact number of destroyed trees is inferred. Editorial canyon-system anchor rounded to 0.01 degree; about 15 km geographic scope. It marks the affected landscape, not a military position, household, orchard, official meeting place, boundary, or event footprint.

Tséyi’ — monument designation with Navajo land rights reserved, 1931

The 1931 authorization and monument designation preserved archaeological resources while expressly reserving Navajo interests in the land and agricultural and grazing uses.

Dates and evidence notes
