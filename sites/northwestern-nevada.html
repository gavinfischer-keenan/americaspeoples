Wetland tools at Lovelock

Wetlands around the Humboldt Sink and Carson Desert supplied resources whose distribution shifted as lake levels changed. The crafted duck decoys of Lovelock Cave show how people turned tule into specialized hunting equipment. The survival of such organic materials is unusual, so a cave assemblage gives an especially close view of activities that were more widely distributed than the preserved objects. Source 1 ↗

Encroachment and Paiute leadership

Pyramid Lake is a Northern Paiute homeland. Armed conflict around the lake in 1860 came with growing encroachment. The United States built Fort Churchill elsewhere in the region that year. Near Lovelock, Sarah Winnemucca organized a school for Paiute children. That effort gives a local example of Paiute educational leadership before federal authorities opened the Stewart boarding school south of Carson City. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Two different school histories

Sarah Winnemucca’s school near Lovelock and the Stewart boarding school came from different authorities and approaches to education. A letter published by Elizabeth Peabody records the local initiative in the 1880s. State historic inventories document a federal boarding institution south of Carson City in 1890. The two records have different dates, locations and institutional histories, even where their students came from some of the same regional communities. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Cave deposits and the life of a place

An object’s survival can produce a spectacular but partial record. A duck decoy gives both a date and a specific example of craft knowledge. The location where it was deposited is only one place within a much larger wetland economy. Nearby communities and sheltered sites have their own timelines, while nineteenth-century records involve a different historical source base. Source 1 ↗
