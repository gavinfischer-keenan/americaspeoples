Canadian Shield — camps, waterways and inland communities

Lakes and rivers through the Canadian Shield brought people to places where they could find stone, intercept caribou, fish, gather and trade. Follow individual places from early hearths to the families whose homes and livelihoods were changed by the flooding of Lac Seul.

A closer reading

Reading signs of a seasonal home

A campsite may have been a family’s home for the part of each year when caribou ran or fish gathered at a narrows. When the family packed up, it left what it did not need to carry away. A hearth, an exhausted stone core, a few broken pot fragments and food remains might survive. The Shield’s archaeological record therefore includes small clusters of objects that repay close attention. At Ashuanipi, the relationships between soils, hearths and later activity help show how some of those traces survived.

Evidence: gov.nl.ca [1] ↗ · gov.nl.ca [2] ↗

What people carried and made was a record of their relations with other families. At Whitefish Lake, a pot’s style can be compared with vessels made in other communities. At Fort McKenzie, post journals followed furs and other trade goods, while interviews and archaeology helped recover the calendar of hunting and visiting. Paired evidence opens up parts of local history that a single record of government, trade or excavation would leave faint.

Evidence: ontarioarchaeology.org [1] ↗ · naskapi.ca [2] ↗

Early camps and stone working

At Cummins, a former shore of Lake Minong runs well inland from present Lake Superior. People worked jasper-rich taconite near an outcrop and left stone flakes, pits and hearths. The quarry was close to places where people camped and got food. Farther northwest, a piece of a quartzite projectile point from a terrace above Black Lake resembles Agate Basin points recovered elsewhere. Its find context had been disturbed, so its age comes from comparisons. Together the sites show how stone, shorelines and water helped shape where people stopped and what they did there. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

On the other side of the Shield, people used the outlet of Kamestastin Lake where caribou crossed. At Shak Selma they left worked white quartz and charcoal beside a small fire. At Napeu Atik a bowl-shaped pit held a quartz tool and the debris of stone working. At Mistanuk across the narrows, the selected deposit contains much more Ramah chert. Burning bone and wood made it possible to date these contexts. The materials vary from one site to the next, even at the same lake. The deposits show choices about tools and work that would disappear if all three places were combined into one camp. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Working with copper, wood and fiber

At Renshaw near Thunder Bay, copper tools preserved traces of the materials once fitted to them. A barbed point still held part of its bark binding. Other finds retained grass cordage and wood. Copper corrosion helped preserve these organic materials, which often disappear from archaeological deposits in the Shield. A date measured on the bark places that object’s use in the fourth millennium BCE. Its maker needed both the metalworking skill to shape the point and knowledge of fibers that could hold it onto a shaft. Source 1 ↗

Where families fished, cooked and gathered

At Kay-Nah-Chi-Wah-Nung, the Place of the Long Rapids, spring sturgeon runs brought people together. People lived beside the rapids, and generations of mound builders made this a place of ceremony. Rainy River First Nations relates these earthworks to a living sacred landscape. The river also linked people to communities far beyond the rapids. Farther northwest at Wanipigow Lake, remains of wild rice and goosefoot survive with evidence of pottery-making traditions. The shore became a productive place to return to over many generations. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Pottery adds details about work that can be difficult to recover from camps in the forest. At Whitefish Lake, most of the pottery at Martin-Bird belongs to the Blackduck tradition. A re-excavation investigated food residues in the deposits and recorded other kinds of pottery as well. At Sipiwesk Lake, researchers compared potsherds with samples of sediment and fired experimental tiles. Some clay mixtures had a local character, while others occurred farther away. Households could carry a pot, collect clay from a distant place or make a vessel as they travelled. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Shared dwellings and routes through the interior

Excavations before the Caniapiscau Reservoir filled found small sites with rounded hearths. Other dwellings were six to twelve metres long with extended fires or a row of hearths. The excavators compare the longer homes to the Innu and Naskapi saaputuwaan, an arrangement in which more than one family could share space. The details help explain how people might organize hunting and divide food. At Ferguson Bay on Ashuanipi Lake, archaeologists found precontact hearths at separate levels. The older deposits and historical Innu traces show that families returned to the shoreline through very different periods. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

At Mashteuiatsh on Lac Saint-Jean, Ilnu community members took part in discovering and excavating a site with fires and concentrated fragments of chalcedony. The stone was worked at the site. Two distinct features preserve activity separated by many centuries. At Waapushukamikw, on the Témiscamie River, traces of quarrying show how people obtained and prepared Mistassini quartzite. For the Cree of Mistissini the white hill and its cave also carry spiritual meaning. Camps, material sources and places of gathering belonged to the same travelled country. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Trade posts enter a country of established routes

Real buildings entered this waterway network in stages. A mission and trading post began at Métabetchouane in 1676, where a river route meets Lac Saint-Jean. Montréal traders set up at Obadjiwan in 1720, beside the narrows of Lake Timiskaming. At Norway House, a later depot was rebuilt near the Nelson River in 1826. Houses, warehouses, chimneys and mission buildings tell the stories of those institutions. Their locations also show a practical reliance on canoe routes, crossings and labour sustained by Indigenous people. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗

At Fort Resolution, a North West Company post stood offshore before the Hudson’s Bay Company built on the mainland in 1819. Violet Mandeville’s history records Indigenous guides hired there, including Akaitcho. Farther along the lake, remains of stone fireplaces at Old Fort Reliance reveal a kind of building later adapted by Dene craftspeople. Across the Shield, trade also had its own seasonal rhythm. When families gathered at Fort McKenzie, they brought meat and furs and acquired supplies before leaving again for their own winter camps. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Treaty-making, schooling and forced moves

Land and livelihoods came under new pressures. The Grand Council Treaty #3 preserves the Paypom account of the negotiations that led to the 1873 treaty. At Norway House two years later, the Treaty 5 text set out terms for land, annuities and a reserve for families who wanted to farm near Fisher River. At Tataskweyak in 1908, a later adhesion followed requests from northern Cree representatives for negotiation. These separate meetings make it possible to see that a treaty number can cover several stages of a relationship. At Sakitawak in 1906, officials heard English River and Clear Lake leaders’ requests for help in hard times, in a town also deeply shaped by Métis families and commerce. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗ Source 6 ↗

The Naskapi Nation remembers a series of moves driven by the Hudson’s Bay Company. One took trade inland from Fort Chimo to Fort McKenzie in 1915. In northwestern Ontario, the residential school at Pelican Lake was operating by the late 1920s. The named children on the school memorial give a human scale to that institutional record. When reserve land at Lac Seul began flooding in 1930, families lost homes, gardens, wild rice beds and graves. Some moved to higher ground as what had been a connected shore became an island. The atlas ends in 1934, within a history that remained active for the people who lived it. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗ Source 6 ↗ Source 7 ↗

Cummins — stone working and a camp on Lake Minong

At the edge of former Lake Minong, people worked jasper-rich taconite to make stone tools. Test excavations identified stone-working debris, hearths and pits at the Cummins quarry and camp.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Dawson places the early camp and quarry around 7000 BC; Parks Canada describes the site as at least 9,000 years old.
Dating method: Artifact chronology, relict shoreline context and preliminary field estimates. The reported cremation age in the 1983 article is expressed as a conventional radiocarbon estimate in BC notation.

The early camp has a relative archaeological chronology. The approximation and minimum age cannot supply a calibrated calendar window or a date of first arrival.
