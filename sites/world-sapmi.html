Sápmi across borders, from early landscapes to 1900

A map of modern frontiers divides Sápmi among Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia. Sámi places and relationships follow river valleys, coasts, inland waters, grazing grounds and routes that long preceded those frontiers. They also follow particular histories. Varanger’s coastal buildings, the Skolt Sámi archive from Suõʹnnʼjel and the market at Jåhkåmåhkke show different ways people organized work and preserved knowledge.

The modern map spans the wider cross-border Sámi homeland and selected evidence through 1900. It gives geographic orientation without drawing a historic ethnic or political boundary. Early archaeological traces in the same landscape have a separate status from later Sámi records.

A chronology shaped by the survival of evidence

The sequence starts with archaeological evidence in landscapes later used by Sámi communities. Ages of artifacts or dwelling floors do not by themselves date an ethnic identity. During later periods, places, households and rights can be studied in several kinds of record at once. Sámi museums, archives and parliaments give community and institutional context for interpreting those records. [1] [3] [4] [9]

Livelihoods across coasts, waters and mountains

At Ceavccageađge / Mortensnes, raised prehistoric beaches help place early activity in a shifting fjord landscape. Later graves, settlement traces and traditional sites record different layers within the same area. A museum visit can follow the physical terrain and explain the contrast between evidence visible against modern coastlines and evidence dated through excavation. The nearby nineteenth-century Sámi boathouse at Høybukt makes coastal work visible at a much later date through its surviving timbers. [1] [2] [19]

In the uplands, archaeological comparisons connect dwelling remains and reindeer use across long distances. Researchers distinguish different constructions and forms of mobility. Hunting, fishing, seasonal movement and different types of herding could overlap. A dwelling phase with clear dates need not represent the same livelihood for every resident. These differences also matter when reading much later state attempts to classify Sámi people according to a single recognized occupation. [3] [22]

Rights in documents, trade and the reach of states

The archive from Suõʹnnʼjel contains original edicts dating from 1601 to 1775. The community’s actions in maintaining and protecting the archive made written evidence usable over generations. It gives unusually strong testimony on fishing and herding rights and shows why historical sources created in one administrative language can still bear evidence of Indigenous strategies. Recent custodianship by the Sámi Archives continues that history in a new institutional setting. [4]

At Jåhkåmåhkke / Jokkmokk the winter market brought together trade, colonial administration and social gatherings. The 1671 tax-land survey gives evidence of recognized uses and Crown claims while recording the government’s own categories. Nasafjäll’s silver mine imposed transport burdens on Sámi workers and herders. Ore, labor and rights belonged to the same landscapes, and each historical source emphasizes a different part of that relationship. [5] [6] [21] [8] [9]

The 1751 treaty and its codicil defined a border and recognized specified Sámi customary practices. In the east, the Norway–Russia settlement of 1826 addressed long-overlapping jurisdiction and uses. The nineteenth-century Pomor network connected Vardø with Russian White Sea merchants, while the Norway–Finland closure of 1852 constrained the movement of herders. These changes affected different institutions, communities and routes. A dated state boundary cannot stand for the entire history of mobility or access. [10] [11] [12] [17]

Schooling, religion and nineteenth-century pressures

The Skyttean school in Lycksele taught Sámi boys in an ecclesiastical setting. Separate nineteenth-century policies in Norway funded Norwegian-language teaching and later narrowed Sámi and Kven use in schools. The 1898 instruction is a dated intervention, and family knowledge and speech persisted in settings outside the classroom. Within Sweden, the grazing law of 1886 reshaped legal rights through collective categories and affected households differently depending on their livelihoods. [7] [15] [16] [17] [22]

The Laestadian movement grew across borders through preaching and relationships in the region. The events at Guovdageaidnu / Kautokeino in 1852 occurred amid religious conflict and legal and economic pressure, and the surviving official records need careful reading alongside Sámi accounts. At the eastern end of the map, the renovation of a Sámi Orthodox chapel in the Lovozero region in 1895 documents a distinct local religious history. These are community histories in their own right as well as histories of outside institutions. [14] [18] [17] [20]
