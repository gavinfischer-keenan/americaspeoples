Four Corners: connected histories and continuing homelands

The Four Corners region contains many overlapping histories. Follow changes between communities, then open a local study to examine the buildings and evidence behind a chapter. The narrative begins with dated early farming settlements; it does not claim to date the region’s first inhabitants.

Communities before and beyond Chaco

Early homes at Mesa Verde and Shabik’eshchee place settled communities in the region well before Chaco’s great-house peak. Later builders worked within landscapes already occupied and remembered. Changes in buildings and pottery help order that history, but they cannot draw fixed borders around every people or language.

Evidence: npshistory.com [1] ↗ · chacoarchive.org [2] ↗

Salmon and Aztec connected the canyon with the San Juan and Animas valleys while developing different histories of construction and residence. Lowry’s communal spaces and Escalante’s intermittent reuse add further variations. The regional picture becomes richer when these places remain distinct, showing how communities maintained, altered or returned to older architecture as circumstances changed.

Migration: a connection that can be mapped without inventing an itinerary

The shared movement model connects the central Mesa Verde and Northern Rio Grande research regions during the thirteenth century. Its basis is a domestic-animal proxy: later Rio Grande turkey mitochondrial patterns are consistent with birds moving from the Mesa Verde population alongside people. It is explicitly a model, not individual human DNA evidence, a complete census or proof of language replacement. Source 1 ↗

The proposed connection links the central Mesa Verde and Northern Rio Grande research regions. It concerns movement between broad areas rather than a known road or a particular household’s itinerary. The evidence does not identify every village of origin or destination. Source 1 ↗

Later Pueblo communities and distinct identities

Pueblo communities have connections to multiple ancestral places and groups. Migration occurred into already inhabited landscapes, and later communities combined continuity with change. Hopi, Zuni, Acoma and Rio Grande histories cannot be compressed into one northern ancestry or one modern political identity. NPS affiliation summaries acknowledge that linking each archaeological branch to a single present community is difficult. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

At Wiyo’owingeh, comparison with a Chaco-era community suggests continuing landscape and cosmological relationships alongside different social and architectural arrangements. This bounded comparison helps explain transformation without making modern pueblos unchanged replicas of Chaco. Source 1 ↗

Diné and Ute histories are part of the region, with their own accounts

Diné life in Canyon de Chelly continues a distinct history of homeland, farming, family and place. Diné emergence accounts and evidence about Athabaskan language relationships answer different questions. Ute tribal histories likewise place their people within mountain homelands and describe seasonal movement, trade, conflict and dispossession. Neither people is treated as an automatic successor produced by a Pueblo archaeological phase. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

In 1895, most Weeminuche moved west under Ignacio in opposition to allotment. The tribal account distinguishes their response from Mouache and Caputa allotments. The broad regional marker preserves that difference without assigning an exact founding year to modern Towaoc. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Colonial coercion, resistance and return

Spanish establishment of Santa Fe brought forced labor and suppression of Pueblo religious practices into the region’s seventeenth-century history. The 1680 revolt challenged that rule. An edited colonial documentary record and the All Pueblo Council of Governors’ account describe the conflict from different perspectives. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

The Diné Long Walk, Bosque Redondo confinement and the 1868 return are separate events. The return treaty restored access to a defined reservation within the homeland while imposing further federal obligations. Its provisions do not show that every promise was fulfilled or that all earlier territory was restored. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
