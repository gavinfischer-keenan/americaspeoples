Wales from the first people to 1900

Cave burials, copper workings and forts form part of the record of Wales, alongside books, contested marches and industrial landscapes. Some connections ran along the Welsh coast, some crossed to Ireland, and others followed river valleys into the borderlands. The map connects selected places across those different settings.

Places in modern Wales and the Welsh borderlands, from the Middle Pleistocene to the onset of the Penrhyn dispute in 1900. Early regional labels follow the relevant archaeological or documentary evidence.

Caves and journeys

The teeth and stone artefacts from Pontnewydd predate the deeply coloured burial at Paviland by a vast interval. Gower’s cliffs and the Great Orme’s caves now frame modern coasts. At different points in the Ice Age, people approached these places through very different environments. Dates from the caves describe separate episodes and surviving people. [1] [2] [3]

Farming communities and the work of metals

The mound at Bryn Celli Ddu reused the site of an older enclosure. The Great Orme mine shows miners following and working ore veins in a different part of the landscape. At Castell Henllys the postholes of houses anchor reconstructions of community life. Some of the most elaborate regional Iron Age objects, including parts of vehicles and weapons, survived near a former Anglesey lake. [4] [5] [6] [7]

Forts, borderlands and Welsh polities

Caerleon and Segontium preserve different scales of Rome’s military presence. Later, an earthwork associated with Mercian king Offa crossed parts of the borderlands. Near Llantysilio the rulers of Powys used a carved Latin inscription on Eliseg’s Pillar to recall dynastic ties. The earthwork and the inscription tell different stories about power across a shifting frontier. [8] [9] [10] [11]

Conquest, diplomacy and Welsh in print

Medieval church networks centred on places such as St Davids. Edward I’s castle and walled town at Caernarfon changed access to land and commerce after conquest. Glyndŵr later used diplomatic correspondence to pursue a different status for Wales and its church. Tudor legislation reorganised secular administration, while William Morgan’s 1588 Bible supported widespread use of written Welsh in worship. [12] [13] [14] [23] [15]

Iron, slate and protest

Furnaces, coalfields, canals and tramroads connected south Wales to markets far from the valleys. The Merthyr and Newport risings and rural toll protests arose from different pressures and forms of organisation. In the northwest, quarry workers’ dispute at Penrhyn began in 1900. Working sites, settlements and transport routes explain how industrial output depended on everyday lives. [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22]
