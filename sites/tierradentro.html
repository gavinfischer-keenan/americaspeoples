A closer reading

Homes, ancestors and underground architecture

Tierradentro’s underground chambers differ in size, supports and decoration. Segovia includes carved pillars and painted interiors; other groups preserve smaller spaces or distinctive roof forms. These choices make each chamber group worth comparing on its own terms. Their variety does not establish that each group belonged to a separate people.

Evidence: publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [1] ↗ · publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [2] ↗ · redalyc.org [3] ↗

Some chambers received remains after an earlier burial elsewhere. The history of a person’s life, the first burial and later placement in a hypogeum can therefore involve separate moments. Carved and painted interiors gave these subterranean places architectural form. They preserve substantial evidence of care and construction even though a complete account of the ceremonies is unavailable.

Evidence: publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [1] ↗ · publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [2] ↗ · redalyc.org [3] ↗

Tierradentro’s monumental chambers were cut into volcanic rock below ground. Descending stairs led to spaces with columns, niches and painted surfaces. Their forms repeated aspects of houses, creating a built environment for ancestors beneath places also used by living communities. Red, black and white decoration transformed the carved rock. The labor combined excavation, architectural planning and painting rather than simply digging a pit.

Evidence: publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [1] ↗

These chambers participated in a sequence of funerary practices. The ICANH guide describes secondary collective burial, in which remains were transferred after an earlier burial and placed in pits or ceramic urns within the chamber. Some urns occupied niches in the walls. This use made the tomb a place revisited by a group over time, linking the living with ancestors through repeated acts of care and ceremony.

Evidence: publicaciones.icanh.gov.co [1] ↗

The public park preserves several concentrations of hypogea and the stone sculptures at El Tablón. Their distribution along hills and ridges creates a broader ceremonial landscape. The chambers’ columns, stairways and patterned walls reveal local solutions to monumental construction in this volcanic terrain. Comparing these underground spaces with nearby regions shows how communities could express social relationships through very different forms of architecture and sculpture.

Evidence: whc.unesco.org [1] ↗

Named groups and architectural differences

The public archaeological groups preserve different choices in chamber size, roof treatment, supports and decoration. Segovia includes elaborate carved pillars and painted interiors; El Duende D4 has a rectangular room and distinctive roof form; El Aguacate generally has smaller chambers without central columns. These differences deserve comparison without automatically turning them into separate peoples or a precise developmental sequence. Source 1 ↗

Burial practices and the houses of the living

Hypogea could receive human remains after initial burial elsewhere, so a bone’s biological age and its later deposition are different events. Carved architectural forms and painted interiors made the chambers more than storage spaces, but the sources do not preserve a complete account of ceremonies or the identities of participants. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Regional survey places the monuments among changing settlements. Early 3 concentrations at Segovia, Middle-period occupation around El Tablón and later residence near San Andrés prevent the funerary landscape from being treated as isolated from daily life. The domestic layer at El Aguacate is especially useful as a warning against assuming that all tomb groups stood apart from habitation. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Two chronological systems and a translation error

The regional study uses Early 3 at 300 BCE–100 CE, Middle at 100–1300 CE and Late at 1300–1650 CE. These local ceramic phases differ from the generalized Alto Magdalena sequence. Their boundaries should not be exchanged merely because period names appear similar. Source 1 ↗

The English park guide prints 600–900 BC for the hypogea where the Spanish version reads 600–900 d.C. The CE reading is used here, with the uncertainty emphasized in the sources. The regional study also reports contamination problems with extremely early apparent bone dates. Neither the mistranslation nor contaminated samples justify a new early tomb-building episode. Living territory and unresolved affiliations

The archaeological park lies within the Nasa resguardo of San Andrés. That present relationship is essential context for understanding the place, while the checked archaeological sources do not establish a single language or an unbroken named identity for every ancient monument builder. Historical accounts themselves describe several peoples and uncertain territorial limits. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Tierradentro and San Agustín share useful points of comparison in statuary, mortuary architecture and the relationship between monuments and settlement. Their local sequences should nevertheless remain separate. More secure dating, contextual research and Indigenous knowledge can refine those relationships without requiring the current gaps to be filled by an invented migration or common political state. El Tablón — Middle-period settlement context beside statuary

El Tablón’s statue concentration coincides with settlement evidence assigned to the local Middle period, as well as earlier Early 3 material. Figures with pectorals are particularly associated with El Tablón.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Local Middle period, 100–1300 CE.
Dating method: Regional survey ceramic chronology and spatial association of settlement and statuary.

The wider Middle interval differs from the guide’s general Regional Classic 1–900 CE. Spatial association cannot independently date each sculpture.

Segovia — painted and carved hypogea

Segovia preserves rock-cut funerary chambers with pillars, niches and painted geometric decoration; S1, S4 and S9 provide named architectural examples. Their conventional 600–900 CE attribution remains provisional.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Provisional conventional hypogeum attribution, approximately 600–900 CE; Spanish ICANH guide.
Dating method: Architectural/ceramic tradition attribution; no direct construction calibration for the named chamber.

English guide misprints BC where Spanish reads d.C.; CE adopted. Contaminated early bone ages excluded. Individual biological samples do not date the excavation of a rock-cut chamber.

El Duende — varied rock-cut chamber architecture

El Duende includes rock-cut chambers such as D4, whose rectangular room, roof form and geometric painting show variation within the Tierradentro hypogeum tradition.

El Aguacate — ridge-top hypogea and distinctive painting

El Aguacate’s relatively small ridge-top chambers generally lack central columns. Chamber A0 preserves motifs described as moons and salamanders, contrasting with more geometric decoration at other groups.

San Andrés valley — Late-period residential evidence

Settlements in the Quebrada de San Andrés continued during the local Late period while concentrations also grew around Inzá and El Hato. The park guide records Recent residential evidence near Alto de San Andrés.
