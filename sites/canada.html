Making a living on the Pacific coast

Footprints at Calvert Island preserve a brief moment on an early shoreline. Much later, stone-walled clam gardens enlarged productive beaches, while large houses anchored communities on the coast and in the Fraser interior. These histories make the coast a place of engineering, skilled work and political life long before European trading ships. At Yuquot, visitors entered a Mowachaht centre with its own authority and relationships. Forts, the fur trade and colonial government changed those relationships; the execution of Tŝilhqot’in leaders and the suppression of potlatches show the force behind that change. Prairie histories beyond one way of life

The Prairie record includes ancient horse and camel hunting, places where people processed animal bodies, and later villages where maize was grown and stored. It is a history of different ways of using land, not a single timeless hunting society. Pemmican connected food production to continental commerce and Métis political life. Batoche began as a ferry and trading settlement before it became a battlefield in 1885. Treaty meetings and residential schools belong beside these places on the map: colonial expansion altered access to land, family life and the terms on which people could live together. Northern tools and established trading worlds

A composite dart from southern Yukon reveals a carefully assembled tool made from wood, stone and binding materials. Centuries of northern history cannot be reduced to outsiders’ arrivals. Gwich’in, Inuvialuit, Tlingit and Tutchone relationships shaped the trading worlds that company posts entered. At Fort Selkirk, Chilkat traders challenged a company’s intrusion in 1852. The Klondike rush later brought another scale of pressure to Tr’ondëk Hwëch’in country. Moosehide’s history records a community’s move in that setting, while northern school and treaty histories expose the reach of colonial institutions. Homes, sea mammals and authority in the Arctic

Arctic archaeology includes hunting tools, carefully built dwellings and settlements that differ across islands and centuries. The Rivendell evidence beside Lake Hazen and the houses at Qaummaarviit belong to local sequences; they do not describe one synchronized change across the entire north. At Qaummaarviit, house construction made warmth and access to hunting grounds part of everyday design. Later whaling stations relied on Inuit knowledge and labour. Missions, trading posts, courts and police brought new demands into places already inhabited. The regional guide follows those encounters through the early twentieth century without treating a new post as the beginning of local history. Waterways join villages, work and diplomacy

Obsidian from a distant western source reached the Lake Huron region, and wooden fish fences survived beneath the Narrows at Mnjikaning. These findings reveal movement and collective work at very different times. Later longhouse villages brought households, food production and political relationships together. Revised dating at the Jean-Baptiste Lainé village changes where that community falls within contact-era history. Conflict in seventeenth-century Wendat country caused deaths and displacement, but community history continued elsewhere. Canoe commerce, the War of 1812, treaties and residential schools then changed the Great Lakes world in different, connected ways. A river already full of communities

McDonald, Droulers and Mailhot-Curran preserve longhouses, food remains and everyday tools from St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities. Cartier’s visits entered a river world supplied and governed by its residents. Later French establishments depended on diplomacy as well as building. Wendake records community rebuilding; the Great Peace of Montréal records negotiations involving many nations. War reached both colonial towns and Indigenous homes, including Odanak in 1759. The nineteenth-century creation of reserves at Kitigan Zibi and Mashteuiatsh placed new administrative boundaries across older homelands rather than creating those peoples’ relationships with the land. Many Atlantic histories at once

Debert’s stone tools, the coastal settlement at Phillip’s Garden and Norse woodworking at L’Anse aux Meadows belong to widely separated chapters. In the sixteenth century, Basque crews at Red Bay turned whales into an Atlantic commodity. Later fishing settlements, Mi’kmaq and Beothuk communities, Acadian farms and colonial forts coexisted around the region. British conquest brought forced departures from Grand-Pré and Île Saint-Jean. Nain’s mission history sits within Inuit Labrador, not outside it. The opening of Shubenacadie’s residential school in 1930 connects this regional history to the wider system that separated Indigenous children from families and communities. Camps, gathering places and inland routes

Across the Shield, harvests brought families back to particular places. At Kamestastin in Labrador, quartz and charcoal mark ancient work beside a caribou crossing. At Kay-Nah-Chi-Wah-Nung on the Rainy River, sturgeon drew seasonal gatherings, and generations of people lived and raised mounds near the rapids. In later centuries, inland trade dealers, governments and missions used waterways already familiar to Indigenous families. Lac Seul shows what was at stake when a dam flooded homes, gardens, harvesting places and graves in the 1930s. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗ Source 4 ↗ Source 5 ↗ Source 6 ↗

Places, finds and events on the map

L’Anse aux Meadows — Norse woodworking in 1021 CE

Kuitems and colleagues identified the 993 CE radiocarbon signal in three different trees and counted annual rings to their outer edges. Agreement among the resulting cutting dates anchors Norse activity to 1021.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: AD 1021
Dating method: Annual tree rings matched to the 993 CE radiocarbon anomaly

This precise date does not date the settlement's founding or abandonment. It also does not show that every Norse activity occurred in that year. No arrival route is inferred from this record.

Shak Selma — quartz tools beside a fire

At Shak Selma, on the southern side of the Kamestastin outflow, people left worked white quartz, grey chert and charcoal in a pit beside a small fire feature.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Two AMS charcoal measurements each 6380 ± 30 radiocarbon BP (Beta-522800 and Beta-522801). Reported components 7338–7259 cal BP (67.8%) and 7417–7352 cal BP (27.6%).
Dating method: Jenkinson’s field reports identify two wood-charcoal samples, one from the quartz pit and one from the adjacent combustion feature. Published calibrated BP converted using the 1950 CE reference.

The map displays the larger probability component, approximately 5389–5310 BCE. The separate older component remains in the source and date note. The ranges concern an episode of activity.

Kay-Nah-Chi-Wah-Nung — sturgeon, gathering and Laurel mounds

Around the Long Rapids, sturgeon fishing brought people together. Traces of homes and earth mounds record work, ceremony and a place visited by many communities over time.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Laurel components, 300 BCE–1100 CE in the national historic designation.
Dating method: Heritage register’s calendar attribution to the Laurel tradition at the site. Earlier activity is documented separately.

An archaeological phase window for repeated activity. It does not date every village trace or every mound.

Wanipigow Lake — pots, wild rice and other plant foods

Excavation at Wanipigow Lake recovered abundant pottery in several styles. Plant remains there also document intensive use of wild rice and goosefoot.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Late pottery styles dated regionally to approximately 700–1640 CE in the provincial designation.
Dating method: Comparative attribution of Blackduck, Selkirk and Sandy Lake pottery; calendar period given by the provincial designation.

A broad range for the ceramic traditions represented at the lake. The plant remains and pottery need not all come from the same occupation.

Kejick Bay — flooding forces families to move at Lac Seul

Water held back by the Ear Falls dam flooded homes, gardens, wild rice beds and graves at Lac Seul. Kejick Bay became an island, and families had to move to higher ground.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Dam completed in 1929. Lac Seul First Nation and Canada give 1930 as the start of flooding of reserve lands. Water levels had risen by 1934.
Dating method: Court findings and filings read alongside the Nation’s account and joint notice.

The visible period ends at the atlas cutoff. Rising water and the effects on families extended beyond it.

Mnjikaning — early wooden fish-weir evidence

Surviving wooden stakes beneath the Narrows between Lakes Simcoe and Couchiching preserve a fishing system whose earliest dated evidence is placed around 3300 BCE. Later Huron-Wendat use and Anishinaabeg stewardship belong to its long history.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: About 3300 BCE, official early-use estimate
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

A rounded early attestation, not a founding year or proof that every stake remained in continuous use. Cultural affiliation of the earliest stake-makers is unknown.

Jean-Baptiste Lainé / Mantle — a later Wendat village chronology

Radiocarbon modeling places the Jean-Baptiste Lainé/Mantle site in the late sixteenth to early seventeenth century. Excavated longhouses surrounded a central plaza; palisades, a ditch and an embankment formed defensive works.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1596–1618 CE, 68.2% highest-posterior-density modeled estimate
Dating method: Published Bayesian radiocarbon model, independent Mantle phase, 68.2% highest-posterior-density range; no recalibration here.

1596–1618 is a modeled probability range for the site’s date, not start and abandonment boundaries or a 22-year lifespan. Older circa 1500–1550 estimates are not adopted.

Tsiionhiakwatha / Droulers — longhouses and worked bone

A primary bone-artifact study places Droulers around 1490–1515 CE and describes seven excavated longhouses, two middens, and a large worked-bone assemblage. It proposes aggregation of smaller communities as one explanation for the village’s size.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 1490–1515 CE, 2021 study; visitor centre uses circa 1450
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The visitor centre gives circa 1450; the newer research estimate is displayed explicitly rather than blended into an invented range. Seven excavated houses do not prove all proposed fifteen houses.

Wendake — establishment of the enduring community

The Huron-Wendat community established itself at the present Wendake locality in 1697. Its own public history presents this settlement as part of an ongoing civilization and community, not a new people created by colonial settlement.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1697, community establishment at Wendake
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1697 dates establishment at this locality, not the beginning of Huron-Wendat history or the arrival of every family. No single route from Huronia is reconstructed.

Montréal — Great Peace diplomacy

Indigenous delegations and French authorities concluded the Great Peace at Montréal on 4 August 1701. Wendat leader Kondiaronk’s diplomacy was central to negotiations involving nations from the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence worlds.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 4 August 1701
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The gathering does not establish permanent peace everywhere or identical interests among all participants. The city reference is not a reconstructed council-site footprint.

Fort Beauséjour — siege and surrender

British forces under Robert Monckton captured Fort Beauséjour on 16 June 1755 after a siege. Renamed Fort Cumberland, it became an instrument of British control over the Chignecto corridor.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 16 June 1755, capitulation
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

This occurrence dates capitulation, not all construction or fighting. Aulac supplies coarse locality context and does not reconstruct troop positions.

Grand-Pré — confinement and deportation order

At Grand-Pré on 5 September 1755, assembled Acadian men and boys were confined and told they would be deported. Orders to military officers show the organized removal of inhabitants from the Minas district.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 5 September 1755, order read to assembled men and boys
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The date is the confinement and announcement, not a single departure day for all families. The modern village anchor is not the exact church interior or embarkation point.

Skmaqn / Port-la-Joye — Acadian deportation from Epekwitk

After the British conquest of Île Saint-Jean in 1758, officials organized the forcible removal of more than 3,000 French and Acadian inhabitants from Port-la-Joye. Shipwrecks and illness made the crossing deadly.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1758, British occupation and deportation organization
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

The marker dates the deportation operation, not every ship’s departure. Epekwitk’s Mi’kmaq community history is distinct from the Acadian removal and did not end with British conquest.

Shubenacadie — residential school opens to children

Shubenacadie residential school opened to students in 1930. Many Mi’kmaw and Wolastoqkew children were compelled to attend a federally funded, church-run institution aimed at assimilation.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1930, school opens to students following construction in 1928–1929
Dating method: Published calendar chronology retained. BCE is represented by astronomical year 1 minus BCE. No uncalibrated radiocarbon BP value is converted.

1930 dates opening to students; establishment and building work occurred earlier. The school continued beyond this atlas’s 1934 endpoint, so the marker must not imply closure in 1934.

Wally’s Beach — horse and camel butchering

Cut-marked horse and camel bones and associated artifacts at Wally’s Beach support hunting and butchering near an ancient river crossing. Revised radiocarbon work places these activities about 13,300 cal BP.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Approximately 13,300 cal BP
Dating method: 1950−13300=−11350; rounded published calibrated chronology, not new calibration.

Rounded calibrated-age anchor. Several carcasses need not represent one hunt; the evidence does not determine a migration route or prove that hunting alone caused megafaunal extinction.

Frog Plain / Seven Oaks — Métis victory in 1816

Cuthbert Grant’s Métis party and Governor Robert Semple’s party clashed at Frog Plain during disputes over pemmican and the fur trade. Métis accounts remember the victory as an assertion of collective rights.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 19 June 1816
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

The account is explicitly grounded in Métis historical interpretation. It is not a claim that Métis identity suddenly originated on a single day.

Batoche — battle during the North-West Resistance

Canadian forces defeated the Métis defending Batoche after four days of fighting in May 1885. The battle was followed by prosecutions, but did not end the Métis community’s presence at Batoche.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 9–12 May 1885
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

Battle window only; the community was not destroyed or extinguished in 1885. Later economic decline and relocation have their own chronologies.

Fort Selkirk — Chilkat Tlingit expel HBC traders

Chilkat Tlingit traders attacked Fort Selkirk and expelled the Hudson’s Bay Company in 1852. The post, founded in 1848, had challenged established coastal–interior trading relationships.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1852
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

Year retained because accounts differ on exact day and relocation chronology. The event did not empty the Northern Tutchone homeland or end Indigenous trading.

Lake Hazen — Rivendell occupation around 1000 BCE

Parks Canada describes Rivendell near Lake Hazen as an occupation around 1000 BCE associated with a transition between Independence I and Independence II archaeological assemblages.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: About 1050–950 BC; order normalized from 950–1050 BC
Dating method: Astronomical year=1−BCE; source calendar endpoints retained.

Site-specific approximate attestation, not a population census or evidence of uninterrupted settlement across northern Ellesmere. Changing assemblages do not demonstrate genocide or cultural replacement by violence.

Dundas Harbour — RCMP detachment opens

An RCMP detachment opened at Dundas Harbour on Devon Island in 1924. Police posts combined law enforcement with patrols and other administrative services in the eastern Arctic.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1924; first operation span 1924–1933
Dating method: Documentary year or published archaeological calendar chronology retained.

This marks the first detachment opening, not the beginning of human use of Devon Island. Later reopening and relocation histories are not silently included.

Bluefish Cave II — dated mandible and cut-mark dispute

Microscopic marks on an approximately 24,000-year-old horse mandible were interpreted as human tongue-removal cuts. If anthropogenic and contemporaneous, they imply a Last Glacial Maximum human presence in eastern Beringia.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: Horse mandible J7.8.17, OxA-33778: 19,650 ± 130 radiocarbon BP; calibrated 24,033–23,314 cal BP (95.4%), Figure 3.
Dating method: AMS radiocarbon on bone collagen; OxCal 4.2 and IntCal13 calibration.

The radiocarbon age and the agent producing marks are separate questions. Cave deposits contain natural and carnivore modifications; a securely dated bone is not by itself evidence of human occupation.

Debert — calibrated chronology and archaeological context

Lothrop and colleagues report a pooled Debert age of 12,630 ± 135 cal BP at 2σ, distinct from the uncalibrated 10,600 ± 47 radiocarbon BP result. The early excavation account describes disturbed deposits and no recovered faunal bones; proposed caribou hunting therefore exceeds direct faunal evidence at Debert.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 12,630 ± 135 cal BP, published 2σ summary; raw pooled age 10,600 ± 47 radiocarbon BP
Dating method: Lothrop et al. Table 3: IntCal13 / OxCal 4.2 published summary; no independent recalibration

A pooled radiocarbon summary does not establish uninterrupted residence. Initial excavation interpretations and later calibrated summaries have different evidentiary roles.

Calvert Island — human footprints in coastal sediment

McLaren and colleagues report 29 human footprints representing at least three people, including a juvenile, on Calvert Island. The conservative published envelope is 13,317–12,633 cal BP; narrower rounded wording elsewhere in the paper should not replace that full envelope.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 13,317–12,633 cal BP, conservative associated-wood chronology
Dating method: Associated pine wood; Calib 7.0.2 / IntCal13 reported by McLaren et al.

This is Pacific coastal presence. It is not evidence for the route into Atlantic Canada, uninterrupted coastal movement, or a village population.

Northern Quadra Island — early clam-garden construction

Rock walls and terraces engineered productive clam habitat. Radiocarbon and sea-level evidence places some garden construction at least about 3500 years ago.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: At least approximately 3500 years ago
Dating method: Approximate 3500-year age from calibrated radiocarbon and sea-level interpretation; 1950 BP base used for map placement.

This approximate earliest reference is not the date of every wall. The much longer history of human–clam relations must not be substituted for construction age.

Louisbourg — siege capitulation

The French garrison surrendered to British forces after a siege. This pin dates the capitulation, not the beginning of all fighting.

Dates and evidence notes

Source dates: 1758-07-26
Dating method: Source-specific archaeological chronology or documentary event chronology; see cited passage.

Locality-level encounter. Coordinates locate the named public battlefield area approximately; they do not trace units, firing positions or a damage boundary. The marker remains as context within its event year after the onset date, not as a claim of continuous fighting.
