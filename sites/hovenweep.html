Hovenweep

Hovenweep is a collection of separated ancestral places. Its canyon-head buildings connect architecture to springs, farming, and ceremonial life, while Goodman Point belongs to a different setting in the Montezuma Valley.

Six monument units, with Horseshoe and Hackberry distinguished

NPS counts six distinct monument units: Square Tower and Cajon in Utah; Holly, Cutthroat Castle, the combined Horseshoe/Hackberry boundary, and Goodman Point in Colorado. Horseshoe and Hackberry are also described as separate groups within their shared boundary. The table distinguishes those two places, producing seven named entries without implying seven separate administrative units. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

The towers were made by ancestral Pueblo people. Their forms and placements vary, and their purposes should not be reduced to a single explanation. NPS presents ceremonial, calendrical, protective, storage, and residential possibilities; some descendant-community consultants emphasize rain prayer and relationships among springs, mountains, and water. These attributed interpretations should remain distinct from measured architecture. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗

Square Tower GroupMost visible structures were built and used around 1200–1300 CE.Room blocks, towers, and complexes occupy the rims, boulders, slopes, and ledges of Little Ruin Canyon. NPS reports remains of nearly thirty kivas. The named Square Tower stands on a boulder near the canyon head.Pueblo, Zuni, and Hopi descendants regard this as a continuing sacred place. Its name does not mean that cultural relationships ended when permanent residence shifted elsewhere.

Source 1 ↗

CajonStructures began in Pueblo I; greatest use about 900–1300 CE.A compact concentration of buildings at the southern edge of Cajon Mesa. Its towers and western building interact with seasonal sunlight and shadow in the interpretation presented by NPS.Rain-prayer and timekeeping interpretations are attributed to tribal consultants, not established as the sole function of every building. The unit is reached through Navajo Nation land; use the designated public approach and parking.

Source 1 ↗

HollyPrimary use about 900–1300 CE.At the north fork of Keeley Canyon: five single-level masonry structures, three towers, possible kiva depressions, a dam, and a retaining wall. Holly Tower has two rooms and two stories on a large boulder.The trail-visible petroglyph panel receives light beams around the summer solstice. NPS presents seasonal timekeeping and rain ceremony as interpretations informed by descendant consultants.

Source 1 ↗

Cutthroat CastleBuildings were used across Pueblo I–III, about 750–1300 CE.Unlike the rim groups, its buildings lie within and along a drainage. The main three-story, D-shaped building sits on a boulder; nearby are towers, dwellings, and possible kiva depressions.NPS identifies a probable above-ground kiva with an outer corridor. Neither its circular shape nor the modern name 'castle' proves a single exclusive function.

Source 1 ↗

HorseshoeEvidence of occupation from around 450 CE; prominent stone construction belongs to the later ancestral Pueblo landscape, around and after 1200.Horseshoe House is a two-story, double-walled D-shaped building with four rooms around a circular room. A separate tower and room block occupy the point between drainage forks.The central room is described as kiva-like. Horseshoe shares a protected boundary and trail with Hackberry; they should remain distinguishable as sites.

Source 1 ↗

HackberryEarly use of the springs from around 450 CE; major masonry construction around 1200 and later.The largest complex in the monument retains traces of towers, rooms, terrace walls, and check dams near its spring, though comparatively little masonry stands upright.Low surviving walls do not imply a minor settlement. It remains sacred to descendant communities.

Source 1 ↗

Goodman PointMajor thirteenth-century village; the cited NPS overview does not provide an exact construction sequence.A large, unexcavated pueblo in the Montezuma Valley, rather than a Cajon Mesa canyon-head tower group. NPS describes public architecture including great kivas, plazas, reservoirs, and enclosing walls in these village remains.Buried architecture should not be reconstructed as fully known. Goodman Point is geographically and architecturally distinct from the five Cajon Mesa units.

Water and living connections

Hovenweep's prominent canyon-head groups are associated with springs and seeps. A spring is a strong reason to locate buildings nearby, but proximity alone does not settle what each tower did. NPS explicitly leaves the purpose of Square Tower open and notes that its adjacent excavated kiva was not connected to it by a tunnel. Source 1 ↗

NPS asks visitors to remain on defined trails, avoid touching or entering structures, and leave artifacts in place. Outlying units require additional travel and walking; rough roads can become impassable when wet. Route conditions and permitted access should be checked with the monument before visiting.
