The Bantu Expansion Across Sub-Saharan Africa

Beginning along the Nigeria–Cameroon borderland around 4,000–3,000 years ago, Bantu-speaking communities carried pottery, yams, pearl millet, and later iron metallurgy across tropical rainforest rivers and savanna corridors to the Cape of Good Hope.

Synthesizes dated radiocarbon ceramic horizons, rainforest paleoecology, lexical phylogeography, and sub-Saharan ancient genomes.

Through the forest or around the margin?

Bayesian linguistic phylogeography and Sangha-Congo levee excavations increasingly favor an early crossing through naturally fractured seasonal forest corridors opened by mid-to-late Holocene drying after ~2,500–2,000 BP, rather than an exclusively northern savanna detour. [4] [5]

New summed radiocarbon syntheses reveal a marked Central African rainforest settlement contraction around 400–600 CE before later Late Iron Age town resurgence. [1]

Encounter, admixture, and mosaic innovation

Ancient and modern genomes confirm that dispersing Bantu groups did not march across an empty landscape: deep Central African hunter-gatherers (as seen directly in Shum Laka genomes), Cushitic-Nilotic eastern herders, and Southern African Khoekhoe–San groups repeatedly exchanged genes, cattle terms, and click consonants. [2] [3]

Cattle, iron furnaces, and plateau chiefdoms in East & Southern Africa

Moving from Great Lakes Urewe brick-tuyere iron furnaces southward down Coastal Kwale and interior highlands, agro-pastoral communities integrated Bantu crop systems with Nilotic/Cushitic and Khoe-San herding terminologies, laying the foundation for later stone-walled plateau kingdoms from Toutswemogala to Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe. [3] [5]
