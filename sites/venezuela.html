Orinoco and llanos communities

The Orinoco’s changing waterways connect several local histories. The evidence from Parmana links people to crops including maize, with diet inferred from scientific analysis of archaeological remains. In the llanos of Barinas, the Gaván project documented settlements and engineered fields. Fields at La Tigra show where people altered water drainage and the shape of usable ground. The settlement studies identify patterns of difference among neighboring places without dating every building. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗ Source 3 ↗

Water crossings to Los Roques

Along the coast, communities from the mainland crossed open water to the Los Roques archipelago. Excavated material at Dos Mosquises includes mammals carried from the mainland together with evidence of marine resource work. These visits can be placed within a broad ceramic-age interval. The archaeology of a single cay provides stronger evidence for particular activities than for a regular sailing timetable. Source 1 ↗

Cubagua and coerced maritime labor

At Cubagua, pearl extraction added a new relationship between maritime resources and coercion. Nueva Cádiz had official city status by 1528, with trading and administrative functions in one place and the work of extracting and opening oysters occurring in other settlements. Archival scholarship identifies different kinds of forced labor. A town marker alone cannot represent that wider work landscape. Source 1 ↗

Comparing local records

Dating techniques allow comparison across very different uses of land and water. In Barinas the period assignments rely on excavation and comparison among field systems and neighboring settlement evidence. At Los Roques, analysis of nonlocal animal remains gives a more direct signal of transport, while the dates apply to its archaeological contexts. The records describe particular places without assigning one chronological sequence to all of Venezuela. Source 1 ↗ Source 2 ↗
