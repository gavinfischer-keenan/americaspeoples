# North, west and north-central Africa: earliest evidence to 15,000 BCE

This first expansion adds **45 locality/phase records, 39 cited source entries, five fossil records and 40 archaeological records**, covering approximately 29 named localities or site complexes. Several phases at the same locality are intentional. These are not 45 separate settlements, nor a complete inventory of African archaeological sites.

Owned catalogue: `data/world/africa-north-west.json`. Research checked 18 November 2026. The research used primary scientific papers, author/institutional repositories and excavation-project pages. Full-text, abstract-only and indexed-text access are distinguished in every source entry. No Consensus report text was copied or republished.

## Chronological decisions

- **15,000 BCE is astronomical year -14999**, equivalent to 16,949 years before the 1950 BP epoch. Every record here ends at or before -14999.
- For published calibrated BP, luminescence ages, geological ages and explicit uncertainty bounds, numeric placement is 1950 minus age. Rounded ka/Ma ages remain approximate; apparent numerical precision is storage, not measurement precision.
- Minimum-age evidence is placed at its reported threshold and labelled as a minimum, rather than assigning an invented older bound. This applies to Bizmoune, Thomas Quarry I Unit L, the revised Tighennif assessment and the early Iberomaurusian anchor at Taforalt.
- A statistical uncertainty interval is not occupation duration. Broad stratigraphic sequences explicitly describe repeated episodes or a complex-level summary.
- **Shum Laka remains in the research queue, not this public map:** the source reports an older context around 32,000 radiocarbon BP. The original determinations must be retrieved and recalibrated before a defensible calendar interval can be added. The earlier uncalibrated display anchor was removed during independent review; later Holocene burial records remain separate.
- Bété I Unit C extends younger than the cutoff. The marker uses only its eligible older portion; the end is expressly an editorial cutoff, not an occupation-ending event.
- Oued el Akarit uses the paper’s direct eggshell AMS range of 44,100–42,650 calibrated BP. The younger contextual OSL estimates are preserved as a disagreement, not averaged into a false consensus.

## Material revisions and exclusions

Independent review updated [El Mnasra](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0261282) to the combined model for the earliest anthropogenic US 9d (139–111 ka, 95% interval). The [Bargny revision](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.qeh.2024.100044) gives Unit 5 OSL 144 ± 7 ka and Ti-H ESR 138 ± 14 ka; the display envelope includes both quoted uncertainties, not a statistically combined confidence interval. These replace earlier generic chronology labels without adding duplicate locality observations.

Thomas Quarry I’s fossil context uses the [2026 primary chronology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12823423/), distinct from the much older Unit L archaeological record. The same paper’s revised minimum interpretation for Tighennif is labelled uncertain and contrasted with the earlier institutional 700 ka estimate. These fossils are not presented as demonstrated direct ancestors.

Taforalt’s famous later burial population, Jebel Sahaba, Iwo Eleru, Ndiayène Pendao and Saxomununya fall after the requested calendar cutoff. They were not moved earlier merely because a source uses “BP.” Earlier Taforalt occupations remain mapped. The existing Shum Laka Holocene genomic samples are not duplicated or attributed to the much earlier occupation.

The prompt’s “Sahnoun” did not resolve to an independently identifiable locality in the verified material. Mohamed Sahnouni is a principal researcher of Aïn Boucherit/Aïn Hanech. Those actual localities are included; no speculative “Sahnoun” point was created.

Existing canonical Jebel Irhoud, Toros-Menalla and Koro Toro observations remain in their original shards. No new record here duplicates their dated fossil observations.

## Location and interpretation

Coordinates are **approximate site or complex points** rounded from primary locality descriptions, coordinates or maps. They are not surveyed findspots. Aïn Hanech and El Kherba deliberately share a generalized research-area point; the two assemblages remain distinct. Conversely, two Aïn Boucherit horizons have slightly different published positions but belong to one locality.

The primary locality sections were used in preference to inconsistent secondary gazetteers. For example, the [Laminia paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7801626/) places the site on the Gambia River in southeastern **Senegal**, not in the modern country of The Gambia. Ounjougou’s locality uses the [chronology paper’s coordinates](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1871101415300261). Tiémassas is generalized to its published Nianing-area locality; the point is not an exact excavation coordinate.

The shared `hominins-unassigned` category is defined here for archaeology without securely identified makers and for taxonomically unresolved remains. It is an indexing category, not a biological species. It prevents Oldowan, Acheulean, Aterian, Sangoan, Iberomaurusian, Dabban or Kubbaniyan from being treated as ethnic peoples or automatically assigned to Homo. Fossil records and occupational evidence remain separate evidence kinds.

There are **no inferred routes, territories or population counts** in this shard. Transport of shells or similarities between tools do not by themselves identify migration tracks. A last known observation is not extinction or regional abandonment.

## Remaining research gaps

This is a supported first pass, not the final “all sites” inventory. Priority follow-up includes dated phases of the Moroccan Atlantic caves and Casablanca quarries, Algeria beyond the Aïn Hanech sequence, Tunisian older Palaeolithic contexts, Libyan interior localities, and additional independently dated Nile quarry/camp sites. Western Sahara, Mauritania, Niger, Nigeria, Benin, Burkina Faso, the northern forest belt and Gabon remain particularly sparse here. Numerous surface assemblages need reliable context and chronology before they can be placed honestly on a calendar player.

The generic Ounjougou later marker should eventually be replaced by independently located and dated constituent levels, with this overview retired to avoid double counting. Wadi Kubbaniya is similarly a complex-level phase summary. The Bîr Tarfawi/Sahara markers date archaeological environmental contexts; they must not be promoted to individually dated hearths or skeletons.
