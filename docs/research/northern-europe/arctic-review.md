# Arctic evidence review

The Arctic files now contain 51 dated entries, 41 evidence sources and three guides. The review checked primary sources for the major archaeological date limits and administrative changes and compared the Europe projection with 22 reused North Atlantic source records. The global evidence file and its original time bounds remain available to the Americas atlas.

## Corrected dates and source use

- Disko Bay’s Early Dorset horizon now uses about 600 BCE to 200 CE, the range the National Museum gives for the Sermermiut and Qajaa midden evidence. The same page reports about 700 BCE to 200 CE for the wider Greenlandic early phase. It also gives about 1100 to 1300 CE for dated Late Dorset material, now represented separately in northwest Greenland. The field project notes earlier Late Dorset evidence and the record identifies that difference. [Early Dorset museum account](https://natmus.dk/organisation/forskning-og-kulturarv/nyere-tid-og-verdens-kulturer/etnografisk-samling/arktisk-forskning/prehistory-of-greenland/early-dorset/), [High Arctic Late Dorset research](https://natmus.dk/organisation/forskning-og-kulturarv/nyere-tid-og-verdens-kulturer/etnografisk-samling/arktisk-forskning/previous-projects/maintaining-contacts/).
- The early Independence II dates of about 800–600 BCE now use a Peary Land regional reference. The source associates Dove Bugt with younger dates near 200 BCE to the start of the Common Era. The new text retains the qualification about marine material in eastern contexts. [Independence II museum account](https://natmus.dk/organisation/forskning-og-kulturarv/nyere-tid-og-verdens-kulturer/etnografisk-samling/arktisk-forskning/prehistory-of-greenland/independence-ii/).
- Reykjavík’s older wall predates the tephra. The numerical range 869–873 represents the ash dating constraint, and the new entry labels it accordingly. Alþingi’s final Þingvellir meeting in 1798 remains distinct from the 1800 abolition after meetings in Reykjavík. The parliament building entry identifies the inauguration and its use beyond the timeline ceiling. [Reykjavík City Museum](https://borgarsogusafn.is/en/adalstraeti-en), [Alþingi history](https://www.althingi.is/pdf/enska.pdf), [Parliament House](https://www.althingi.is/english/about-the-parliament/the-parliament-house).
- Eiðisvatn’s age model gives a sheep signal near 500 CE within a 370–610 CE interval. The new Á Sondum point adds separate direct evidence from charred barley beneath later buildings. The two selected calibrated intervals in the excavation report are 351–543 and 601–765 CE. The guide and the evidence keep these as distinct windows and leave settler identity open. Early Norse settlement displays as a century-level horizon. Faroese political history distinguishes the conventional 1035 formal act from effective royal rule established later and cites the Løgting’s own institutional history. [Eiðisvatn sampling study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-021-00318-0), [Á Sondum excavation report](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1501880), [Faroese government chronology](https://www.faroeislands.fo/the-big-picture/history-of-the-faroe-islands/historical-timeline), [Løgting history](https://www.logting.fo/um-logtingid/soga/soga-logtingsins/).

## Continuity and reuse

- Iceland’s continuity record has a publication-neutral title and uses 1900 as the display limit. Greenland has an ongoing Inuit continuity record alongside the approximate regional endpoints of the Norse settlements. The date around 1200 follows the Greenland National Museum’s model of Thule arrival. It describes a large-scale movement and changing regional histories. Tasiilaq in 1894 is a colonial institution in an inhabited Tunumiit area. [Greenland National Museum on Thule](https://en.nka.gl/museum/exhibitions/new-people-the-thule-culture/), [Greenland National Museum 2009 report](https://nka.gl/fileadmin/user_upload/Museet._AArsrapport_2009_komp.pdf).
- Nipisat’s three calibrated activity windows and attestation type are preserved from the canonical record, including the exclusion of a problematic 520 BCE sample. Iceland and Greenland carry explicit ongoing continuity. The Norse abandonment anchors remain inferred and approximate. The wedding year at Hvalsey is secure for the purpose of this timeline. The exact date follows the canonical record. A Kujataa nomination gives a different day in September, while the cited conference material and archaeological publications support the conventional 16 September date. The wedding and the surrounding documentary record describe one attestation alongside longer, independently assessed occupation. [Kujataa nomination](https://kujalleq.cowiplan.dk/media/1265/kujataa__unesco_nomination_2015.pdf), [Hvalsey Conference record](https://www.eaglehill.us/JONAonline/articles/JONA-Sp-2/04-Frontmatter.shtml).
- Modern region labels aid navigation. The evidence distinguishes the Norwegian Crown, later Danish governance and present-day geography. Both Norse settlements lay in southwest Greenland. Their relative names describe points of settlement, distinct from modern divisions across Greenland.

## Links and remaining gaps

Changed the Icelandic settlement exhibit and Íslendingabók translation URLs to the live museum and Viking Society pages. The guide maps now link directly to the Á Sondum excavation report as well as the Eiðisvatn study. The Icelandic heritage agency’s Skálholt page produced a HEAD failure in a batch scan, but the title and source content were independently retrieved. UNESCO and Alþingi rate-limited the same direct HEAD client and their cited pages were retrieved through browser research. The links to documents on each UNESCO listing expose the official site maps.

Coverage remains comparative and selected. Earlier Greenlandic occupation is relatively thin outside the major sites, and archaeology cannot supply a continuous site-by-site population sequence. The Faroes have sparse late medieval farm, harbour and community records and could use additional local archaeological sources. Iceland benefits most from more evidence outside Reykjavík, Skálholt, Þingvellir and Hólar, especially farming, fishing, transport and published census data. The 1021 cutting date is explicitly available within the Newfoundland connection. This update preserves the canonical connection and its wider uncertainty bracket.

The separate route-rendering change is tracked in the Europe normalizer. That change determines whether the Iceland–Greenland and Greenland–Newfoundland links display as movement rather than trade.
