# Northern Europe warfare and Picts research handoff

The warfare file adds 75 sourced conflict records across nine regional bins. It covers war, raiding, territorial campaigns, sieges and political repression from 685 to 1855. The Picts guide adds one map with 14 site and hypothesis markers, four evidence sections and 24 references. The records’ purpose is to show particular events and the evidence for them, including competing political coalitions across borders.

Classification gives 52 battles, 11 sieges, six campaigns, five raids and one repression entry. Where a campaign survives chiefly as a sequence of discrete encounters, the entries preserve the documented events. Existing general catalogue conflicts, including Lindisfarne in 793, Hastings, Bannockburn, Culloden, the Boyne and Dybbøl, remain in their existing records. The additional conflicts include 23 battlefield records from the Historic Environment Scotland inventory, with the selection emphasizing Scottish forces fighting each other.

## Reading the military geography

- All 23 Scottish inventory anchors come from the published National Grid coordinates for the individual designation, transformed from EPSG:27700 to EPSG:4326 and rounded to three decimal places. The anchor represents a larger recorded battlefield. Inventory maps and boundaries are cited through the official pages, with no copied polygons or troop movements. [Inventory dataset](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=PORTAL%3Adownloads%3A%3A%3A%3A%3ADATASET%3ABTL), [Harlaw](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL11), [Inverlochy I](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL34), [Inverlochy II](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL24), and [Bothwell Bridge](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL5)
- Harlaw represents competing claims over Ross, with participants from multiple mainland and island districts. Inverlochy in 1431 belongs to disputes between the crown and the Lordship of the Isles. Inverlochy in 1645 belongs to the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. The Royalist and Covenanter sequence also covers Tippermuir, Auldearn, Alford, Kilsyth and Philiphaugh. Kilsyth uses 15 August as given by the inventory header and a contemporary account; one passage in the same inventory gives 16 August. [Harlaw](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL11), [Kilsyth](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL13), and [Philiphaugh](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/designation/BTL14)
- Iona’s 802 burning and 806 killings are separate annal entries. The Alt Clut siege is dated 870 and the Dublin return is dated 871. One schematic line connects the campaign locality with the reported return port. The annal reports captives of several named backgrounds and does not identify their individual origins or embarkation points. An origin for the attackers at Lindisfarne cannot be inferred from their later label. The 795 Rechru episode has competing location identifications and remains outside the added map records. [Annals of Ulster](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/T100001A.html), [early Irish raids research](https://celt.ucc.ie/General%20Vikings%20in%20Ireland.pdf), and [Historic Environment Scotland on Dumbarton](https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2025/07/vikings-scotland-dumbarton/)
- The Great Army sequence uses York’s capture, Northumbrian counterattack, the Torksey and Repton winter camps, and the later confrontation at Ethandun. The 1066 northern sequence distinguishes Fulford and Stamford Bridge. Clontarf records a Leinster–Dublin alliance with support from the Isles and the defeat by Brian Bóruma’s coalition. The map does not treat all the participants as two ethnically uniform populations. [York Archaeology](https://learning.yorkarchaeology.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/JORVIK-Fact-Sheet-The-Viking-Capture-of-York.pdf), [Torksey excavation study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquaries-journal/article/winter-camp-of-the-viking-great-army-ad-8723-torksey-lincolnshire/C54BB610EA9E0E567DC2C0622BA753EB), [Repton radiocarbon study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/viking-great-army-in-england-new-dates-from-the-repton-charnel/30DFE4A0D5581DEBC8B43096A37985EE), [English Heritage on 1066](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/1066-and-the-norman-conquest/northern-battles-1066/), and [National Museum of Ireland on Clontarf](https://source.museum.ie/en-IE/Museums/Archaeology/Exhibitions/Clontarf-1014-Brian-Boru-and-the-Battle-for-Dublin)

Both events illustrate why campaign intent, action, battle location and subsequent political authority need separate evidence. Post-medieval Nordic examples include the Swedish attack on Copenhagen in 1659, war at Lund and Napue, Norwegian fortresses in 1716 and 1718, Russian victories in Finland in 1808 and the Crimean War in Åland and at Viapori. ## Picts and Alba

- The guide distinguishes the late Roman name from communities with longer archaeological histories. It uses the early symbol contexts at Dunnicaer alongside direct radiocarbon dating of the symbol-bearing bone from the Broch of Burrian. A genetically sampled person and a carved object cannot stand for an entire political or linguistic identity. [Roman naming study](https://doi.org/10.1080/03044181.2022.2076723), [symbol chronology](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2018.68), and [ancient DNA study](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1010360)
- Brittonic Celtic is the best-supported broad linguistic classification. The guide explains what names and short texts contribute, the distinction between ogham script and language, and the limited evidence for reading the symbols. It includes Dyce, the Drosten Stone and the Dupplin Cross and links to archaeological and institutional maps. [Katherine Forsyth’s linguistic study](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/2522/), [Dyce](https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/dyce-symbol-stones/history-and-stories/), and [St Vigeans](https://www.historicenvironment.scot/visit/all/st-vigeans-sculptured-stones-museum/history-and-stories/)
- The 685 encounter is attested. The guide identifies Dunnichen and the Badenoch alternative separately. Fortriu’s proposed northern location provides context for annal entries in 839 and 904, and the event pins explicitly identify unknown localities at regional scale. Annal usage of Alba about 900, the persistence of local communities and differences across the Forth, Clyde and islands inform the formation narrative. [Alex Woolf on Dun Nechtain and Fortriu](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/dun-nechtain-fortriu-and-the-geography-of-the-picts/), [Dauvit Broun on Alba](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/104300/), and [Buckquoy dating study](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/370606/)

The coverage leans toward battles with locatable official references, public castle sites and firmly dated chronicles. Documentary survival varies considerably by area. Svolder, Rechru and several traditions about Finnish crusades need separate treatment if added later. Exact ship paths, numbers of combatants, raids named only in much later accounts and the changing extent of local control remain unevenly supported. The evidence and guide files use original summaries and bibliographies. Institutional plans, page images and archival facsimiles remain at their cited sources.

The production guide loader, Europe data loader and normalized evidence record validator passed after this contribution. That run found 396 combined Europe entries, 60 guides, and 218 validated Nordic and warfare records.
