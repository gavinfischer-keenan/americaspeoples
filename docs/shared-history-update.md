# A shared world, with terrain and local places — 0.10

Watch opens with **Americas · all histories**, centered on the Americas with the Pacific and Australia in the overview, and automatically plays from the earliest published evidence to 1934. Every story, expedition and manual date uses the same contemporary world. A story changes the camera and highlights; it does not remove other communities, conflicts, connections or expeditions. The overview button restores the complete history-layer selection.

The free movie slows during dated expeditions and pauses at evidence changes. Contemporaneous travelers are shown together. Following an expedition skips sections outside the fixed atlas scope (95°E eastward to 30°W, 60°S–80°N). Tracks between documented stops remain schematic. Visits, exactly dated losses and conflict onsets appear after their supported date; political boundaries remain explicitly labeled year-end snapshots rather than exact-day sovereignty claims.

The map uses self-hosted Natural Earth relief globally and USGS terrain at closer Four Corners and Hawaiian Islands scales. Chaco, Mesa Verde and Hovenweep have additional local relief, as do Oʻahu, Maui and northern Hawaiʻi. Modern terrain and coastlines are reference geography, not a reconstruction of ancient shorelines. Deeper zoom and close-place focus make the terrain and individual archaeological points usable together.

## Local places

42 source-linked records add individually located archaeological places: 15 Chaco great houses/ceremonial structures, 10 Mesa Verde places or distinct phases, four Wupatki pueblo references, two Ocmulgee structures, Etowah, and ten Mesoamerican/Andean city references. Multiple phases at Step House remain separate. Chaco's first and last story scenes now frame the canyon and its actual buildings.

NPS public archaeological points locate the Chaco/Mesa Verde buildings. A point does not stand in for a surveyed footprint. INAH coordinates locate Monte Albán, Palenque, Tula, El Tajín and Tlatelolco. UNESCO reference points for Tikal, Copán, Chichén Itzá, Chan Chan and Tiwanaku are explicitly city/site anchors; Tikal's coordinate is rounded to whole minutes. Known locations do not become “unknown” because a construction date or political interpretation is uncertain.

Construction records remain as established historical places after their dated building phase. That persistence does not assert people still lived in every building. Occupation records become earlier-place context when their evidence ends; an evidence gap alone does not establish abandonment.

## Pacific and contemporary context

18 additional records include a qualified East Polynesia–Hawaiʻi arrival connection; four Hawaiian interisland basalt-provenance relationships; Kahikinui and Lahaina context; historical China, Columbia River and whaling links; early Mololo and Laili evidence; a modeled Raja Ampat crossing corridor; and four conflicts contemporary with the Beagle era in Brazil and the Andes. Existing colonial cities now remain visible around expeditions because the story filter no longer removes them.

The Hawaiian arrival connection uses the Athens–Rieth–Dye 2014 model (940–1130 CE, 95% HPD), while alternative models remain available. Its southern endpoint represents East Polynesia, not a proven departure island. The Kahikinui basalt links use the site's broad 1400–1800 archaeological context; neither every transfer date nor continuous yearly traffic is known. Undated Hawaiian/Tuamotu adzes remain undated, rather than manufacturing a sailing interval. Historical trade endpoints name today's China and the contemporary Qing context.

The Pacific story now begins long before Lapita. Mololo's public coordinate is directly published by Gaffney et al. (2024); its early evidence includes ages beyond reliable radiocarbon limits. Laili's 95.4% onset range is retained as uncertainty around initial occupation, not uninterrupted habitation across the entire range. The Laili point remains explicitly regional pending coordinate verification.

Primary source references and claim-specific limits are part of every exported record. Consensus was used for discovery, followed by primary-source checks. The cumulative research budget stands at **25 calls of 100**, all nine research runs using Deep; no raw Consensus report is redistributed.

## Close views and uncertainty

At close zoom, the story panel becomes a chronology of buildings, occupation phases and local conflicts in the visible area. Its date buttons change the shared clock; the rest of the world remains contemporaneous. Individual buildings appear at their documented construction dates/windows. The map does not interpolate town area or resident counts from those dates. Pan, labels, local counters, detailed terrain, reference geometry and evidence rendering follow the viewport; offscreen records retain their dates and return when panned into view.

The inspector separates **When** and **Where**. A mapped archaeological building can have approximate dating; a well-dated battle can have only a valley-level location. These uncertainties do not substitute for each other. The full movie also exposes its calendar date during historical playback, while undated/annual evidence retains its published precision.

Ten further Hawaiian records locate Puʻukoholā construction, the Lapakahi community, and selected conflicts in Puna, south Kona, east Maui, ʻĪao, offshore Waipiʻo, Kawaihae and Oʻahu. Named parties are retained where supported. Puna’s rebellion keeps the 1780–1781 alternative-date window; Kawaihae’s killing preserves disagreements over responsibility. Battle points are named-locality references, not tactical reconstructions. Lapakahi’s circa-1300 reference is explicitly derived from the state brochure’s rounded settlement age. The Hawaiʻi story now includes four conflict chapters within the wider inhabited archipelago.

## Remaining coverage

This is a selected expansion, not every known settlement or building in the Americas. The existing Hovenweep/Canyons of the Ancients group records are retained; some are still public group/access locators rather than individually verified building positions. The atlas still needs more Hawaiian house sites and conflicts, a systematic small-house inventory at Chaco, more Four Corners villages, mound-center inventories and finer Maya/Inca residential detail. Georeferenced building plans must carry their own construction phases and reuse terms before they can be drawn as dated footprints. The map does not invent room layouts or fill unknown neighborhoods with generic buildings.

Historic border coverage and archaeological position precision remain uneven. Paraguay is already represented from 1811 in the boundary data, and Brazil in the Beagle years is labeled as the Empire of Brazil. Future additions should continue to separate documented use, political claims, actual construction, uncertainty and later occupation.

Coastlines still use generalized Natural Earth outlines. At close island zoom their angular edges are visible; finer shoreline geometry remains a separate coverage need even where the relief and place coordinates are more detailed.
