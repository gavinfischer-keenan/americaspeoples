# Amazon, Gran Chaco and Guianas: dated communities and connections

This campaign adds **35 observations to the shared master map**: 16 in Amazonia, 10 in the Gran Chaco and 9 in the Guianas. There are 27 observation/origin locality IDs and 29 including route destinations, not 35 new towns. The collection contains 14 establishment/building milestones, 10 local attestations, one post-occupation interval, five exchange connections and five conflicts or violent events. The atlas now contains **2,038 total records: 1,980 shared and 58 focused**. No new record is confined to a topic movie; all fit the shared timeline ending in 1934.

## Research and budget

One completed **Consensus Deep** dossier guided this campaign. Cumulative usage began at 58/100. Call 59 submitted the Deep brief; calls 60–66 checked its status. **Cumulative usage: 66/100, with 34 calls remaining**, counting polls. No Quick or Pro searches were used. The [Deep research thread](https://consensus.app/search/research-one-comprehensive-deep-dossier-for-an-edu/MdBUz2haRFu3yi2FF5Glug/) and full returned document are retained as the discovery trail; the full report stays private.

The map cites underlying papers, archival research and community/custodial histories, with original factual summaries and passage locators. Research summaries are leads, not historical authority. Source prose, report text and photographs are not redistributed. Existing noncommercial licenses and third-party terms are unchanged.

## Date and map behavior

The five connections have explicit activity windows: 1758, 1763, 1787, 1853 and approximately 1905. A single dated dispatch does not become a centuries-long continuously animated route. **Cessation remains unknown** after the last evidence. The two-endpoint lines indicate a connection, not a surveyed river course or a ship's logged itinerary. The 1905 Maripaston connection is an account of goods and communication, not an individually documented voyage.

Earlier place markers remain available as labeled historical evidence. An attestation is not a founding date or proof of continuous occupancy. The first Cuyuni post has a documented 1758 destruction/end, while later posts were elsewhere and Indigenous life continued. Conflict markers do not become permanent settlement markers.

Cottica's 1791–1815 window spans alternative historical interpretations of its establishment, not a 24-year construction program. Pauxis's 1740s attack has a coarse date; the source passage does not securely name its attackers. No invented casualty totals are included.

## Geographic checks

New localities use rounded public town/site references with explicit precision and provenance. Borba uses the town, not its huge municipality; Christiaankondre uses the village, not Galibi district; Maripaston uses the historical village, not the mining concession. Cottica on the Lawa is distinguished from the coastal Cottica River. Fort Zeelandia in Guyana is distinguished from the fort of that name in Paramaribo. Concepción de Baures is the Mojos mission, not Concepción in Chiquitos; the [Bolivian national archive catalog](https://www.archivoybibliotecanacionales.org.bo/images/contenido/Archivo/Catalogos/Mojos_Chiquitos.pdf), ficha 115, independently identifies that locality.

The first Cuyuni post, Caiza attack, Marouini violence and Marabitanas fort retain **regional reference points** with stated uncertainty (75, 70, 90 and 20 km respectively). Their exact site positions are unresolved; zooming does not turn these into building coordinates. San Fernando and Timbó similarly use local vicinity references. Public OSM references identify modern geographic anchors, not historical dates or occupation footprints. Coordinate references derived from © OpenStreetMap contributors retain the [ODbL terms](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright); see THIRD_PARTY.md.

## Source access and decisions

Full papers/manuscripts were checked for Harris, Gregório, Carvalho, Salinas, Dalla-Corte, Iñigo Carrera/Musante, Mendoza, van Stipriaan and Fleury; institutional material supports the other entries. Fleury's publisher page was protected during checking, so the author-uploaded full text was read through Academia. The CLIM community report's historical passage was available in the indexed PDF, but later retrieval was restricted; this supports only the narrow Kuma’ka/Albina claim, not a claim to have reviewed all its village histories.

The dossier's Mojos and Enlhet book-review citations were not substituted for the original studies. Modern Tëpu ethnography was not backdated to an eighteenth-century village. The 1762 promise of wagons/oxen at San Fernando was not added as completed trade; only the independently dated 1763 exchange account was used. A fort proposal is not a built fort.

## Gaps still open

- Cofán/Siona rubber-era villages and relocations: recover original village names, dated locations and community histories.
- Mojos and Chiquitos: more actual town phases, canoe itineraries and Indigenous producers' accounts; not just imperial trade controls.
- Gran Chaco: seasonal settlements, Enlhet perspectives, frontier labor and local conflicts beyond missions and the interstate war.
- Guianas: Wayana and other upper-river villages with public georeferencing; changes in Maroon settlements and nineteenth-century freight networks.
- More observations before and after each one-date attestation. Missing dates do not indicate abandonment, and documented arrival of outsiders does not mean previous isolation.

The [coverage audit](world-coverage-audit.html) compares the campaign with the completed v0.15 baseline of 2,003 total / 1,945 shared records. The [detailed matrix](coverage-audit.html) now includes an explicit Gran Chaco row. Overlapping regions and repeated observations must not be added together as a population or settlement census.
