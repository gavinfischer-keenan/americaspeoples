Stratified Sequences and Chronological Depth
Geoarchaeological excavations across Baja California confirm millennia of human occupation preserved in deeply
stratified deposits (Davis et al., 2025; Fujita, 2010). On Cedros Island, alluvial fan sequences at Cerro Pedregoso
reveal stratified cultural horizons dating between ~12,000 and 9600 cal BP, with underlying paleosols suggesting
older terminal Pleistocene activity (Davis et al., 2025). Nearby at El Peregrino and Colina Castor, Bayesian
modelling of over 40 radiocarbon determinations places stratified cultural layers between 12,905 and 12,050 cal BP
around fossil freshwater springs (Davis et al., 2025). Marine shell and lithic artifacts were transported inland up to
15 km from submerged coastlines, reflecting focused processing at spring margins rather than permanent
residential towns (Davis et al., 2025).
In the southern peninsula, excavations across 30 sites on Espíritu Santo and La Partida islands record human use
beginning during the terminal Pleistocene at Covacha Babisuri and La Ballena #3 (Davis et al., 2025; Fujita, 2010).
Cave and rockshelter middens exhibit multi-millennial sequences extending to European contact, whereas open
mesa top sites show mid-Holocene abandonment (Fujita, 2010). Along the Pacific coast of Baja California Sur, the
Faro Punta Marquez site preserves a trans-Holocene record spanning from ~10,600 cal BP through late prehistory
(Fujita et al., 2026). Early strata document mangrove and estuarine exploitation, followed by a shift to rocky-shore
mollusks as sea levels stabilized (Fujita et al., 2026).

Archaeological testing demonstrates that stratigraphic continuity in Baja California represents regional ecological
persistence rather than continuous sedentary occupation at individual sites (Jazwa et al., 2023; Ritter et al., 1995;
 Moore, 1999). In the Río Rosario Valley, settlement patterns were highly mobile and dispersed due to terrestrial
resource constraints, especially the distribution of coastal agave (Agave shawii) (  Moore, 1999). In the Bahía de
los Ángeles and Bahía de las Ánimas regions, over 74 sites span 6,000 years of recurrent coastal harvesting,
showing that thinly scattered mobile populations did not intensify or aggregate until ~1500–1000 BP (Ritter et al.,
1995; Ritter, 1998).
This mobile foraging adaptation contrasts with neighboring desert areas of northwestern Mexico, where
agricultural villages produced sedentary stratigraphy featuring pit houses, canals, and storage architecture (
Goguitchaichvili et al., 2023).

record nearly continuous coastal exploitation from 4000 BC onward by mobile Papaguerían groups (Mitchell et al.,

sedentary village lifeways prior to 1000 CE (Jazwa et al., 2023; Ritter, 1998;  Moore, 1999).
