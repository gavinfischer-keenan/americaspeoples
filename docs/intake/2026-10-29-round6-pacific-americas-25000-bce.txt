    Evaluating human occupation around 25,000 BCE requires distinguishing between claims in North and Central America, evidence in South America, and the archaeological timeline of the Pacific Islands.
    North and Central American Evidence

    Purported archaeological sites predating the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM; ~26,500–19,000 cal BP, or ~24,500–17,000 BCE) in North and Central America remain highly contested across the discipline Becerra-Valdivia2020Potter2021. At Chiquihuite Cave in Mexico, excavators reported lithic artifacts and chronological models suggesting human dispersals began as early as 33,000–31,000 years ago Ardelean2020. Similarly, butchered mammoth remains at the Hartley locality in New Mexico were dated to 38,900–36,250 cal BP, potentially reflecting an early, non-Upper Paleolithic human dispersal Rowe2022
    . However, substantial critical reviews argue that all proposed Pre-Paleoindian sites older than 16,000 cal BP lack unambiguous diagnostic artifacts or intact cultural stratigraphy Potter2021.

    In contrast, widely accepted archaeological and genomic evidence firmly documents human populations arriving south of the continental ice sheets only after 16,000 to 14,500 cal BP Steffen2024
    Potter2018Waters2019. Early securely dated complexes, such as the Cooper's Ferry site in Idaho (~16,000 cal BP) and Clovis horizons (~13,000 cal BP), establish post-LGM settlement patterns via deglaciated coastal or interior corridors Becerra-Valdivia2020Lesnek2018
    Davis2019
    . Paleoceanographic simulations indicate that coastal migration was physically viable during intervals such as 24,500 to 22,000 cal BP, but physical evidence of human transit dating to 25,000 BCE is still lacking Steffen2024

    South American Archaeological Records

    In South America, robust archaeological evidence for hunter-gatherers consistently emerges several millennia after 25,000 BCE Prates2020
    Steele2009. Statistical analyses of screened radiocarbon databases identify the earliest reliable continental threshold between 16,600 and 15,100 cal BP (~14,600–13,100 BCE) Prates2020
    . Claims for occupations around 25,000 BCE are largely unverified or rely on ephemeral traces that do not meet standard validity criteria Prates2020

    Figure 2 Comparative regional chronology of verified human arrival across Pacific and maritime zones

    Pleistocene human seafaring was confined to Near Oceania, reaching Sahul and its proximal offshore islands before 40,000 BP Bednarik1989. True oceanic Pacific Islands (Polynesia and Micronesia) remained entirely uninhabited until the late Holocene Rowe2022
    . While ancient East Asian ancestries shared with Australasian populations point to deep Pleistocene lineages, this genetic structure does not substantiate human settlement of Remote Oceania or the Americas around 25,000 BCE Rowe2022
    . Strong consensus maintains that verified occupation across the Americas and Remote Oceania occurred much later Braje2020Waters2019.
