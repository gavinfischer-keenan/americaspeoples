Research on prehistoric conflict across the Americas has advanced through three methodological phases since
2000.
FIGURE 1 Methodological phases tracking genetic and bioarchaeological evidence of prehistoric American conflict
While skeletal trauma and burnt architecture are ubiquitous markers of ancient combat, paleogenomic studies
demonstrate that warfare rarely caused continent-wide lineage replacements; rather, conflict altered allele
distributions through local demographic fissioning, sex-biased mortality, and post-contact population collapses
(Eerkens et al., 2015; Posth et al., 2018; Llamas et al., 2016).
Pre-2010 Baseline
Early evaluations of conflict in the Americas relied almost exclusively on bioarchaeological skeletal trauma,
settlement patterns, and macroscopic site destructions rather than molecular ancient DNA (Lambert, 2002; Walker,
2001). Cranial depressions, embedded projectile points, and fortified hilltop sites documented pervasive intra- and
intergroup conflict that escalated markedly across North America and the Central Andes around AD 1000 (Arkush
& Tung, 2013; Lambert, 2002; Walker, 2001). However, whether lethal violence involved extermination by foreign
invaders or localized raids remained difficult to resolve without genetic proxies for relatedness and geographic
origin (Kurin et al., 2014; Walker, 2001).

2010–2020 Turning Point
Post-2020 Consolidation
Recent high-resolution paleogenomics demonstrates that ancient warfare was primarily mediated through localized
demographic dynamics rather than mass demographic replacements (Novak et al., 2021; Willerslev & Meltzer, 2021;
Villa-Islas et al., 2023). In central Mexico, populations maintained genetic continuity across 2,300 years of
environmental and sociopolitical turmoil, despite historical narratives alleging violent demographic takeovers by
northern groups (Villa-Islas et al., 2023). Similarly, in the high-altitude Bogotá Altiplano of Colombia, a basal hunter-
gatherer lineage was replaced around 2,000 BP by Central American-affiliated agrarian populations that
subsequently persisted through the militaristic Muisca period without further replacement (Krettek et al., 2025).
The most pronounced genetic signatures associated with conflict appear in the massive extinction of pre-
Columbian maternal lineages following European colonial warfare and epidemic shock, which eliminated
substantial haplogroup diversity along vulnerable coastal zones (Llamas et al., 2016; Valverde et al., 2016).

In Central California, an isotopic and ancient mtDNA study of a prehistoric seven-male mass grave at CA-ALA-

Continental paleogenomic transects in South and Central America identified an early population replacement
around 9,000 years ago, followed by long-term regional continuity that endured despite recurrent state
expansions (Posth et al., 2018).
•
Complete mitogenomic sequencing from the Central Coast of Peru showed that the imperialist expansion of the
Wari Empire produced minimal genetic disruption, ruling out widespread demographic extermination or
complete lineage replacement (Valverde et al., 2016).
•
Biogeochemical and osteological analyses of the Late Intermediate Period in Peru (AD 1160–1260) identified
non-local women with severe trauma, linking inter-polity warfare to mobility-by-abduction (Kurin et al., 2014).

The most striking research gap in the literature is the scarcity of high-resolution, genome-wide studies directly
applied to prehistoric conflict and mass grave contexts in Latin America (De La Fuente Castro & Figueiro, 2024).
While bioarchaeological analyses of cranial trauma and strontium isotope proveniencing are well developed in
regions like the Central Andes and Patagonia, paleogenomic work has primarily addressed broad continental
migrations rather than micro-scale warfare dynamics (Standen et al., 2023; Gordón, 2015; Willerslev & Meltzer,
2021; Murphy & Juengst, 2020). Addressing this gap requires integrating kinship-level genomic sequencing with
forensic osteology to determine whether ancient massacres involved indiscriminate violence, targeted family
executions, or extra-regional combatants (Eerkens et al., 2015; Novak et al., 2021).
Overall, the genetic remnants of indigenous American warfare manifest primarily as localized kin-structured
raiding, female captivities, and post-contact bottlenecks rather than sweeping pre-Columbian demographic
replacements.
