Evidence from historical linguistics and archaeology confirms that language dispersals, exchange networks, and
intergroup conflict were deeply interconnected across the ancient Americas, though the specific mechanisms
varied by region.
Trade Routes and Language Dispersal
In the pre-Hispanic Andes, the successive dispersals of major languages—Aymara, Quechua, and Pukina—
reflected the establishment of prestigious lingua francas that integrated long-distance exchange systems
(Hornborg, 2014). Ethnolinguistic integration often preceded political control, suggesting that shared languages
facilitated trade networks before state formation consolidated them (Hornborg, 2014). The procurement of
prestigious trade goods like Spondylus shells played a role in reproducing Andean societies and shaping prehistory
(Hornborg, 2014). In Mesoamerica, linguistic convergence defining the "Meso-American Linguistic Area" was
enacted through overlapping networks of political, economic, and religious interaction initially established and
maintained by trade and intermarriage alliances (Marin, 2018). Economic vocabulary from Nahuatl records
demonstrates that towns and households profited from trade in central Mexico prior to 1521 (Seijas, 2019), while
linguistic data from Maya dictionaries argue for the central role of merchants in Maya society (Seijas, 2019).
Geographic environments determining potential gains from trade shaped the co-evolution of language; ethnic
groups separated across regions with high variation in land productivity were more linguistically similar than groups
across homogeneous regions, driven by inter-ethnic trade (Dickens, 2021). In Amazonia, the Arawak language
family expanded through regional integration via long-distance travel and trade (Eriksen, 2011).
Warfare and Linguistic Boundaries
Warfare and military domination served as alternative strategies that brought languages into close contact,
occasionally driving language shift alongside trade (Marin, 2018). In the Andes, two large-scale waves of escalated
conflict occurred in the Final Formative (400 BC–AD 100) and the Late Intermediate period (AD 1000–1400), as
supported by cranial trauma and settlement pattern data (Arkush & Tung, 2013). In northern Colombia, the Panche
—a Carib-affiliated group described by the Spanish as warriors—left a settlement pattern record that allows
archaeologists to assess pre-Hispanic warfare in societies without strong defensive signatures (, 2025). Classic
Maya warfare at Piedras Negras involved fortifications, weaponry, and captive-taking, with material and epigraphic
evidence reconstructing a history of conflict within the polity (Scherer et al., 2022). In Amazonia, the transition to
AD 1000 brought socio-political changes evidenced by increased cultural diversity, conflict, and regional
integration, with ethnogenetic processes provoked by assimilation, intermarriage, war, and trade (De Souza
Barreto, 2025). In northwestern Amazonia, close interaction between Tanimuka and Yukuna speakers intensified in
the last 500 years, leading to substantial contact-induced language changes in Tanimuka consistent with language
shift and asymmetrical power relations (Arias et al., 2022).

In northern Peru, linguistic divisions correspond to differences in the archaeological record within overarching
cultures, suggesting that language and material culture both index different pre-Hispanic social identities and
allegiances (Urban, 2021). The northern Andes show a fractal picture of language replacement associated with
migration, where river valleys served as major axes of access between the Andes and Amazonia (Urban, 2021). The
distribution of "international style" gold artifacts coincides suggestively with Chibchan languages, linking trade
goods to language group expansions (Urban, 2021). In western North America, the spatial extents of projectile point
types most closely resemble large language groupings and phyla, suggesting that point distributions reflect
extensive social interaction networks rather than bounded prehistoric cultures (Buchanan et al., 2017). A continent-
wide gradient in the grammatical structure of Indigenous languages mirrors early north-south dispersal paths,
reflecting deep population history that trade and conflict later reshaped (Urban & Guzmán Naranjo, 2025).

Across the Americas, language distributions, trade networks, and warfare were structurally linked: trade routes
created the interaction spheres that spread lingua francas, while warfare and migration displaced populations and
reconfigured linguistic boundaries, leaving legacies visible in both the archaeological and linguistic records.
