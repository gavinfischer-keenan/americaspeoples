What archaeological and palaeoecological evidence supports pre ‑ European
occupation?
Charcoal, artifacts, and molecular proxies suggest pre-European presence, but
evidence remains debated.
Evaluating the presence of pre-European human occupation across remote islands relies on three distinct facets:
sedimentological and fire indicators, material culture and faunal assemblages, and biomolecular markers of human
subsistence.
Palaeoecological Fire Records
Sediment cores and peat deposits reveal environmental changes that researchers frequently link to early
anthropogenic disturbance, though distinguishing human ignition from natural fire regimes remains challenging
(Zangrando & Borrero, 2022; Elias et al., 2022).
Archaeological Lithics and Fauna
Physical artifacts and modified animal bones provide direct material signatures of landscape use, though their
contextual integrity is heavily contested in island environments (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022; Saarinen et al., 2021).
Biomolecular and Subsistence Traces
Emerging molecular tools such as sedimentary ancient DNA (sedaDNA) and lipid biomarkers detect anthropogenic
presence and subsistence introductions even where conventional macroscopic features are absent (Brown et al.,
2021; Smith et al., 2015).
Sediment cores from peat bogs preserve macrofossil charcoal dating to 10,500–11,550 cal BP, which
researchers have cited as an indicator of early human burning (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022).
•
Palynological profiles show marked arboreal pollen declines and grass increases, though chronologies often
display discrepancies between bulk dates and pollen concentrates (Elias et al., 2022).
•
Long-term regional sequences link vegetation shifts to natural climate transitions, demonstrating that fire
regimes can expand without definitive evidence of human agency (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022; Elias et al.,
2022).
•
Isolated lithic projectile points and a bone harpoon point recovered from surface deposits demonstrate
technological affinities with mainland maritime hunter-gatherers (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022).
•
Dense accumulations of marine mammal bones have been interpreted as butchery refuse from indigenous
maritime nomads (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022).
•
Skeletal remains of the extinct canid Dusicyon australis suggest human transport, though genetic divergence
estimates well predate known maritime seafaring (Zangrando & Borrero, 2022).
