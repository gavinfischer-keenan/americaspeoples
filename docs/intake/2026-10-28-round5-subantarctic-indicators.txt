Palaeoecological Indicators of Sub-Antarctic Human Activity
Sedimentary archives from sub-Antarctic and peri-Antarctic islands preserve multi-proxy records of anthropogenic
disturbance, bridging ephemeral early visits, historical exploitation eras, and modern scientific footprints (Kristan et
al., 2022; Saunders et al., 2013; Convey & Lebouvier, 2009; Hamley et al., 2021).
Lacustrine and Sedimentary Stratigraphy
On sub-Antarctic Macquarie Island, lake sediment cores reveal an unproductive lacustrine state persisting for
approximately 7,100 years before human interference (Saunders et al., 2013). Following the anthropogenic
introduction of European rabbits, rats, and mice around AD 1898, sediment accumulation rates accelerated over
100-fold due to massive catchment erosion (Saunders et al., 2013). Concurrently, total organic carbon and total
nitrogen quadrupled, driving cultural eutrophication that allowed two previously rare diatom taxa to dominate the
flora (Saunders et al., 2013; Burney, 1997).
In the South Atlantic on South Georgia Island, geochemical profiles in lake cores tracking the period from 1854 CE
to the present capture the crash and subsequent recovery of native marine predator colonies (Kristan et al., 2022;
Kristan, 2021). Down-core concentrations of biological remains, specifically Antarctic fur seal (Arctocephalus
gazella) hairs and king penguin (Aptenodytes patagonicus) feathers, drop markedly during the intense commercial
sealing and whaling eras before rising sharply between 1926 and 1950 alongside shifts in $\delta^{13}\text{C}$ and
$\delta^{15}\text{N}$ (Kristan et al., 2022; Kristan, 2021). On King George Island in maritime Antarctica, lake cores
adjacent to stations register heavy metal enrichment, fuel hydrocarbon residues, and distinct shifts in bacterial 16S
rRNA metabarcoding profiles dominated by pollution-resistant clades (Bertoglio et al., 2025; Moya et al., 2025).
Biomarkers and Combustion Products
Sedimentary charcoal fluxes reflect intentional burning, such as the sudden orders-of-magnitude surge in
charcoal accumulation rates documented at New Island around 550 to 400 BP (Hamley et al., 2021; Schreuder
et al., 2019).
•
Hemispheric black carbon deposited in northern Antarctic Peninsula ice cores shows a threefold increase after
1297 CE, tracing long-range transport of fire plumes from southern high-latitude forest clearance (McConnell et
al., 2021).
•
Fecal sterol biomarkers, particularly coprostanol and epi-coprostanol, isolate human presence in island
catchments from natural avian guano inputs (Argiriadis et al., 2018; Allen et al., 2022).
•
Cellulose combustion tracers, such as the monosaccharide anhydride levoglucosan and incomplete-
combustion polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), track regional biomass burning regimes (Argiriadis et al.,
2018; Schreuder et al., 2019).

Across oceanic island ecosystems globally, palynological time series show that the arrival of humans accelerates
vegetation turnover rates by a median factor of 11 relative to pre-settlement baselines (Nogué et al., 2021). On sub-
Antarctic islands, this turnover manifests through the direct physical impacts of sealing and whaling camps, the
destabilizing grazing pressure of introduced mammals, and trophic reorganizations that permanently alter fragile
Southern Ocean terrestrial communities (Convey & Lebouvier, 2009; Bergstrom & Selkirk, 2007; Matthews et al.,
2026).
