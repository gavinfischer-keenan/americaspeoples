    The initial colonization of deglaciated corridors around 14,000–13,000 calibrated years before present evolved into stable regional adaptations and intensified late prehistoric resource use Nash2024Ellis2011Fulton2020.
    Late Pleistocene Deglaciation and Initial Colonization

    Pioneering foragers entered the deglaciated landscape of the central and lower Great Lakes during the Late Glacial period around 14,000 to 13,000 cal BP Nash2024. In southwestern Michigan, excavations at the Belson site uncovered a stratified Clovis campsite on an outwash plain where the ancestral St. Joseph River drained glacial meltwater into Ohio tributaries Nash2024
    . Occupants targeted an open spruce-fir parkland that offered vantage points over large game herds crossing meltwater channels Nash2024
    . Multiple buried components below the plow zone yielded fluted projectile points and flake tools exhibiting classic Clovis knapping techniques Nash2024
    . Protein residue tests from these buried tools reveal the exploitation of a broad spectrum of late Pleistocene mammals Nash2024.

    Other early fluted point assemblages across the basin—including Paleo Crossing and Sheriden Cave in northern Ohio, Palmer in southeast Michigan, and the Rogers site in southern Ontario—confirm an established human presence skirted glacial boundaries prior to 12,000 BP Nash2024
    Calkin1977. At the Belson site, over 235 km separated the tools from their source stone at the Attica chert quarries in west-central Indiana, indicating wide foraging ranges centered around high-quality lithic sources Nash2024
    . Regional syntheses indicate that early Paleoindian groups rapidly colonized newly deglaciated terrains despite lingering glacial margins Ellis2011Legg2017Lothrop2016.

    During the Younger Dryas climatic reversal (ca. 10,800–10,000 $^{14}\text{C}$ BP), regional environmental shifts prompted substantial modifications in hunter-gatherer settlement choices Ellis2011. Toolstone analyses across 83 sites with concave-based points reveal that early groups moved primarily along north-south axes to exploit caribou and widely spaced resources Ellis2011. As climate conditions shifted and deglaciated terrains dried out, foraging populations relocated toward newly formed glacial lake shores and wetland systems left by draining proglacial bodies Ellis2011. In Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, geoarchaeological modeling demonstrates that late Paleoindian groups selectively situated campsites on high ground adjacent to hilly terrain and inland lakes to intercept migrating big game Legg2017.

    Submerged landscape archaeology provides clear proof of these early adaptations; on the Alpena-Amberley Ridge beneath modern Lake Huron, researchers documented stone drive lines used for caribou hunting during the Lake Stanley lowstand 10,000 to 8,000 years ago Sonnenburg2025.
    Archaic Intensification and Woodland Transitions
        Paleodemographic proxies record accelerated population expansion beginning in the Late Archaic around 5400–2500 BP, altering regional forest structure through increased burning Fulton2020.
        Woodland communities constructed pottery and diversified local cooking between 50 and 1500 CE, as documented at stratified occupations like the Cloudman site on Drummond Island Peterson2024.
        Subsistence transitioned from early hunting toward intensive wild rice harvesting, nut exploitation, and aquatic foraging, alongside pottery vessel enlargement over time Peterson2024.
        Cultigen records show that maize arrived regionally around 2500–1100 BP and expanded into intensive farming between 1100 and 300 BP, establishing sustainable pre-contact agricultural villages Peterson2024Fulton2020.

    Human occupation of the Great Lakes began along the margins of retreating late Pleistocene ice sheets around 14,000–13,000 years ago, establishing continuous settlement chains that persisted through the Holocene Nash2024Fulton2020Sonnenburg2025.
