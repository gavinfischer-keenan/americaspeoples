# Communities and connections: three regional gaps and early trade

Version 0.24.0 adds **81 shared map records** and corrects **4 existing records**. These are 78 evidence entries plus three linked route representations of local finds, rather than 81 independent discoveries. These additions are part of the master map: changing the year shows them alongside contemporary places, peoples and events elsewhere. Three new regional guides supply 28 contextual chapters. The independent trade topic gains 13 chapters and retains its own clock.

## Costa Rica and Panama: homes, monuments and skilled work

At Las Mercedes, people built stone-faced platforms, long paved approaches and a connected monumental center. At Guayabo, building and rebuilding shaped a settlement with roads, drainage and ceremonial spaces. Farther south, the Diquís settlements preserve raised mounds, stone construction and the celebrated spheres. The new local records distinguish the dated ramp at Finca 6 from the separate problem of when individual spheres were made and positioned.

Panamanian communities left house-related deposits, food remains, shell-working debris and richly furnished burials. The sequence now includes Sitio Sierra, Sitio Conte, El Caño and Cerro Juan Díaz. Sitio Drago adds a Caribbean coastal community whose pottery and ornaments connect it with neighbors across the isthmus.

The campaign began with only **one distinct dated locality** in the Costa Rica/Panama 501–1000 CE audit cell. Sixteen new archaeological observations broaden the history without treating a cemetery as the location of every associated home. The earlier Cerro Juan Díaz point has been corrected in place, keeping its existing record identity.

## Atlantic Brazil: generations building coastal landscapes

Coastal communities gathered shellfish, caught fish, hunted and made tools from animals including whales. They also built enduring places for the dead. Shell mounds grew through repeated construction and burial practices; later layers at some sites contain much more fish bone than shell. Those changes describe how people used the landscape, rather than a simple sequence of empty and occupied dots.

Sixteen new observations cover twelve localities, including Rio Caipora, Piaçaguera, Beirada, Encantada III, Morro do Ouro, Cabeçuda, Amourins, Jabuticabeira II, Içara, Lagoa Freitas Riacho dos Franciscos II and Sernambetiba. The guide follows selected dated deposits into later coastal histories. Its photograph shows actual Morro do Ouro excavation sections and sampling blocks.

The later additions are concentrated on the southern coast. In the finer audit, Eastern Brazil improves from one to three localities in 3000–1000 BCE, and Sernambetiba adds one locality in each of 1000–1 BCE and 1–500 CE. Its two bins from 501 through 1500 CE remain at zero dated localities. Those eastern gaps are still open; the new southern sites do not fill them by proxy.

## Solomon Islands and Vanuatu: life after first settlement

The story continues beyond Lapita arrivals. Communities cultivated land, built homes, buried their dead and exchanged stone and other goods. New records follow places on Santo, Malakula, Efate, Tikopia, Taumako, the Roviana islands and neighboring islands. Excavated drains, walls, houses and burial contexts give the centuries after settlement their own events.

Eighteen new local observations are joined by three later exchange connections: Tutuila stone at Kahula, New Britain obsidian at Sikopo, and Banks Islands glass at Emae. The existing Taumako record has moved from an offshore point to a named island reference; its history and identity are preserved.

## More trade on the shared clock

The earlier map had **seven exchange-line observations intersecting 0–1200 CE** after excluding founding migrations. It now has **36**. A connection can represent successive imports across centuries or a single dated shipment; its description explains which. Multiple records can describe goods or phases on the same connection, so these totals are not counts of independent trade routes.

The additions connect source areas with places where goods were found. They include intraregional stone procurement as well as long-distance links. American obsidian, ceremonial materials and construction resources join Pacific ceramics and toolstone. Yap–Fais imports recur through dated archaeological phases. Northern Bali received Indian and later Chinese wares. The Belitung cargo represents one shipwreck, not a trading service running throughout its estimated date range.

Lines follow supported dates and are schematic connections between endpoints. They do not claim to trace a surveyed road or an exact sailing track. The last included evidence does not establish that contact stopped. The later Inca imperial road system is not backdated into this period; earlier Andean exchange uses its own evidence.

## What remains thin

These are substantial additions to selected gaps. Eastern Brazil from 501 to 1500 CE remains a priority, alongside northern Brazilian coastal sites, finer household histories in Costa Rica and Panama, more island-by-island Melanesian sequences, and routes with securely paired source and receiving dates. Broad date ranges do not prove uninterrupted habitation or annual journeys. Disjoint calibrated dates are now shown as separate windows rather than filling their intervening gaps. A gap after settlement is a research question, not an automatic abandonment event.

Both coverage reports now read the canonical shared dataset, including collections that older filename-based scanning omitted. The comparison baseline is v0.23.1, with 2,458 shared records. [The detailed matrix](coverage-audit.html) counts distinct localities separately from observations; [the broad comparison](world-coverage-audit.html) shows changed region/time cells. Neither estimates the completeness of the archaeological record.

Two Consensus Deep dossiers were used privately, with independent checks against original studies and official sources. Six calls including retrievals were used for this combined campaign, leaving **54 of the authorized additional calls**. Full provider reports are not redistributed.
