# Canada, the Aleutian Islands and illustrated histories

This update adds a Canada overview with seven regional guides and contextual movies: Pacific Canada, the Prairies, Yukon and the Northwest, the Arctic, the Great Lakes, the St. Lawrence and the Atlantic region. A separate Aleutian Islands guide follows maritime communities from early stone tools through houses, seasonal movement, colonial violence, forced labour and industrial whaling. All of these movies retain the shared historical clock and surrounding world.

The expansion contributes 62 Canadian and 24 Aleutian evidence records to the master dataset. One Canadian mound record remains undated because its sources provide an open age threshold, not a closed calendar range. It remains readable in the guide and evidence index. Two older Amaknak records are expanded in place, and four house-sample records retain their individual dates at the same public locality. Existing evidence is reused where appropriate, including Calvert Island, Bluefish Caves, Debert, Lake Huron obsidian, L’Anse aux Meadows and earlier colonial establishments. Calvert gains a public island reference rather than a second footprint record.

## People, work and change

Canadian additions include clam gardens, fish weirs, longhouse villages, Prairie horticulture, a composite Yukon hunting dart, Arctic houses, canoe and maritime trade, Métis settlements, treaty meetings, battles, forced departures and residential schools. Modern Canada is a geographic frame for distinct histories, not an ancient cultural unit. Archaeological labels are not automatically treated as languages or ancestral identities.

Aleutian additions distinguish Anangula’s early blade-making from its later village, separate dated Quarry Site charcoal samples, and describe Amaknak’s stone houses and complex hearths. Later records show Russian violence, company-directed relocations, Pribilof sealing labour, Korovinski’s gardens, successive Unalaska churches and Akutan’s whaling industry. The shared cutoff remains 1934; that cutoff does not imply that communities or institutions ended then.

The calibrated age of a sample is not silently converted into a settlement’s entire lifespan. Local occupation sequences retain known breaks. Broad island or community references are labeled as such; public site coordinates are used when verified. Map symbols distinguish documented conflict and coercive relocation from ordinary settlement evidence.

## Reading beyond a list

Twenty-two earlier guides gain substantial original historical prose. New photographs and museum-object images accompany the writing, with captions distinguishing surviving remains, reconstructions, protective modern structures and museum collections. Each file has a named creator, source page, verified license, modification statement and integrity hash. The image’s own license remains independent of the atlas’s noncommercial terms.

Related-dive cards explain the connection and open across the whole card. Guide-to-topic connections also appear in the topic’s “See also” panel. The source register includes the additional evidence, prose citations and image credits. Referenced essays and private Consensus reports are not redistributed as atlas writing.

## Coverage still to develop

This is a substantial regional expansion, not a census of every Canadian or Aleutian site. Further priorities include Canadian Shield and northern inland sequences, local Atlantic and Pacific communities between the selected milestones, individual school and treaty experiences, and more Aleutian village plans. Some guides still have fewer photographs than others. Source-supported additions should continue to join the master map as well as the relevant dive.

One additional Consensus Deep dossier supported this batch. Counting dispatch and retrieval conservatively, 12 of the newly authorized 75 calls have been used across the current allowance; 63 remain. The provider report stays private, while original summaries link to the underlying studies, community accounts and institutional histories.
